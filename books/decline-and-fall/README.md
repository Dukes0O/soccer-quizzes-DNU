# The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, Chapters XV–XVI — Modern English Edition

**Edward Gibbon, 1776** · rendered into modern English, 2026

A modern-English rendering of the two most famous — and in their day most
scandalous — chapters of Gibbon's *History of the Decline and Fall of the
Roman Empire* (Volume 1), produced for readers working through the Great Books
of Western Thought. Each chapter is very long, so it is presented in five
parts; every paragraph is rendered in full, and each part file contains an
**Overview**, **Key Concepts**, an **Archaic Terms** glossary, and the
complete **Modern Translation**.

## Why these chapters matter

Chapter XV asks a question no historian had dared put so coolly: setting
aside providence, what *human* causes explain Christianity's conquest of the
Roman world? Gibbon's five "secondary causes" — inherited zeal, the promise
of immortality, miraculous claims, austere morals, and disciplined church
government — scandalized 18th-century England precisely because the analysis
is delivered deadpan, with the irony doing the arguing. Chapter XVI then
audits the persecutions and finds the toleration of pagan Rome greater, and
the number of martyrs far smaller, than church tradition claimed — closing
with the famous tally that Christians have inflicted more deaths on each
other than Rome ever inflicted on them. For a Great Books reader these
chapters are the model of Enlightenment historiography: Hume's skepticism and
Voltaire's wit applied to sacred history, and the direct provocation for a
generation of replies.

## Translation notes

Gibbon's irony is the substance of these chapters, and the rendering
preserves it — sly sentences stay sly in modern English; nothing is flattened
or editorialized. Names, offices, places, and dates are kept as Gibbon gives
them; Latin phrases are retained with a brief gloss. **Gibbon's footnotes are
omitted from this edition** — they are mostly scholarly citations (with some
famous asides); the full annotated text remains available at the Project
Gutenberg source above.
