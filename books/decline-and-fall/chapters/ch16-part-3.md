# Chapter XVI, Part 3 — The Death of Cyprian, the Cult of Martyrdom, and the Long Intervals of Toleration

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XVI (The Conduct of the Roman Government Towards the Christians, from the Reign of Nero to That of Constantine), Part 3 of 5
>

## Overview

This part opens with Gibbon's set-piece account of the martyrdom of Cyprian, bishop of Carthage (AD 257–258) — chosen deliberately as a representative case. Gibbon narrates the whole affair in careful detail precisely because it is so undramatic: a comfortable exile to a pleasant seaside town, a courteous arrest, an elegant last supper with friends, a reluctant judge, a swift beheading without torture, and an unmolested public funeral. The unstated argument is that even the death of the most famous martyr of the age looks nothing like the popular picture of savage persecution — and Cyprian was the *first* African bishop to be executed at all.

From this case Gibbon widens out into an analysis of martyrdom as an institution. He examines the theological rewards promised to martyrs (immediate heaven, a seat beside Christ at the judgment), the earthly celebrity that awaited confessors, and the "fever of the mind" that drove some Christians to volunteer for death — to the bafflement of Roman magistrates, one of whom asked why they didn't simply use ropes and cliffs. He then catalogs, with equal candor, the three routes by which less heroic Christians escaped: lawful flight, purchased certificates of sacrifice, and outright apostasy followed by post-crisis repentance.

The final movement demolishes the traditional narrative of "ten persecutions" as a fifth-century numerological invention (modeled on the plagues of Egypt and the horns of the Apocalypse) and surveys the actual record from Commodus to Gallienus: long stretches of open toleration, Christians at court, church buildings and property purchased even in Rome, punctuated only by brief and local severities under Severus, Maximin, Decius, and Valerian. The part closes with the portrait of Paul of Samosata, the venal, luxurious, theatrical bishop of Antioch — Gibbon's evidence that forty years of peace and prosperity proved "far more dangerous to their virtue than the severest trials of persecution."

## Key Concepts

- **The martyrdom of Cyprian as test case** — the fullest documented execution of the era shows mild procedure, judicial reluctance, no torture, and public funeral honors; and Cyprian was the first of Africa's many bishops deemed worthy of the "crown."
- **The economy of martyrdom** — martyrs were promised instant admission to eternal bliss (bypassing purgation) and a role as Christ's assessors at the last judgment, plus lasting earthly fame; confessors who survived acquired holy status they sometimes abused.
- **Voluntary martyrdom** — some Christians denounced themselves, disrupted pagan rites, and demanded sentencing; pagan observers read this eagerness to die as despair, insensibility, or frenzy rather than heroism.
- **Three modes of escape** — flight (approved by the bishops, condemned only by Montanist rigorists); buying false certificates (*libelli*) of compliance; and open apostasy, reversed in crowds once danger passed.
- **The myth of the "ten persecutions"** — a schematic count devised by fifth-century church writers to match the ten plagues of Egypt and the ten horns of the Apocalypse, not a fact of second- and third-century experience.
- **Suspicious tales of imperial favor** — Tertullian's stories of protective edicts by Tiberius and Marcus Aurelius dissolve under scrutiny; Marcus "despised the Christians as a philosopher, and punished them as a sovereign."
- **De facto toleration** — under Commodus (via the concubine Marcia), the Severan household, Alexander Severus and Mamaea, and Philip, Christians enjoyed protection, court access, and property rights long before any legal recognition.
- **Prosperity as spiritual hazard** — the forty-year peace after Valerian produced figures like Paul of Samosata, whose wealth, pomp, and self-indulgence show what security did to the church's primitive discipline.

## Modern Translation

When Valerian was consul for the third time and Gallienus for the fourth, Paternus, proconsul of Africa, summoned Cyprian to appear before him in his private council chamber (AD 257). There he informed him of the imperial order he had just received: those who had abandoned the Roman religion must immediately return to the ceremonies of their ancestors. Cyprian answered without hesitation that he was a Christian and a bishop, devoted to the worship of the true and only God, to whom he offered daily prayers for the safety and prosperity of the two emperors, his lawful sovereigns. With modest confidence he claimed the privilege of a citizen in refusing to answer certain offensive — and in fact illegal — questions the proconsul had put to him. The penalty pronounced for Cyprian's disobedience was banishment, and he was taken without delay to Curubis, a free maritime city of Zeugitania, pleasantly situated in fertile country about forty miles from Carthage. The exiled bishop enjoyed the comforts of life and the consciousness of his own virtue. His reputation spread across Africa and Italy; an account of his conduct was published for the edification of the Christian world; and his solitude was frequently interrupted by the letters, visits, and congratulations of the faithful. When a new proconsul arrived in the province, Cyprian's fortunes seemed for a while to improve still further. He was recalled from banishment, and though not yet permitted to return to Carthage, he was assigned his own gardens near the capital as his place of residence.

At last, exactly one year after Cyprian was first arrested, Galerius Maximus, proconsul of Africa, received the imperial warrant for the execution of Christian teachers. The bishop of Carthage knew he would be singled out among the first victims, and human frailty tempted him to slip away by secret flight from both the danger and the honor of martyrdom. But he soon recovered the fortitude his position demanded, returned to his gardens, and calmly awaited the ministers of death. Two high-ranking officers entrusted with the commission placed Cyprian between them in a chariot; and since the proconsul was not then at leisure, they took him not to a prison but to a private house in Carthage that belonged to one of them. An elegant supper was provided for the bishop's entertainment, and his Christian friends were allowed to enjoy his company one last time, while the streets filled with a crowd of the faithful, anxious and alarmed at the approaching fate of their spiritual father. In the morning he appeared before the proconsul's tribunal. The magistrate, after establishing Cyprian's name and situation, ordered him to sacrifice and urged him to consider the consequences of disobedience. Cyprian's refusal was firm and final, and the magistrate, after consulting his council, pronounced — with some reluctance — the sentence of death. It ran as follows: "That Thascius Cyprianus be immediately beheaded, as the enemy of the gods of Rome, and as the chief and ringleader of a criminal association which he has seduced into impious resistance against the laws of the most holy emperors, Valerian and Gallienus." The method of execution was the mildest and least painful that could be inflicted on anyone convicted of a capital offense; nor was torture used to extract from the bishop of Carthage either a recantation of his principles or the names of his accomplices.

The moment the sentence was proclaimed, a general cry of "We will die with him!" rose from the listening crowd of Christians waiting before the palace gates. These generous outpourings of zeal and affection were neither useful to Cyprian nor dangerous to those who uttered them. He was led away under a guard of tribunes and centurions, without resistance and without insult, to the place of execution — a broad, level plain near the city, already filled with a great crowd of spectators. His faithful presbyters and deacons were permitted to accompany their holy bishop. They helped him remove his outer garment, spread linen on the ground to catch the precious relics of his blood, and received his instruction to give twenty-five pieces of gold to the executioner. Then the martyr covered his face with his hands, and at a single stroke his head was severed from his body (September 14, AD 258). His corpse lay exposed for some hours to the curiosity of the Gentiles; but that night it was removed and carried in triumphal procession, by splendid torchlight, to the Christians' burial ground. Cyprian's funeral was publicly celebrated without any interference from the Roman magistrates, and those of the faithful who performed the last offices for his person and his memory faced no danger of inquiry or punishment. It is remarkable that, out of so great a multitude of bishops in the province of Africa, Cyprian was the first who was judged worthy to obtain the crown of martyrdom.

Cyprian's choice was to die a martyr or live an apostate, and on that choice hung the alternative of honor or infamy. Even if we supposed that the bishop of Carthage had professed the Christian faith merely as an instrument of his avarice or ambition, he was still bound to sustain the character he had assumed; if he possessed the smallest degree of manly fortitude, he had to expose himself to the cruelest tortures rather than trade the reputation of a lifetime, by a single act, for the abhorrence of his Christian brethren and the contempt of the Gentile world. But if Cyprian's zeal rested on sincere conviction of the truth of the doctrines he preached, the crown of martyrdom must have appeared to him as an object of desire rather than terror. It is not easy to extract any distinct ideas from the vague though eloquent declamations of the Fathers, or to determine the exact degree of immortal glory and happiness they confidently promised to those fortunate enough to shed their blood in the cause of religion. They taught with fitting diligence that the fire of martyrdom made up every deficiency and expiated every sin; that while the souls of ordinary Christians had to pass through a slow and painful purification, the triumphant sufferers entered at once into the full enjoyment of eternal bliss, where, in the company of the patriarchs, the apostles, and the prophets, they reigned with Christ and sat as his assessors in the universal judgment of mankind. The assurance of a lasting reputation on earth — a motive so congenial to human vanity — often served to animate the martyrs' courage. The honors that Rome or Athens bestowed on citizens who had fallen for their country were cold and empty gestures of respect compared with the ardent gratitude and devotion the primitive church lavished on the victorious champions of the faith. The annual commemoration of their virtues and sufferings was observed as a sacred ceremony, and in the end it grew into religious worship. Among the Christians who had publicly confessed their principles, those who — as very frequently happened — were released from the tribunals or prisons of the pagan magistrates received the honors justly owed to their imperfect martyrdom and generous resolve. The most pious women begged permission to kiss the fetters they had worn and the wounds they had received. Their persons were considered holy, their opinions were accepted with deference, and they too often abused, through spiritual pride and licentious conduct, the preeminence their zeal and courage had won them. Distinctions like these, while they display the exalted merit of those who suffered and died for the profession of Christianity, also betray how small their number was.

The sober discretion of the present age will more readily censure than admire — but can more easily admire than imitate — the fervor of the first Christians, who, in the lively phrase of Sulpicius Severus, desired martyrdom more eagerly than his own contemporaries pursued a bishopric. The letters Ignatius composed while being carried in chains through the cities of Asia breathe sentiments utterly repugnant to ordinary human feeling. He earnestly begs the Romans that, when he is exposed in the amphitheater, they will not deprive him of the crown of glory by their kind but ill-timed intercession; and he declares his resolve to provoke and enrage the wild beasts that might be employed as the instruments of his death. Stories are told of martyrs who actually did what Ignatius had intended: who goaded the lions to fury, urged the executioner to hurry his work, leaped cheerfully into the fires kindled to consume them, and displayed sensations of joy and pleasure in the midst of the most exquisite tortures. Several examples survive of a zeal impatient of the very restraints the emperors had provided for the security of the church. Christians sometimes supplied by voluntary confession the lack of an accuser, rudely disrupted the public services of paganism, and, crowding around the magistrates' tribunals, demanded that the sentence of the law be pronounced and carried out. Such behavior was too remarkable to escape the notice of the ancient philosophers, but they seem to have regarded it with far less admiration than astonishment. Unable to conceive the motives that sometimes carried believers' fortitude beyond the bounds of prudence or reason, they treated this eagerness to die as the strange product of obstinate despair, dull insensibility, or superstitious frenzy. "Unhappy men!" exclaimed the proconsul Antoninus to the Christians of Asia. "Unhappy men! If you are so weary of your lives, is it so hard for you to find ropes and precipices?" He was extremely cautious — as a learned and pious historian observes — about punishing men whose only accusers were themselves, since the imperial laws had made no provision for so unexpected a case; so, condemning a few as a warning to their brethren, he dismissed the multitude with indignation and contempt. Yet despite this disdain, real or affected, the intrepid constancy of the faithful worked more salutary effects on minds that nature or grace had prepared for the easy reception of religious truth. On these melancholy occasions there were many among the Gentiles who pitied, who admired, and who were converted. The generous enthusiasm passed from the sufferer to the spectators; and the blood of the martyrs, in the well-known saying, became the seed of the church.

But although devotion had raised this fever of the mind, and eloquence continued to inflame it, it gradually gave way to the more natural hopes and fears of the human heart — the love of life, the dread of pain, and the horror of dissolution. The more prudent leaders of the church found themselves obliged to restrain the reckless ardor of their followers, and to distrust a resolve that too often deserted its owners in the hour of trial. As the lives of the faithful grew less austere and self-denying, they grew daily less ambitious for the honors of martyrdom; and the soldiers of Christ, instead of distinguishing themselves by voluntary deeds of heroism, frequently deserted their post and fled in confusion before the enemy it was their duty to resist. There were, however, three methods of escaping the flames of persecution, and they did not carry equal degrees of guilt. The first was generally allowed to be innocent; the second was of a doubtful, or at least a venial, nature; but the third amounted to direct and criminal apostasy from the Christian faith.

I. A modern inquisitor would be surprised to learn that whenever a Roman magistrate received a denunciation of some person in his jurisdiction who had joined the sect of the Christians, the charge was communicated to the accused, who was allowed a convenient interval to settle his domestic affairs and prepare an answer to the crime imputed to him. If he had any doubt of his own constancy, this delay gave him the opportunity to preserve both his life and his honor by flight — to withdraw to some obscure retreat or distant province and patiently await the return of peace and security. So reasonable a measure was soon sanctioned by the advice and example of the holiest prelates, and it seems to have been censured by few except the Montanists, who strayed into heresy through their strict and obstinate adherence to the rigor of the old discipline.

II. The provincial governors, whose greed outweighed their zeal, had tolerated the practice of selling certificates (*libelli*, as they were called) attesting that the persons named in them had complied with the laws and sacrificed to the Roman gods. By producing these false declarations, wealthy and timid Christians could silence the malice of an informer and reconcile, to some degree, their safety with their religion. A slight penance atoned for this profane pretense.

III. In every persecution there were great numbers of unworthy Christians who publicly disowned or renounced the faith they had professed, and who confirmed the sincerity of their abjuration by the legal acts of burning incense or offering sacrifice. Some of these apostates yielded at the magistrate's first threat or exhortation, while the endurance of others was broken down by prolonged and repeated torture. The frightened faces of some betrayed their inward remorse, while others walked up to the altars of the gods with confidence and alacrity. But the disguise imposed by fear lasted no longer than the immediate danger. As soon as the severity of persecution slackened, the doors of the churches were besieged by returning crowds of penitents who detested their idolatrous submission and who begged — with equal ardor but with varying success — to be readmitted into the society of Christians.

IV. Despite the general rules established for the conviction and punishment of Christians, the fate of those sectaries, in an extensive and arbitrary government, must still have depended in large measure on their own behavior, the circumstances of the times, and the temper of their rulers, supreme as well as subordinate. Zeal might sometimes provoke the superstitious fury of the pagans, and prudence might sometimes avert or soften it. A variety of motives could dispose the provincial governors either to enforce the laws or to relax them, and the most powerful of these motives was their regard not only for the public edicts but for the secret intentions of the emperor — a glance from whose eye was enough to kindle or extinguish the flames of persecution. Whenever occasional severities were exercised in different parts of the empire, the primitive Christians lamented, and perhaps magnified, their own sufferings; but the celebrated number of *ten* persecutions was fixed by the ecclesiastical writers of the fifth century, who enjoyed a more distinct view of the church's prosperous and adverse fortunes from the age of Nero to that of Diocletian. It was the ingenious parallels of the *ten* plagues of Egypt and the *ten* horns of the Apocalypse that first suggested this calculation to their minds; and in applying the faith of prophecy to the truth of history, they took care to select those reigns that were in fact the most hostile to the Christian cause. But these passing persecutions served only to revive the zeal and restore the discipline of the faithful, and the moments of extraordinary rigor were offset by much longer intervals of peace and security. The indifference of some princes and the indulgence of others allowed the Christians to enjoy a toleration of their religion that, if not perhaps legal, was nonetheless actual and public.

Tertullian's *Apology* contains two very ancient, very singular, and at the same time very suspicious instances of imperial clemency: edicts supposedly published by Tiberius and by Marcus Antoninus, designed not only to protect the innocence of the Christians but even to proclaim the stupendous miracles that had attested the truth of their doctrine. The first of these examples comes with certain difficulties that might trouble a skeptical mind. We are asked to believe *that* Pontius Pilate reported to the emperor the unjust death sentence he had pronounced against an innocent — and, as it appeared, divine — person, thereby exposing himself to the danger of martyrdom without acquiring its merit; *that* Tiberius, who openly professed his contempt for all religion, immediately conceived the plan of placing the Jewish Messiah among the gods of Rome; *that* his servile senate dared to disobey its master's commands; *that* Tiberius, instead of resenting their refusal, contented himself with protecting the Christians from the severity of the laws — many years before any such laws were enacted, and before the church had assumed any distinct name or existence; and finally, *that* the memory of this extraordinary transaction was preserved in the most public and authentic records, which somehow escaped the knowledge of every historian of Greece and Rome and were visible only to the eyes of an African Christian who composed his apology one hundred and sixty years after the death of Tiberius. The edict of Marcus Antoninus is supposed to have sprung from his devotion and gratitude for the miraculous deliverance he obtained in the Marcomannic war. The distress of the legions, the timely storm of rain and hail, thunder and lightning, and the dismay and defeat of the barbarians have all been celebrated by the eloquence of several pagan writers. If there were Christians in that army, it was natural for them to claim some credit for the fervent prayers they had offered up, in the moment of danger, for their own safety and the public's. But we are assured — by monuments of brass and marble, by the imperial medals, and by the Antonine column — that neither the prince nor the people felt any sense of this signal obligation, since they unanimously attributed their deliverance to the providence of Jupiter and the intervention of Mercury. Throughout his entire reign, Marcus despised the Christians as a philosopher, and punished them as a sovereign.

By a singular twist of fate, the hardships the Christians had endured under the government of a virtuous prince ceased the moment a tyrant came to power; and as they alone had experienced the injustice of Marcus, so they alone were protected by the leniency of Commodus (AD 180). The celebrated Marcia, the most favored of his concubines — and the woman who eventually contrived the murder of her imperial lover — entertained a singular affection for the oppressed church. Though she could hardly reconcile the practice of vice with the precepts of the Gospel, she may have hoped to atone for the frailties of her sex and profession by declaring herself the patroness of the Christians. Under Marcia's gracious protection they passed the thirteen years of a cruel tyranny in safety; and when the empire settled in the house of Severus, they formed a domestic but more honorable connection with the new court. The emperor was persuaded that during a dangerous illness he had derived some benefit, spiritual or physical, from the holy oil with which one of his slaves had anointed him. He always treated with particular distinction several persons of both sexes who had embraced the new religion. Both the nurse and the tutor of Caracalla were Christians; and if that young prince ever displayed a sentiment of humanity, it was prompted by an incident which, however trifling, bore some relation to the cause of Christianity. Under the reign of Severus the fury of the populace was checked; the rigor of the old laws was suspended for a time; and the provincial governors were content to receive an annual present from the churches within their jurisdiction as the price — or the reward — of their moderation. The controversy over the precise date for celebrating Easter set the bishops of Asia and Italy against each other, and was regarded as the most important business of this period of leisure and tranquility (AD 196). Nor was the peace of the church disturbed until the growing number of converts seems at last to have caught the attention, and alienated the mind, of Severus. Intending to check the progress of Christianity, he published an edict which, though aimed only at new converts, could not be strictly enforced without exposing the most zealous of their teachers and missionaries to danger and punishment. Even in this mitigated persecution we can still detect the indulgent spirit of Rome and of polytheism, which so readily admitted every excuse in favor of those who practiced the religious ceremonies of their fathers.

But the laws Severus had enacted soon expired with that emperor's authority, and the Christians, after this accidental storm, enjoyed a calm of thirty-eight years (AD 211–249). Until this period they had usually held their assemblies in private houses and secluded places. They were now permitted to build and consecrate suitable edifices for religious worship, to purchase land — even in Rome itself — for the use of the community, and to conduct the elections of their ecclesiastical ministers in a manner so public, and at the same time so exemplary, as to earn the respectful attention of the Gentiles. This long repose of the church was accompanied by dignity. The reigns of those princes who traced their origins to the Asiatic provinces proved the most favorable to the Christians. The eminent members of the sect, instead of having to beg the protection of a slave or a concubine, were admitted into the palace in the honorable characters of priests and philosophers; and their mysterious doctrines, already widespread among the people, gradually attracted the curiosity of their sovereign. When the empress Mamaea passed through Antioch, she expressed a wish to converse with the celebrated Origen, whose fame for piety and learning had spread across the East. Origen obeyed so flattering an invitation; and though he could hardly expect to succeed in converting an artful and ambitious woman, she listened with pleasure to his eloquent exhortations and dismissed him with honor to his retirement in Palestine. Mamaea's sentiments were adopted by her son Alexander, and that emperor's philosophic devotion was marked by a singular though injudicious regard for the Christian religion. In his private chapel he placed statues of Abraham, Orpheus, Apollonius, and Christ, as an honor justly due to those respectable sages who had instructed mankind in the various ways of paying homage to the supreme and universal Deity. A purer faith, and a purer worship, were openly professed and practiced in his household. Bishops, perhaps for the first time, were seen at court (AD 235); and after Alexander's death, when the inhuman Maximin vented his fury on the favorites and servants of his unfortunate benefactor, a great number of Christians of every rank and of both sexes were swept up in the indiscriminate massacre — which, on their account, has improperly received the name of a Persecution.

Despite Maximin's cruel disposition, the effects of his resentment against the Christians were quite local and temporary; and the pious Origen, though proscribed as a marked victim, was still reserved to carry the truths of the Gospel to the ears of monarchs. He addressed several edifying letters to the emperor Philip, to his wife, and to his mother (AD 244); and as soon as that prince — who was born in the neighborhood of Palestine — had usurped the imperial scepter, the Christians gained a friend and a protector. Philip's public and even partial favor toward the adherents of the new religion, and his constant reverence for the ministers of the church, lent some color to the suspicion, current in his own time, that the emperor himself had become a convert to the faith; and it supplied some grounds for a fable invented later, that he had been purified by confession and penance of the guilt he had contracted by the murder of his innocent predecessor. The fall of Philip (AD 249) brought, with the change of masters, a new system of government so oppressive to the Christians that their entire previous condition, ever since the time of Domitian, was represented as a state of perfect freedom and security compared with the harsh treatment they experienced under the short reign of Decius. The virtues of that prince scarcely permit us to suspect that he acted from mean resentment against the favorites of his predecessor; it is more reasonable to believe that, in pursuing his general design of restoring the purity of Roman manners, he wished to rid the empire of what he condemned as a recent and criminal superstition. The bishops of the most considerable cities were removed by exile or death; the vigilance of the magistrates prevented the clergy of Rome, for sixteen months, from proceeding to a new election; and it was the opinion of the Christians that the emperor would more patiently endure a rival for the purple than a bishop in the capital. If we could suppose that the penetration of Decius had detected pride beneath the disguise of humility, or that he could foresee the temporal dominion that might gradually arise from claims of spiritual authority, we might be less surprised that he regarded the successors of St. Peter as the most formidable rivals to the successors of Augustus.

The administration of Valerian (AD 253–260) was marked by a levity and inconstancy ill suited to the gravity of the *Roman Censor*. In the first part of his reign he surpassed in clemency even those princes who had been suspected of attachment to the Christian faith. In the last three and a half years, listening to the insinuations of a minister addicted to the superstitions of Egypt, he adopted the maxims and imitated the severity of his predecessor Decius. The accession of Gallienus, which multiplied the calamities of the empire, restored peace to the church; and the Christians obtained the free exercise of their religion by an edict addressed to the bishops and framed in terms that seemed to acknowledge their office and public character. The old laws, though never formally repealed, were allowed to sink into oblivion; and — apart from certain hostile intentions attributed to the emperor Aurelian — the disciples of Christ passed more than forty years in a state of prosperity far more dangerous to their virtue than the severest trials of persecution.

The story of Paul of Samosata, who held the metropolitan see of Antioch while the East was in the hands of Odenathus and Zenobia (AD 260), may serve to illustrate the condition and character of the times. The wealth of that prelate was itself sufficient evidence of his guilt, since it was neither inherited from his fathers nor acquired by the arts of honest industry. Paul, in fact, regarded the service of the church as a very lucrative profession. His ecclesiastical jurisdiction was venal and rapacious; he extorted frequent contributions from the wealthiest of the faithful, and converted a considerable share of the public revenue to his own use. His pride and luxury made the Christian religion odious in the eyes of the Gentiles. His council chamber and his throne, the splendor of his public appearances, the suppliant crowd soliciting his attention, the multitude of letters and petitions to which he dictated answers, and the perpetual hurry of business in which he was involved — all these were circumstances far better suited to the state of a civil magistrate than to the humility of a primitive bishop. When he harangued his people from the pulpit, Paul affected the figurative style and theatrical gestures of an Asiatic sophist, while the cathedral rang with the loudest and most extravagant acclamations in praise of his divine eloquence. Toward those who resisted his power or refused to flatter his vanity, the prelate of Antioch was arrogant, rigid, and inexorable; but he relaxed the church's discipline, and lavished its treasures, on his dependent clergy, who were permitted to imitate their master in gratifying every sensual appetite. For Paul indulged himself very freely in the pleasures of the table, and he had received into the episcopal palace two young and beautiful women as the constant companions of his leisure hours.
