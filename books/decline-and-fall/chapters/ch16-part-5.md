# Chapter XVI, Part 5 — The End of the Persecution and the Reckoning of the Martyrs

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XVI (The Conduct of the Roman Government Towards the Christians, from the Reign of Nero to That of Constantine), Part 5 of 5
>
> [← Chapter XVI, Part 4](ch16-part-4.md) · [Book index](../README.md)

## Overview

This final part brings the Great Persecution — and the whole chapter — to its close. Gibbon first surveys the West: a short but violent persecution in Italy and Africa under Maximian, ended abruptly when the usurper Maxentius, courting Christian support, restored peace to the churches. In the East, Galerius pressed the persecution for years until a painful and lingering illness taught him what despotism could not learn on its own; his deathbed edict of toleration in AD 311 is quoted in full, a document whose grudging clemency Gibbon lets speak for itself. Maximin briefly honored the truce, then engineered a subtler campaign — building a pagan counter-church, complete with hierarchy and vestments, and orchestrating "spontaneous" petitions from the cities begging him to expel the Christians — before civil war and death removed the church's last enemy in AD 313.

Then comes the passage for which the chapter is famous. Gibbon refuses to fill his pages with racks, hooks, and red-hot beds, because he cannot decide what to transcribe until he decides what to believe — and Eusebius, the principal source, has admitted to recording only what glorified religion and suppressing what disgraced it. From Eusebius's own figures for Palestine, Gibbon works out a deliberately deflationary arithmetic: roughly fifteen hundred deaths in the East over ten years — "an annual consumption of one hundred and fifty martyrs" — and fewer than two thousand judicial executions in the whole empire across the entire persecution.

The closing paragraph delivers the chapter's celebrated final blow. Taking the well-attested figure of more than a hundred thousand Protestants executed in the Netherlands under Charles V, Gibbon observes that Christians have inflicted far greater cruelties on one another than they ever suffered from pagans — and that if even that modern, well-documented number is doubted, then the ancient martyrologies, written by a courtier and a polemicist under Constantine's protection, deserve far less confidence still. The chapter thus ends by turning the church's own weapons of evidence against its most cherished narrative.

## Key Concepts

- **The Edict of Toleration (AD 311)** — Galerius's dying reversal, quoted in full: a self-justifying document that grants Christians freedom of assembly while still calling their religion an "impious folly," and asks their prayers in return.
- **Maximin's pagan counter-church** — a hierarchy of pontiffs and provincial high priests, deliberately copied from Christian ecclesiastical organization, created to give polytheism the discipline its defeat had revealed it lacked.
- **Manufactured public opinion** — the addresses from Nicomedia, Antioch, and Tyre, engineered by magistrates and priests to dress the court's known wishes as popular demand for expelling the Christians.
- **Eusebius as compromised witness** — his own admission that he recorded what honored the church and suppressed what disgraced it, which for Gibbon poisons the entire evidentiary well of martyrdom accounts.
- **Voluntary martyrdom** — the bishops' need to restrain Christians who sought out arrest, whether from despair, hope of expiating sin, or the comfortable living that prisoners drew from the charity of the faithful.
- **The arithmetic of martyrdom** — Gibbon's extrapolation from Eusebius's ninety-two Palestinian martyrs to a total of fewer than two thousand executions empire-wide over ten years.
- **The final tally** — the closing comparison: Protestants executed in one province in one reign (100,000+, on Grotius's authority) versus primitive martyrs in three centuries and a whole empire — Christians have persecuted each other far more savagely than the pagans ever persecuted them.

## Modern Translation

The provinces of Italy and Africa went through a short but violent persecution. Diocletian's harsh edicts were carried out strictly — and cheerfully — by his colleague Maximian, who had long hated the Christians and who took pleasure in acts of blood and violence. In the autumn of the persecution's first year, the two emperors met at Rome to celebrate their triumph; several oppressive laws seem to have come out of their private consultations, and the presence of their sovereigns spurred on the diligence of the magistrates. After Diocletian laid down the purple, Italy and Africa were administered in the name of Severus and stood exposed, without any protection, to the implacable hostility of his master Galerius. Among the martyrs of Rome, Adauctus deserves posterity's notice. He came from a noble Italian family and had risen through the successive honors of the palace to the important office of treasurer of the emperor's private estates. What makes Adauctus especially remarkable is that he appears to be the only person of rank and distinction who suffered death during the entire course of this general persecution.

The revolt of Maxentius immediately restored peace to the churches of Italy and Africa; and the same tyrant who oppressed every other class of his subjects showed himself just, humane, and even partial toward the afflicted Christians. He was counting on their gratitude and affection, and he quite naturally assumed that the injuries they had suffered — and the dangers they still feared from his most bitter enemy — would secure the loyalty of a party already considerable in numbers and wealth. Even Maxentius's treatment of the bishops of Rome and Carthage can be taken as proof of his tolerance, since the most orthodox princes would probably have taken the same measures with their own established clergy. Marcellus, the first of these prelates, had thrown the capital into turmoil by the severe penance he imposed on the many Christians who had renounced or disguised their religion during the recent persecution. Factional rage erupted in frequent and violent riots; the blood of the faithful was shed by one another's hands; and exiling Marcellus — whose prudence seems to have been less conspicuous than his zeal — turned out to be the only measure capable of restoring peace to the distracted church of Rome. The behavior of Mensurius, bishop of Carthage, appears to have been even more blameworthy. A deacon of that city had published a pamphlet attacking the emperor. The offender took refuge in the episcopal palace, and though it was rather early in history to be asserting claims of ecclesiastical immunity, the bishop refused to hand him over to the officers of justice. For this treasonable resistance Mensurius was summoned to court — and instead of receiving a legal sentence of death or banishment, he was allowed, after a brief examination, to return to his diocese. So happy was the condition of the Christian subjects of Maxentius that whenever they wanted to obtain bodies of martyrs for their own use, they had to purchase them from the most distant provinces of the East. A story is told of Aglae, a Roman lady descended from a consular family and possessed of an estate so vast that it required seventy-three stewards to manage it. Among these, Boniface was his mistress's favorite; and since Aglae mixed love with devotion, he is reported to have been admitted to share her bed. Her fortune allowed her to gratify the pious desire of acquiring some sacred relics from the East. She entrusted Boniface with a considerable sum of gold and a large quantity of aromatic spices; and her lover, attended by twelve horsemen and three covered carriages, set out on a long pilgrimage as far as Tarsus in Cilicia.

The bloodthirsty temper of Galerius, the first and principal author of the persecution, was a standing terror to those Christians whose misfortunes placed them within his dominions; and it is fair to presume that many people of middling rank — bound by the chains of neither wealth nor poverty — very often abandoned their native country and sought refuge in the milder climate of the West. As long as Galerius commanded only the armies and provinces of Illyricum, he could scarcely find or make any considerable number of martyrs in a warlike country that had received the missionaries of the Gospel more coldly and reluctantly than any other part of the empire. But once he had gained supreme power and the government of the East, he indulged his zeal and cruelty to the fullest — not only in the provinces of Thrace and Asia, which fell under his immediate jurisdiction, but also in Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, where Maximin gratified his own inclinations by rendering rigorous obedience to the stern commands of his benefactor. But the repeated disappointment of his ambitious designs, the experience of six years of persecution, and the salutary reflections that a lingering and painful disease suggested to his mind at last convinced Galerius that the most violent exertions of despotism are not enough to exterminate a whole people or to break their religious convictions. In AD 311, wishing to repair the harm he had caused, he published — in his own name and in those of Licinius and Constantine — a general edict which, after a pompous recital of the imperial titles, proceeded as follows:

> "Among the important cares that have occupied our mind for the benefit and preservation of the empire, it was our intention to correct and reestablish all things according to the ancient laws and public discipline of the Romans. We were particularly anxious to reclaim, to the way of reason and nature, the deluded Christians who had renounced the religion and ceremonies instituted by their fathers and — presumptuously despising the practice of antiquity — had invented extravagant laws and opinions according to the dictates of their own fancy, and had gathered a mixed society from the various provinces of our empire. The edicts we published to enforce the worship of the gods have exposed many of the Christians to danger and distress; many have suffered death; and many more, who still persist in their impious folly, are left without *any* public exercise of their religion. We are therefore disposed to extend to these unhappy people the effects of our accustomed clemency. We permit them, accordingly, freely to profess their private opinions and to assemble in their meeting-houses without fear or molestation — provided always that they maintain due respect for the established laws and government. By a further rescript we shall make our intentions known to the judges and magistrates; and we hope our indulgence will move the Christians to offer prayers to the Deity they worship — for our safety and prosperity, for their own, and for that of the republic."

The language of edicts and manifestos is not usually where one should look for the real character or the secret motives of princes; but since these were the words of a dying emperor, his situation may perhaps be accepted as a pledge of his sincerity.

When Galerius signed this edict of toleration, he was well assured that Licinius would readily fall in with the wishes of his friend and benefactor, and that any measure in favor of the Christians would win Constantine's approval. But the emperor did not venture to put in the preamble the name of Maximin, whose consent mattered most and who succeeded a few days later to the provinces of Asia. During the first six months of his new reign, however, Maximin made a show of adopting his predecessor's prudent counsels; and though he never condescended to secure the church's tranquility by a public edict, his Praetorian prefect Sabinus sent a circular letter to all the governors and magistrates of the provinces, dwelling at length on the imperial clemency, acknowledging the invincible obstinacy of the Christians, and directing the officers of justice to drop their futile prosecutions and to look the other way at the secret assemblies of those enthusiasts. As a result of these orders, great numbers of Christians were released from prison or freed from the mines. The confessors returned to their home countries singing hymns of triumph; and those who had bent before the violence of the storm begged, with tears of repentance, to be readmitted into the bosom of the church.

But this treacherous calm did not last long, and the Christians of the East could place no confidence in the character of their sovereign. Cruelty and superstition were the ruling passions of Maximin's soul: the first supplied the means of persecution, the second pointed out its targets. The emperor was devoted to the worship of the gods, the study of magic, and the belief in oracles. The prophets and philosophers whom he revered as heaven's favorites were frequently raised to the government of provinces and admitted into his most secret councils. They easily persuaded him that the Christians owed their victories to their regular discipline, and that the weakness of polytheism flowed chiefly from a lack of union and subordination among the ministers of religion. A system of government was accordingly set up which was plainly copied from the organization of the church. In all the great cities of the empire the temples were repaired and beautified by Maximin's order, and the officiating priests of the various deities were placed under the authority of a superior pontiff, whose appointed role was to oppose the bishop and advance the cause of paganism. These pontiffs in turn acknowledged the supreme jurisdiction of the metropolitans, or high priests of the province, who acted as the emperor's own immediate deputies. A white robe was the badge of their dignity, and these new prelates were carefully selected from the noblest and wealthiest families. Through the influence of the magistrates and the priestly order, a great number of dutiful addresses were procured — particularly from the cities of Nicomedia, Antioch, and Tyre — which artfully presented the court's well-known intentions as the general sentiment of the people: they urged the emperor to consult the laws of justice rather than the dictates of his clemency, expressed their abhorrence of the Christians, and humbly prayed that these impious sectarians might at least be excluded from the limits of their respective territories. Maximin's answer to the address he obtained from the citizens of Tyre still survives. He praises their zeal and devotion in terms of the highest satisfaction, holds forth on the obstinate impiety of the Christians, and betrays — by the readiness with which he consents to their banishment — that he regarded himself as receiving a favor rather than granting one. The priests as well as the magistrates were empowered to enforce his edicts, which were engraved on tablets of brass; and though they were advised to avoid the shedding of blood, the most cruel and degrading punishments were inflicted on Christians who refused to submit.

The Christians of Asia had everything to fear from the severity of a bigoted monarch who prepared his measures of violence with such deliberate calculation. But scarcely a few months had passed before the edicts published by the two Western emperors (AD 313) forced Maximin to suspend the pursuit of his designs. The civil war he so rashly launched against Licinius consumed all his attention; and the defeat and death of Maximin soon delivered the church from the last and most implacable of her enemies.

In this general survey of the persecution first authorized by Diocletian's edicts, I have deliberately refrained from describing the particular sufferings and deaths of the Christian martyrs. It would have been an easy task — from the history of Eusebius, from the declamations of Lactantius, and from the most ancient acts of the martyrs — to assemble a long series of horrifying and disgusting pictures, and to fill many pages with racks and scourges, with iron hooks and red-hot beds, and with every variety of torture that fire and steel, savage beasts, and more savage executioners could inflict on the human body. These gloomy scenes might be enlivened by a crowd of visions and miracles, designed to delay the death, celebrate the triumph, or reveal the relics of those canonized saints who suffered for the name of Christ. But I cannot decide what I ought to transcribe until I am satisfied how much I ought to believe. The gravest of the ecclesiastical historians, Eusebius himself, indirectly confesses that he has recorded whatever might redound to the glory of religion, and suppressed everything that could tend to its disgrace. Such an admission naturally raises the suspicion that a writer who has so openly violated one of the fundamental laws of history has not been very scrupulous about observing the other; and the suspicion gains further weight from the character of Eusebius, which was less tinged with credulity, and more practiced in the arts of courts, than that of almost any of his contemporaries. On certain particular occasions — when the magistrates were provoked by personal motives of interest or resentment, or when the zeal of the martyrs drove them to forget the rules of prudence, and perhaps of decency, to overturn the altars, to hurl curses at the emperors, or to strike the judge as he sat on his tribunal — it may be presumed that every mode of torture that cruelty could invent, or constancy endure, was exhausted on those doomed victims. Two circumstances, however, have been mentioned incautiously by our sources, and they hint that the general treatment of Christians arrested by the officers of justice was less intolerable than it is usually imagined to have been. First, the confessors condemned to labor in the mines were permitted — by the humanity or the negligence of their keepers — to build chapels and to practice their religion freely in the midst of those dismal quarters. Second, the bishops found it necessary to check and censure the over-eager zeal of Christians who voluntarily threw themselves into the magistrates' hands. Some of these were people crushed by poverty and debt, blindly seeking to end a miserable existence with a glorious death. Others were drawn by the hope that a short imprisonment would expiate the sins of a whole lifetime; and still others were moved by the less honorable prospect of a plentiful living — and perhaps a considerable profit — from the alms that the charity of the faithful bestowed on prisoners. After the church had triumphed over all her enemies, both the self-interest and the vanity of the former captives prompted them to magnify the merit of their respective sufferings. A convenient distance of time or place gave ample scope to the growth of fiction; and the frequent examples that could be cited of holy martyrs whose wounds had healed instantly, whose strength had been renewed, and whose lost limbs had been miraculously restored, proved extremely convenient for removing every difficulty and silencing every objection. The most extravagant legends, since they served the honor of the church, were applauded by the credulous multitude, countenanced by the power of the clergy, and attested by the suspicious evidence of ecclesiastical history.

Vague descriptions of exile and imprisonment, of pain and torture, are so easily exaggerated or softened by the pencil of a skillful orator that we are naturally led to inquire into a fact of a more definite and stubborn kind: the number of people who were put to death as a consequence of the edicts published by Diocletian, his associates, and his successors. The more recent legend-writers record whole armies and cities swept away at a stroke by the indiscriminate rage of persecution. The more ancient writers content themselves with pouring out a generous stream of loose and tragic invective, without condescending to establish the precise number of those who were permitted to seal their belief in the Gospel with their blood. From the history of Eusebius, however, it can be gathered that only nine bishops were punished with death; and his particular enumeration of the martyrs of Palestine assures us that no more than ninety-two Christians were entitled to that honorable title. Since we do not know what degree of episcopal zeal and courage prevailed at the time, we cannot draw any useful inferences from the first of these facts; but the second supports a very important and probable conclusion. In the Roman distribution of provinces, Palestine may be reckoned as one-sixteenth of the Eastern empire; and since there were some governors who, out of real or affected mercy, kept their hands unstained with the blood of the faithful, it is reasonable to believe that the country which gave birth to Christianity produced at least a sixteenth of the martyrs who suffered death within the dominions of Galerius and Maximin. The whole might therefore amount to about fifteen hundred — a number which, if divided equally across the ten years of the persecution, allows an annual consumption of one hundred and fifty martyrs. Allotting the same proportion to the provinces of Italy, Africa, and perhaps Spain, where after two or three years the rigor of the penal laws was suspended or abolished, the total number of Christians in the Roman empire on whom a capital sentence was judicially carried out shrinks to somewhat fewer than two thousand persons. And since it cannot be doubted that the Christians were more numerous, and their enemies more enraged, in the time of Diocletian than in any earlier persecution, this probable and moderate calculation may teach us to estimate the number of primitive saints and martyrs who sacrificed their lives for the important purpose of introducing Christianity into the world.

We shall conclude this chapter with a melancholy truth that forces itself upon the reluctant mind: even if we admit, without hesitation or inquiry, everything that history has recorded or devotion has invented on the subject of martyrdoms, it must still be acknowledged that the Christians, in the course of their internal dissensions, have inflicted far greater severities on one another than they ever experienced from the zeal of unbelievers. During the ages of ignorance that followed the fall of the Roman empire in the West, the bishops of the imperial city extended their dominion over the laity as well as the clergy of the Latin church. The edifice of superstition they had erected — which might long have defied the feeble efforts of reason — was at length assaulted by a crowd of daring fanatics who, from the twelfth to the sixteenth century, assumed the popular character of reformers. The church of Rome defended by violence the empire she had acquired by fraud; a system of peace and benevolence was soon disgraced by proscriptions, wars, massacres, and the institution of the holy office of the Inquisition. And since the reformers were animated by the love of civil as well as religious freedom, the Catholic princes joined their own interest to that of the clergy and enforced by fire and sword the terrors of spiritual censures. In the Netherlands alone, more than one hundred thousand of the subjects of Charles V are said to have died at the executioner's hand; and this extraordinary number is attested by Grotius — a man of genius and learning, who kept his moderation amid the fury of contending sects, and who composed the annals of his own age and country at a time when the invention of printing had multiplied the means of information and increased the danger of detection. If we are obliged to submit our belief to the authority of Grotius, it must be allowed that the number of Protestants executed in a single province and a single reign far exceeded that of the primitive martyrs in the space of three centuries and across the whole Roman empire. But if the sheer improbability of the fact should prevail over the weight of the evidence — if Grotius should stand convicted of exaggerating the merit and sufferings of the Reformers — then we shall naturally be led to ask what confidence can be placed in the doubtful and imperfect monuments of ancient credulity; and what degree of credit can be assigned to a courtly bishop and a passionate declaimer who, under the protection of Constantine, enjoyed the exclusive privilege of recording the persecutions inflicted on the Christians by the vanquished rivals, or the disregarded predecessors, of their gracious sovereign.
