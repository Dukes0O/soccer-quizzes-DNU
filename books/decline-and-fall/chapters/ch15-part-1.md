# Chapter XV, Part 1 — The Five Causes and the First: Zeal Inherited from Judaism

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XV (The Progress of the Christian Religion, and the Sentiments, Manners, Numbers, and Condition of the Primitive Christians), Part 1 of 5
>
> [Book index](../README.md) · [Chapter XV, Part 2 →](ch15-part-2.md)

## Overview

This part opens the most famous — and in its day the most scandalous — chapter of Gibbon's history. He announces his subject with elaborate politeness: since the triumph of Christianity is undeniably part of Roman history, a "candid but rational" historian must ask how it happened. The official answer, he concedes with a straight face, is the truth of the doctrine and the providence of God. But since Providence works through human passions and circumstances, the historian may humbly confine himself to the *secondary* causes — and he lists five: the Christians' intolerant zeal, the doctrine of a future life, the miraculous powers claimed by the early church, the austere morals of the believers, and the discipline of the Christian community as a growing state-within-a-state. The entire chapter is organized around this list, and the pious framing is the vehicle for the irony: by explaining the church's growth entirely through natural causes, Gibbon quietly makes the supernatural explanation unnecessary.

The remainder of this part develops the first cause. Christian zeal, Gibbon argues, was inherited from Judaism — the one people of the ancient world who refused the easy religious tolerance of their neighbors — but stripped of the features that had made Judaism repellent to converts: national exclusivity, burdensome ritual law, and circumcision. Along the way he permits himself a notoriously barbed digression on the Old Testament itself, observing that the Israelites who personally witnessed the miracles of Moses constantly lapsed into idolatry, while their distant descendants, who saw no miracles at all, believed with unshakable firmness — "in contradiction to every known principle of the human mind."

The part then traces the painful separation of church from synagogue: the Judaizing Christians of Jerusalem, their retreat to Pella, their eventual renunciation of the Mosaic law under Hadrian to gain admission to the new colony of Aelia Capitolina, and the fate of the holdout Ebionites, condemned by both religions. Opposite them stand the Gnostics, who rejected the Old Testament entirely and whose objections to it Gibbon rehearses with suspicious thoroughness under cover of reporting heresy. The part closes with what orthodox, Ebionite, and Gnostic all shared: the conviction that the pagan gods were real demons, which turned the tolerant syncretism of the ancient world into an abomination no Christian could touch.

## Key Concepts

- **Secondary causes** — Gibbon's governing device: professing to leave the "first cause" (divine truth) to the theologians, he explains Christianity's growth entirely by five natural, human causes — a framework that structures Chapters XV–XVI.
- **Intolerant zeal (the first cause)** — an exclusive devotion to one God, inherited from Judaism but freed from the "narrow and unsocial spirit" that had kept converts away from the law of Moses.
- **Judaism as defensive, not missionary** — a religion "admirably fitted for defence, but never designed for conquest": tied to one nation, one land, one temple, and guarded by rites (above all circumcision) that repelled proselytes.
- **The Nazarenes and Ebionites** — the original Jewish-Christian congregation of Jerusalem, which kept the Mosaic law for a century, conformed under Hadrian to enter Aelia Capitolina, and left behind a remnant (the Ebionites) rejected as apostates by Jews and heretics by Christians.
- **The Gnostics** — learned, wealthy Gentile heretics who rejected the Old Testament and its God outright; their more than fifty sects, Gibbon argues, actually helped Christianity spread by offering Gentiles a version free of Jewish scripture.
- **Demonology of paganism** — the belief, universal among early Christians of every party, that the pagan gods were fallen angels impersonating Jupiter, Venus, and the rest; this made even trivial participation in civic religion an act of rebellion against God.

## Modern Translation

A fair-minded but rational inquiry into how Christianity arose and established itself must count as an essential part of the history of the Roman empire. While that great body was being attacked by open violence or undermined by slow decay, a pure and humble religion gently worked its way into people's minds, grew up in silence and obscurity, drew fresh strength from opposition, and finally planted the triumphant banner of the cross on the ruins of the Capitol. Nor was Christianity's influence confined to the period or the borders of the Roman empire. After a revolution of thirteen or fourteen centuries, that religion is still professed by the nations of Europe — the portion of humankind most distinguished in arts and learning as well as in arms. Through the industry and zeal of Europeans it has been spread widely to the most distant shores of Asia and Africa; and through their colonies it has been firmly established from Canada to Chile, in a world unknown to the ancients.

But this inquiry, however useful or entertaining, comes with two peculiar difficulties. The scanty and suspect materials of church history seldom let us pierce the dark cloud that hangs over the church's first age. And the great law of impartiality too often obliges us to expose the failings of the uninspired teachers and believers of the gospel — failings which, to a careless observer, may seem to cast a shadow on the faith they professed. But the pious Christian's scandal, and the unbeliever's false sense of triumph, should both end the moment they remember not only *by whom*, but also *to whom*, the Divine Revelation was given. The theologian may indulge in the pleasing task of describing Religion as she descended from Heaven, dressed in her native purity. A more melancholy duty falls to the historian. He must uncover the inevitable mixture of error and corruption that she picked up during a long residence on earth, among a weak and degenerate race of beings.

Our curiosity naturally prompts us to ask by what means the Christian faith won so remarkable a victory over the established religions of the earth. To this question an obvious but satisfactory answer can be given: it was owing to the convincing evidence of the doctrine itself, and to the guiding providence of its great Author. But truth and reason seldom get so favorable a reception in the world; and since the wisdom of Providence frequently stoops to use the passions of the human heart, and the general circumstances of mankind, as instruments for carrying out its purpose, we may still be allowed — with all due submission — to ask not, indeed, what were the first causes, but what were the secondary causes of the Christian church's rapid growth. It will perhaps appear that its progress was most effectively favored and assisted by the following five: I. The inflexible and, if the expression may be permitted, the intolerant zeal of the Christians — derived, it is true, from the Jewish religion, but purified of the narrow and unsocial spirit which had deterred the Gentiles from embracing the law of Moses instead of inviting them to it. II. The doctrine of a future life, improved by every additional circumstance that could lend weight and force to that important truth. III. The miraculous powers ascribed to the primitive church. IV. The pure and austere morals of the Christians. V. The union and discipline of the Christian republic, which gradually formed an independent and growing state in the heart of the Roman empire.

I. We have already described the religious harmony of the ancient world, and the ease with which the most different and even hostile nations embraced, or at least respected, one another's superstitions. A single people refused to join in the common intercourse of mankind. The Jews, who under the Assyrian and Persian monarchies had languished for many ages as the most despised portion of their slaves, emerged from obscurity under the successors of Alexander; and as they multiplied to a surprising degree in the East, and afterwards in the West, they soon excited the curiosity and wonder of other nations. The sullen stubbornness with which they maintained their peculiar rites and unsociable manners seemed to mark them out as a distinct species of men, who boldly proclaimed — or faintly disguised — their implacable hostility toward the rest of humankind. Neither the violence of Antiochus, nor the arts of Herod, nor the example of the surrounding nations could ever persuade the Jews to combine the institutions of Moses with the elegant mythology of the Greeks. Following their maxims of universal toleration, the Romans protected a superstition they despised. The polished Augustus graciously ordered that sacrifices be offered for his prosperity in the temple of Jerusalem — while the humblest descendant of Abraham who had paid the same homage to the Jupiter of the Capitol would have been an object of horror to himself and to his brethren. But the conquerors' moderation was not enough to soothe the jealous prejudices of their subjects, who were alarmed and scandalized by the emblems of paganism that necessarily made their way into a Roman province. Caligula's mad attempt to place his own statue in the temple of Jerusalem was defeated by the unanimous resolve of a people who dreaded death far less than such an idolatrous profanation. Their attachment to the law of Moses was equal to their detestation of foreign religions. The current of their zeal and devotion, squeezed into a narrow channel, ran with the strength — and sometimes with the fury — of a torrent.

This inflexible perseverance, which looked so odious or so ridiculous to the ancient world, takes on a more awe-inspiring character now that Providence has deigned to reveal to us the mysterious history of the chosen people. But the devout, even scrupulous, attachment to the Mosaic religion that was so conspicuous among the Jews of the second temple becomes still more surprising when compared with the stubborn unbelief of their ancestors. When the law was given in thunder from Mount Sinai, when the tides of the ocean and the course of the planets were suspended for the convenience of the Israelites, and when temporal rewards and punishments followed immediately upon their piety or disobedience, they perpetually relapsed into rebellion against the visible majesty of their Divine King, set up the idols of the nations in the sanctuary of Jehovah, and copied every fantastic ceremony practiced in the tents of the Arabs or the cities of Phoenicia. As Heaven's protection was — deservedly — withdrawn from the ungrateful race, their faith acquired a proportionate degree of vigor and purity. The contemporaries of Moses and Joshua had watched the most amazing miracles with careless indifference. Yet under the pressure of every calamity, belief in those same miracles preserved the Jews of a later period from the universal contagion of idolatry; and — in contradiction to every known principle of the human mind — that singular people seems to have given a stronger and readier assent to the traditions of their remote ancestors than to the evidence of their own senses.

The Jewish religion was admirably fitted for defense, but it was never designed for conquest; and it seems probable that the number of proselytes was never much greater than the number of apostates. The divine promises were originally made, and the distinguishing rite of circumcision imposed, on a single family. When the descendants of Abraham had multiplied like the sands of the sea, the Deity from whose mouth they received a system of laws and ceremonies declared himself the proper — one might almost say the national — God of Israel, and with the most jealous care separated his favorite people from the rest of mankind. The conquest of the land of Canaan was accompanied by so many wonders and so much bloodshed that the victorious Jews were left in a state of irreconcilable hostility with all their neighbors. They had been commanded to exterminate some of the most idolatrous tribes, and the execution of the Divine will was seldom slowed by the weakness of human feeling. With the other nations they were forbidden to contract marriages or alliances; and the ban on receiving them into the congregation — in some cases perpetual — almost always extended to the third, the seventh, or even the tenth generation. The duty of preaching the faith of Moses to the Gentiles was never taught as a precept of the law, nor were the Jews inclined to take it on as a voluntary obligation. In admitting new citizens, that unsociable people acted from the selfish vanity of the Greeks rather than the generous policy of Rome. The descendants of Abraham were flattered by the belief that they alone were heirs of the covenant, and they feared cheapening their inheritance by sharing it too easily with the strangers of the earth. Wider acquaintance with mankind extended their knowledge without correcting their prejudices; and whenever the God of Israel gained new worshippers, he owed far more to the fickle temper of polytheism than to the active zeal of his own missionaries. The religion of Moses seems to have been instituted for a particular country as well as for a single nation; and if the command that every male present himself before the Lord Jehovah three times a year had been strictly obeyed, the Jews could never have spread beyond the narrow limits of the promised land. That obstacle was indeed removed by the destruction of the temple of Jerusalem — but the most considerable part of the Jewish religion perished with it; and the Pagans, who had long wondered at the strange report of an empty sanctuary, were at a loss to discover what could be the object, or the instruments, of a worship that had neither temples nor altars, neither priests nor sacrifices. Yet even in their fallen state the Jews, still asserting their lofty and exclusive privileges, shunned rather than courted the society of strangers. They still insisted with inflexible rigor on the parts of the law it remained in their power to practice. Their peculiar distinctions of days and foods, and a variety of trivial but burdensome observances, were so many sources of disgust and aversion to the other nations, whose habits and prejudices ran directly counter to them. The painful and even dangerous rite of circumcision was, by itself, enough to turn a willing proselyte away from the door of the synagogue.

Under these circumstances, Christianity offered itself to the world armed with the strength of the Mosaic law and freed from the weight of its fetters. An exclusive zeal for religious truth and the unity of God was taught as carefully in the new system as in the old; and everything now revealed to mankind about the nature and designs of the Supreme Being was fitted to increase their reverence for that mysterious doctrine. The divine authority of Moses and the prophets was admitted — indeed established — as the firmest foundation of Christianity. From the beginning of the world an unbroken series of predictions had announced and prepared the long-expected coming of the Messiah, who, in accommodation to the crude conceptions of the Jews, had more often been represented as a King and Conqueror than as a Prophet, a Martyr, and the Son of God. By his expiatory sacrifice, the imperfect sacrifices of the temple were at once consummated and abolished. The ceremonial law, which consisted only of types and figures, gave way to a pure and spiritual worship equally suited to every climate and every condition of mankind; and for the initiation of blood was substituted a more harmless initiation of water. The promise of divine favor, instead of being confined to the descendants of Abraham, was offered universally — to the free man and the slave, to the Greek and the barbarian, to the Jew and the Gentile. Every privilege that could raise the convert from earth to heaven, that could exalt his devotion, secure his happiness, or even gratify that secret pride which, under the appearance of devotion, insinuates itself into the human heart, was still reserved for members of the Christian church; but at the same time all mankind was permitted — indeed urged — to accept this glorious distinction, which was not only offered as a favor but imposed as an obligation. It became the most sacred duty of a new convert to spread among his friends and relations the priceless blessing he had received, and to warn them against a refusal that would be severely punished as criminal disobedience to the will of a benevolent but all-powerful Deity.

Freeing the church from the bonds of the synagogue was, however, a work of some time and some difficulty. The Jewish converts, who acknowledged Jesus as the Messiah foretold by their ancient oracles, respected him as a prophetic teacher of virtue and religion; but they clung stubbornly to the ceremonies of their ancestors, and wanted to impose those ceremonies on the Gentiles who were continually swelling the number of believers. These Judaizing Christians seem to have argued with some plausibility from the divine origin of the Mosaic law and the immutable perfections of its great Author. They maintained *that*, if the Being who is the same through all eternity had intended to abolish the sacred rites that distinguished his chosen people, their repeal would have been no less clear and solemn than their original proclamation; *that*, instead of the frequent declarations which either assume or assert the perpetuity of the Mosaic religion, it would have been presented as a provisional scheme meant to last only until the coming of the Messiah, who would instruct mankind in a more perfect mode of faith and worship; *that* the Messiah himself, and the disciples who conversed with him on earth, instead of sanctioning by their example the most minute observances of the Mosaic law, would have proclaimed to the world the abolition of those useless and obsolete ceremonies, rather than allowing Christianity to remain for so many years obscurely mixed in among the sects of the Jewish church. Arguments like these appear to have been deployed in defense of the dying cause of the Mosaic law; but the industry of our learned divines has abundantly explained away the ambiguous language of the Old Testament and the ambiguous conduct of the apostolic teachers. It was proper to unfold the system of the Gospel gradually, and to pronounce, with the utmost caution and tenderness, a sentence of condemnation so repugnant to the inclinations and prejudices of the believing Jews.

The history of the church of Jerusalem offers vivid proof of the need for these precautions, and of how deep an impression the Jewish religion had made on the minds of its adherents. The first fifteen bishops of Jerusalem were all circumcised Jews, and the congregation they presided over combined the law of Moses with the doctrine of Christ. It was natural that the original tradition of a church founded only forty days after the death of Christ, and governed for almost as many years under the direct supervision of his apostle, should be received as the standard of orthodoxy. The distant churches very frequently appealed to the authority of their venerable Parent, and relieved her hardships with generous contributions of alms. But once numerous and wealthy congregations were established in the great cities of the empire — in Antioch, Alexandria, Ephesus, Corinth, and Rome — the reverence Jerusalem had inspired in all the Christian colonies gradually faded. The Jewish converts, or, as they were afterwards called, the Nazarenes, who had laid the foundations of the church, soon found themselves swamped by the growing multitudes who enlisted under the banner of Christ from all the various religions of polytheism; and the Gentiles, who with the approval of their own particular apostle had rejected the intolerable weight of the Mosaic ceremonies, in the end refused their more scrupulous brethren the same toleration they had at first humbly requested for their own practice. The ruin of the temple, the city, and the public religion of the Jews fell heavily on the Nazarenes; for in their manners, though not in their faith, they kept up an intimate connection with their impious countrymen — whose misfortunes the Pagans attributed to the contempt, and the Christians more justly to the wrath, of the Supreme Deity. The Nazarenes withdrew from the ruins of Jerusalem to the little town of Pella beyond the Jordan, where that ancient church languished for more than sixty years in solitude and obscurity. They still enjoyed the comfort of making frequent and devout visits to the *Holy City*, and the hope of one day being restored to those seats which both nature and religion taught them to love as well as revere. But at length, in the reign of Hadrian, the desperate fanaticism of the Jews filled the measure of their calamities; and the Romans, exasperated by their repeated rebellions, exercised the rights of victory with unusual severity. The emperor founded a new city on Mount Zion under the name of Aelia Capitolina and gave it the privileges of a colony; and, decreeing the severest penalties against any Jew who dared approach its precincts, he stationed a vigilant garrison of a Roman cohort to enforce his orders. The Nazarenes had only one way to escape the general proscription, and on this occasion the force of truth was assisted by the influence of worldly advantage. They elected as their bishop Marcus, a prelate of Gentile stock, most probably a native of Italy or one of the Latin provinces. At his urging, the greater part of the congregation renounced the Mosaic law, which they had practiced for more than a century. By this sacrifice of their habits and prejudices they purchased free admission into Hadrian's colony, and cemented more firmly their union with the Catholic church.

When the name and honors of the church of Jerusalem had been restored on Mount Zion, the crimes of heresy and schism were imputed to the obscure remnant of Nazarenes who refused to follow their Latin bishop. They kept their former home at Pella, spread into the villages around Damascus, and formed a small church in the Syrian city of Beroea — or, as it is now called, Aleppo. The name of Nazarenes was thought too honorable for these Christian Jews, and from the supposed poverty of their understanding, as well as of their circumstances, they soon received the contemptuous label of Ebionites. Within a few years of the church's return to Jerusalem, it became a matter of doubt and controversy whether a man who sincerely acknowledged Jesus as the Messiah, but who still observed the law of Moses, could possibly hope for salvation. The humane temper of Justin Martyr inclined him to answer yes; and though he expressed himself with the most guarded diffidence, he ventured to decide in favor of such an imperfect Christian, provided he was content to practice the Mosaic ceremonies without claiming they were generally useful or necessary. But when Justin was pressed to state the view of the church, he admitted that there were a great many orthodox Christians who not only excluded their Judaizing brethren from any hope of salvation, but refused to have anything to do with them in the ordinary offices of friendship, hospitality, and social life. The harsher opinion prevailed over the milder — as was only to be expected — and a permanent wall of separation was fixed between the disciples of Moses and those of Christ. The unfortunate Ebionites, rejected by one religion as apostates and by the other as heretics, found themselves forced to take on a more definite identity of their own; and although traces of that obsolete sect can be found as late as the fourth century, they gradually melted away into either the church or the synagogue.

While the orthodox church kept a judicious middle course between excessive veneration for the law of Moses and improper contempt for it, the various heretics veered into equal but opposite extremes of error and extravagance. From the acknowledged truth of the Jewish religion, the Ebionites had concluded it could never be abolished. From its supposed imperfections, the Gnostics inferred just as hastily that it had never been instituted by the wisdom of the Deity. There are certain objections to the authority of Moses and the prophets that present themselves all too readily to the skeptical mind — though they can arise only from our ignorance of remote antiquity and our incapacity to judge the divine economy (God's plan of government) adequately. These objections were eagerly embraced, and just as petulantly pressed, by the vain learning of the Gnostics. Since these heretics were for the most part averse to the pleasures of the senses, they sourly denounced the polygamy of the patriarchs, the love affairs of David, and the harem of Solomon. The conquest of the land of Canaan, and the extermination of its unsuspecting natives, they could not see how to reconcile with common notions of humanity and justice. And when they recalled the bloody catalogue of murders, executions, and massacres that stain almost every page of the Jewish annals, they concluded that the barbarians of Palestine had shown their idolatrous enemies quite as much compassion as they had ever shown their own friends or countrymen. Passing from the followers of the law to the law itself, they asserted that a religion consisting only of bloody sacrifices and trifling ceremonies — a religion whose rewards, like its punishments, were all of a bodily and temporal nature — could not possibly inspire the love of virtue or restrain the violence of passion. The Mosaic account of the creation and fall of man was treated with profane derision by the Gnostics, who would not listen patiently to the Deity's rest after six days' labor, the rib of Adam, the garden of Eden, the trees of life and of knowledge, the talking serpent, the forbidden fruit, and the sentence pronounced against all humankind for the venial offense of their first parents. The God of Israel was impiously represented by the Gnostics as a being subject to passion and to error — capricious in his favor, implacable in his resentment, meanly jealous of his superstitious worship, and confining his partial providence to a single people and to this transitory life. In such a character they could discover none of the features of the wise and omnipotent Father of the universe. They granted that the religion of the Jews was somewhat less criminal than the idolatry of the Gentiles; but their fundamental doctrine was that the Christ whom they adored as the first and brightest emanation of the Deity had appeared on earth to rescue mankind from its various errors, and to reveal a *new* system of truth and perfection. The most learned of the church fathers, by a very singular concession, have imprudently admitted the sophistry of the Gnostics. Acknowledging that the literal sense is repugnant to every principle of faith as well as of reason, they consider themselves safe and invulnerable behind the ample veil of allegory, which they carefully spread over every tender part of the Mosaic dispensation.

It has been remarked, with more ingenuity than truth, that the virgin purity of the church was never violated by schism or heresy before the reign of Trajan or Hadrian, about a hundred years after the death of Christ. We may observe, with much more accuracy, that during that period the disciples of the Messiah were allowed a freer latitude of both faith and practice than has ever been permitted in later ages. As the terms of communion were gradually narrowed, and the spiritual authority of the dominant party was exercised with growing severity, many of its most respectable adherents, when called upon to renounce their private opinions, were provoked instead to assert them, to follow out the consequences of their mistaken principles, and to raise openly the standard of rebellion against the unity of the church. The Gnostics were distinguished as the most polished, the most learned, and the wealthiest bearers of the Christian name; and that general label, which expressed a superiority of knowledge, was either assumed by their own pride or ironically bestowed by the envy of their adversaries. They were almost without exception of Gentile stock, and their principal founders seem to have been natives of Syria or Egypt, where the warmth of the climate disposes both mind and body to indolent, contemplative devotion. The Gnostics blended with the faith of Christ many sublime but obscure tenets drawn from oriental philosophy, and even from the religion of Zoroaster, concerning the eternity of matter, the existence of two principles, and the mysterious hierarchy of the invisible world. Once they launched out into that vast abyss, they surrendered themselves to the guidance of a disordered imagination; and since the paths of error are various and infinite, the Gnostics divided imperceptibly into more than fifty distinct sects, of which the most celebrated appear to have been the Basilidians, the Valentinians, the Marcionites, and, in a still later period, the Manichaeans. Each of these sects could boast its own bishops and congregations, its own doctors and martyrs; and in place of the four gospels adopted by the church, the heretics produced a multitude of histories in which the actions and sayings of Christ and his apostles were adapted to their respective tenets. The success of the Gnostics was rapid and extensive. They covered Asia and Egypt, established themselves in Rome, and sometimes penetrated the provinces of the West. For the most part they arose in the second century, flourished during the third, and were suppressed in the fourth or fifth, by the prevalence of more fashionable controversies and by the superior ascendancy of the reigning power. Though they constantly disturbed the peace of religion, and frequently disgraced its name, they helped to advance rather than retard the progress of Christianity. Gentile converts, whose strongest objections and prejudices were directed against the law of Moses, could find admission into many Christian societies that demanded of their untutored minds no belief in a prior revelation. Their faith was gradually strengthened and enlarged, and the church was ultimately the beneficiary of the conquests of its most inveterate enemies.

But whatever differences of opinion existed among the Orthodox, the Ebionites, and the Gnostics about the divinity or the binding force of the Mosaic law, they were all animated by the same exclusive zeal, and by the same abhorrence of idolatry, that had distinguished the Jews from the other nations of the ancient world. The philosopher, who regarded the system of polytheism as a compound of human fraud and error, could hide a smile of contempt behind the mask of devotion, without any fear that either the mockery or the compliance would expose him to the resentment of powers that were invisible — or, as he conceived them, imaginary. But the established religions of Paganism appeared to the primitive Christians in a far more odious and menacing light. It was the universal conviction of church and heretics alike that the demons were the authors, the patrons, and the objects of idolatry. Those rebellious spirits, degraded from the rank of angels and cast down into the infernal pit, were still permitted to roam the earth, to torment the bodies and seduce the minds of sinful men. The demons had quickly discovered and exploited the human heart's natural inclination to worship; and by artfully diverting mankind's adoration away from their Creator, they usurped the place and honors of the Supreme Deity. Through the success of their malicious schemes they gratified their own vanity and revenge at a stroke, and obtained the only comfort they were still capable of feeling: the hope of dragging the human race down into a share of their guilt and misery. It was confessed — or at least imagined — that they had divided among themselves the most important roles of polytheism: one demon assuming the name and attributes of Jupiter, another of Aesculapius, a third of Venus, a fourth perhaps of Apollo; and that, thanks to their long experience and aerial nature, they were able to play their chosen parts with sufficient skill and dignity. They lurked in the temples, instituted festivals and sacrifices, invented fables, pronounced oracles, and were frequently allowed to perform miracles. The Christians, who could so readily explain every seemingly supernatural occurrence by the intervention of evil spirits, were disposed — even eager — to accept the most extravagant fictions of Pagan mythology. But the Christian's belief was accompanied by horror. He regarded the most trifling mark of respect to the national worship as direct homage paid to the demon, and as an act of rebellion against the majesty of God.
