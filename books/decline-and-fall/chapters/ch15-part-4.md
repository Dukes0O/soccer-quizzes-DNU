# Chapter XV, Part 4 — Church Government, Wealth, and Discipline; the Five Causes Reviewed

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XV (The Progress of the Christian Religion, and the Sentiments, Manners, Numbers, and Condition of the Primitive Christians), Part 4 of 5
>

## Overview

This part completes Gibbon's fifth "secondary cause" of Christianity's success — the union and discipline of the Christian republic — and then pivots to the chapter's final movement: an assessment of how far, and why, the new religion actually spread. He first traces the escalation of ecclesiastical hierarchy: just as bishops had risen above presbyters, metropolitan bishops now rose above their episcopal brethren, and the bishop of Rome, armed with the double apostolate of Peter and Paul and the prestige of the capital, began claiming an obedience the churches of Asia and Africa vigorously refused — a "Punic war" of invectives fought between Cyprian of Carthage and the Roman pontiff.

Gibbon then examines the two "most efficacious instruments of government" the clergy wielded: rewards and punishments. Under rewards he covers church finance — the brief experiment in community of goods, the doctrine of tithes, the surprising wealth of the third-century churches, and the genuinely admirable charity (support of widows, orphans, prisoners, and exposed infants) that, he concedes, "very materially conduced to the progress of Christianity." Under punishments he covers excommunication and public penance, contrasting the rigor of the Spanish council of Illiberis with the comparative mildness of Ancyra, and closing on Cyprian's thunderous insistence that episcopal authority *is* Christianity itself.

The last third of the part recapitulates the five secondary causes as a military campaign — zeal supplying valor, the church's constitution supplying discipline — and explains why polytheism was so poorly defended: its priests were amateurs without corporate interest, and educated skepticism had already hollowed out popular belief, leaving a vacancy in the human heart that some new superstition was bound to fill. The famous deadpan aside that Providence happened to interpose "a genuine revelation" at exactly this moment is Gibbon's irony at its purest. He ends by beginning his geographical survey: Rome's roads, laws, and common languages carried the faith everywhere, and in Syria, Asia Minor, and Greece — as Lucian's complaints and Pliny's letter to Trajan attest — the churches were oldest and most numerous.

## Key Concepts

- **Metropolitans and primates** — bishops of provincial capitals who became perpetual presidents of synods and repeated, one level up, the usurpation the bishops had practiced on the presbyters.
- **Roman primacy resisted** — Rome's church, greatest and (in the West) most ancient, claimed the inheritance of St. Peter; the provinces allowed a "primacy of order and association" but rejected monarchy, with Cyprian leading the resistance.
- **Laity and clergy** — a distinction unknown to Greeks and Romans, born of the growth of ecclesiastical authority; the clergy governed through rewards and punishments.
- **Church revenue** — from a short-lived community of goods to voluntary offerings, tithes urged as divine obligation, and eventually landed estates despite mortmain-style laws.
- **The patrimony of the poor** — organized charity keyed to distress rather than merit, which won pagan respect and drew in the destitute, the sick, and rescued exposed infants.
- **Excommunication and penance** — expulsion carrying both social disgrace and the terror of the keys of Hell and Paradise; readmission only by slow, public, humiliating gradations.
- **The five secondary causes reviewed** — zeal, the expectation of another world, miracles, rigid virtue, and church constitution, marshaled like an army against an undisciplined multitude.
- **The weakness of polytheism** — part-time aristocratic priests without corporate interest, and a populace whose belief had been undermined by fashionable skepticism yet still craved the supernatural.

## Modern Translation

The same forces that had first destroyed the equality of the presbyters now introduced among the bishops a preeminence of rank, and from that, a superiority of jurisdiction. Whenever they met in provincial synod each spring and autumn, differences of personal merit and reputation made themselves keenly felt in the assembly, and the many were governed by the wisdom and eloquence of the few. But orderly public proceedings required a more regular and less resented form of distinction, so the office of perpetual president of each province's councils was conferred on the bishop of its principal city. These ambitious prelates, who soon acquired the lofty titles of Metropolitans and Primates, quietly prepared to usurp over their episcopal brethren the same authority the bishops had so recently assumed over the college of presbyters. Nor was it long before a rivalry for preeminence and power broke out among the metropolitans themselves. Each made a point of advertising, in the most grandiose terms, the worldly honors and advantages of the city over which he presided; the numbers and wealth of the Christians under his pastoral care; the saints and martyrs who had arisen among them; and the purity with which they had preserved the tradition of the faith, handed down through an unbroken line of orthodox bishops from the apostle, or apostolic disciple, credited with founding their church. On every ground, civil or ecclesiastical, it was easy to foresee that Rome would enjoy the respect — and would soon claim the obedience — of the provinces. The community of the faithful was proportioned to the capital of the empire, and the Roman church was the greatest, the most numerous, and, as far as the West was concerned, the most ancient of all the Christian establishments, many of which had received their religion through the pious labors of her missionaries. Instead of *one* apostolic founder — the utmost boast of Antioch, Ephesus, or Corinth — the banks of the Tiber were believed to have been honored with the preaching and martyrdom of the *two* most eminent apostles; and the bishops of Rome very prudently claimed to inherit whatever prerogatives had ever been attributed either to the person or to the office of St. Peter. The bishops of Italy and the provinces were willing to allow them a primacy of order and association — that was their carefully chosen phrase — within the Christian aristocracy. But the power of a monarch they rejected with abhorrence, and the aspiring genius of Rome met, from the nations of Asia and Africa, a more vigorous resistance to her spiritual dominion than she had formerly met to her temporal one. The patriotic Cyprian, who ruled the church of Carthage and the provincial synods with the most absolute sway, opposed the ambition of the Roman pontiff with resolution and success, artfully tied his own cause to that of the eastern bishops, and, like Hannibal, sought out new allies in the heart of Asia. If this Punic war was fought without any shedding of blood, the credit belongs far less to the moderation of the contending prelates than to their weakness. Invectives and excommunications were the only weapons *they* had, and throughout the whole controversy they hurled them at each other with equal fury and devotion. The hard necessity of censuring either a pope, or a saint and martyr, embarrasses modern Catholics whenever they are obliged to relate the details of a dispute in which the champions of religion indulged passions far better suited to the senate or the camp.

The growth of ecclesiastical authority gave birth to the memorable distinction between laity and clergy, a distinction unknown to the Greeks and Romans. The first of these terms took in the whole body of the Christian people; the second, following the meaning of the word, was reserved for the chosen portion set apart for the service of religion — a celebrated order of men that has supplied modern history with its most important, though not always its most edifying, subjects. Their mutual hostilities sometimes disturbed the peace of the infant church, but their zeal and energy were united in the common cause; and the love of power — which, under the most artful disguises, could insinuate itself into the breasts of bishops and martyrs alike — spurred them to increase the number of their subjects and to enlarge the limits of the Christian empire. They had no worldly force at their disposal, and for a long time the civil magistrate discouraged and oppressed them rather than helped them; but within their own society they had acquired, and they employed, the two most effective instruments of government: rewards and punishments. The first flowed from the pious generosity of the faithful, the second from their devout fears.

I. The community of goods, which had so pleasantly entertained the imagination of Plato, and which existed in some degree among the austere sect of the Essenians, was adopted for a short time in the primitive church. The fervor of the first converts prompted them to sell the worldly possessions they despised, lay the proceeds at the feet of the apostles, and content themselves with an equal share of the general distribution. The progress of the Christian religion relaxed, and gradually abolished, this generous institution — which, in hands less pure than the apostles', would all too quickly have been corrupted and abused by the returning selfishness of human nature. Converts to the new religion were permitted to keep their inheritances, to receive legacies and bequests, and to increase their private property by every lawful means of trade and industry. Instead of a total sacrifice, the ministers of the gospel accepted a moderate proportion; and at their weekly or monthly assemblies, each believer, according to the need of the moment and the measure of his wealth and piety, presented his voluntary offering for the common fund. Nothing, however trivial, was refused; but it was diligently taught that on the matter of tithes the Mosaic law remained divinely binding, and that since the Jews, under a less perfect dispensation, had been commanded to pay a tenth of all they possessed, the disciples of Christ ought to distinguish themselves by a superior degree of generosity, and earn some merit by giving up a superfluous treasure that must so soon be annihilated along with the world itself. It hardly needs saying that the revenue of any particular church, being so uncertain and fluctuating, must have varied with the poverty or wealth of the faithful, scattered as they were through obscure villages or gathered in the great cities of the empire. In the time of the emperor Decius, the magistrates believed that the Christians of Rome possessed very considerable wealth; that vessels of gold and silver were used in their worship; and that many of their converts had sold their lands and houses to swell the public riches of the sect — at the expense, admittedly, of their unfortunate children, who found themselves beggars because their parents had been saints. We should listen with distrust to the suspicions of strangers and enemies; but on this occasion those suspicions gain a very plausible and probable coloring from two circumstances — the only ones that have come down to us specifying precise sums or conveying any distinct idea. At almost the same period, the bishop of Carthage, drawing on a society less wealthy than Rome's, collected a hundred thousand sesterces (above eight hundred and fifty pounds sterling) on a sudden appeal of charity, to ransom the brethren of Numidia who had been carried off as captives by the barbarians of the desert. About a hundred years before the reign of Decius, the Roman church had received, in a single donation, two hundred thousand sesterces from a stranger from Pontus who proposed to settle in the capital. These offerings were mostly made in money; the society of Christians was neither eager nor able to acquire, to any considerable degree, the encumbrance of landed property. Several laws, enacted with the same purpose as our statutes of mortmain, provided that no real estate could be given or bequeathed to any corporate body without a special privilege or particular dispensation from the emperor or the senate — who were rarely inclined to grant one in favor of a sect that was at first the object of their contempt, and at last of their fears and jealousy. A transaction under the reign of Alexander Severus, however, reveals that the restriction was sometimes evaded or suspended, and that the Christians were permitted to claim and possess land within the limits of Rome itself. The progress of Christianity and the civil turmoil of the empire combined to relax the severity of the laws; and before the close of the third century, many substantial estates had been bestowed on the wealthy churches of Rome, Milan, Carthage, Antioch, Alexandria, and the other great cities of Italy and the provinces.

The bishop was the natural steward of the church. The common fund was entrusted to his care without audit or oversight; the presbyters were confined to their spiritual functions; and the more dependent order of deacons was employed solely in managing and distributing the ecclesiastical revenue. If we may believe Cyprian's vehement denunciations, too many of his African brethren, in carrying out this charge, violated every precept not only of evangelical perfection but even of ordinary moral virtue. Some of these unfaithful stewards squandered the riches of the church on sensual pleasures; others diverted them to private gain, fraudulent purchases, and rapacious moneylending. But as long as the contributions of the Christian people remained free and unconstrained, such betrayals of their confidence cannot have been very frequent, and the general uses to which their generosity was put reflected honor on the religious society. A decent portion was reserved for the maintenance of the bishop and his clergy; a sufficient sum was allotted for the expenses of public worship, of which the love-feasts — the *agapae*, as they were called — formed a very agreeable part. The whole remainder was the sacred patrimony of the poor. At the bishop's discretion it was distributed to support the widows and orphans, the lame, the sick, and the aged of the community; to comfort strangers and pilgrims; and to relieve the misfortunes of prisoners and captives, especially when their sufferings had been brought on by their firm attachment to the cause of religion. A generous traffic of charity united the most distant provinces, and the smaller congregations were cheerfully assisted by the alms of their wealthier brethren. An institution of this kind, which looked less to the merit of its object than to his distress, contributed very materially to the progress of Christianity. Pagans moved by ordinary humanity, while they mocked the doctrines of the new sect, acknowledged its benevolence. The prospect of immediate relief and future protection drew into its hospitable bosom many of those unhappy people whom the neglect of the world would have abandoned to the miseries of want, sickness, and old age. There is also some reason to believe that great numbers of infants who had been exposed by their parents — according to the inhuman practice of the times — were frequently rescued from death, baptized, educated, and maintained by the piety of the Christians, at the expense of the public treasury.

II. Every society has the undoubted right to exclude from its communion and its benefits those members who reject or violate the rules established by general consent. In exercising this power, the censures of the Christian church were directed chiefly against scandalous sinners, particularly those guilty of murder, fraud, or sexual immorality; against the authors, or followers, of any heretical opinions condemned by the judgment of the episcopal order; and against those unhappy persons who, whether by choice or under compulsion, had polluted themselves after baptism by any act of idolatrous worship. The consequences of excommunication were temporal as well as spiritual. The Christian against whom it was pronounced lost all share in the offerings of the faithful. The ties of religious and of private friendship alike were dissolved: he found himself an unclean object of abhorrence to the very people he most esteemed, or by whom he had been most tenderly loved; and, insofar as expulsion from a respectable society could stamp a mark of disgrace on his character, he was shunned or suspected by mankind at large. The situation of these unfortunate exiles was painful and melancholy enough in itself; but, as usually happens, their fears far exceeded their actual sufferings. The benefits of the Christian communion were those of eternal life, and they could not erase from their minds the awful conviction that the Deity had committed the keys of Hell and of Paradise to the very ecclesiastical governors who had condemned them. The heretics, it is true — sustained by the consciousness of their own intentions, and by the flattering hope that they alone had discovered the true path of salvation — tried to recover in their separate assemblies the comforts, temporal as well as spiritual, they no longer drew from the great society of Christians. But almost all of those who had reluctantly yielded to the power of vice or idolatry felt their fallen condition, and anxiously longed to be restored to the benefits of the Christian communion.

On the treatment of these penitents, two opposite opinions — one of justice, the other of mercy — divided the primitive church. The more rigid and inflexible casuists refused them, forever and without exception, even the lowest place in the holy community they had disgraced or deserted; abandoning them to the remorse of a guilty conscience, they indulged them only with a faint ray of hope that the contrition of their life and death might possibly be accepted by the Supreme Being. A milder view was embraced, in practice as well as in theory, by the purest and most respectable of the Christian churches. The gates of reconciliation and of Heaven were seldom shut against the returning penitent; but a severe and solemn regime of discipline was instituted which, while it served to expiate his crime, would powerfully deter onlookers from imitating his example. Humbled by public confession, emaciated by fasting, and clothed in sackcloth, the penitent lay prostrate at the door of the assembly, begging with tears for the pardon of his offences and soliciting the prayers of the faithful. If the fault was especially heinous, whole years of penance were reckoned inadequate satisfaction to divine justice; and it was always by slow and painful stages that the sinner, the heretic, or the apostate was readmitted into the bosom of the church. A sentence of perpetual excommunication was, however, reserved for certain crimes of extraordinary magnitude — particularly for the inexcusable relapses of penitents who had already experienced, and abused, the clemency of their ecclesiastical superiors. Depending on the circumstances or the number of the guilty, the application of Christian discipline varied at the bishops' discretion. The councils of Ancyra and Illiberis were held at about the same time, one in Galatia and the other in Spain; but their respective canons, which still survive, seem to breathe very different spirits. The Galatian who had repeatedly sacrificed to idols after his baptism could obtain pardon by a penance of seven years, and if he had seduced others into imitating his example, only three more years were added to his term of exile. But the unhappy Spaniard who had committed the same offence was denied all hope of reconciliation, even at the moment of death; and his idolatry stood at the head of a list of seventeen other crimes against which a sentence no less terrible was pronounced. Among these we may single out the inexpiable guilt of slandering a bishop, a presbyter, or even a deacon.

This well-tempered blend of liberality and rigor — this judicious dispensing of rewards and punishments according to the maxims of policy as well as of justice — constituted the *human* strength of the church. The bishops, whose paternal care extended to the government of both worlds, understood the importance of these prerogatives; and, covering their ambition with the fair pretext of a love of order, they were jealous of any rival in the exercise of a discipline so necessary to prevent desertion among the troops who had enlisted under the banner of the cross, and whose numbers grew more considerable every day. From Cyprian's imperious declamations we would naturally conclude that the doctrines of excommunication and penance formed the most essential part of religion, and that it was far less dangerous for the disciples of Christ to neglect their moral duties than to despise the censures and authority of their bishops. Sometimes we might imagine we were listening to the voice of Moses, commanding the earth to open and swallow up in consuming flames the rebellious race that refused obedience to the priesthood of Aaron; and sometimes we might suppose we were hearing a Roman consul asserting the majesty of the republic and declaring his inflexible resolve to enforce the rigor of the laws. "If such irregularities are suffered with impunity" — it is thus that the bishop of Carthage chides the leniency of his colleague — "if such irregularities are suffered, there is an end of episcopal vigor; an end of the sublime and divine power of governing the Church; an end of Christianity itself." Cyprian had renounced worldly honors — honors, it is probable, he would never have obtained; but such absolute command over the consciences and understanding of a congregation, however obscure or despised by the world, is more truly gratifying to the pride of the human heart than the possession of the most despotic power imposed by arms and conquest on a reluctant people.

In the course of this important, though perhaps tedious, inquiry, I have tried to display the secondary causes that so effectively assisted the truth of the Christian religion. If among these causes we have discovered any artificial ornaments, any accidental circumstances, or any mixture of error and passion, it can hardly be surprising that mankind should be most powerfully affected by motives suited to their imperfect nature. It was by the aid of these causes — exclusive zeal, the immediate expectation of another world, the claim of miracles, the practice of rigid virtue, and the constitution of the primitive church — that Christianity spread with such success through the Roman empire. To the first of them the Christians owed their invincible valor, which disdained any terms with the enemy they were resolved to vanquish. The three causes that follow supplied that valor with the most formidable arms. The last of them united their courage, directed their arms, and gave their efforts the irresistible weight that a small band of well-trained and intrepid volunteers has so often possessed over an undisciplined multitude ignorant of the cause and careless of the outcome of the war. Among the various religions of polytheism, a few wandering fanatics of Egypt and Syria, who preyed upon the credulous superstition of the populace, were perhaps the only order of priests who derived their whole livelihood and standing from their sacerdotal profession, and who felt any deep personal stake in the safety or prosperity of their tutelary deities. The ministers of polytheism, at Rome and in the provinces alike, were for the most part men of noble birth and ample fortune, who received the care of a celebrated temple or a public sacrifice as an honorable distinction, exhibited the sacred games — very often at their own expense — and performed the ancient rites with cold indifference, according to the laws and customs of their country. Engaged as they were in the ordinary business of life, their zeal and devotion were rarely animated by self-interest or by the habits of an ecclesiastical character. Confined to their respective temples and cities, they remained without any bond of discipline or government; and while they acknowledged the supreme jurisdiction of the senate, of the college of pontiffs, and of the emperor, those civil magistrates contented themselves with the easy task of maintaining, in peace and dignity, the general worship of mankind. We have already seen how various, how loose, and how uncertain the religious sentiments of polytheists were. They were left, almost without restraint, to the natural workings of a superstitious imagination. The accidental circumstances of their lives and situations determined the object, as well as the degree, of their devotion; and as long as their adoration was successively prostituted to a thousand deities, it was scarcely possible for their hearts to feel a very sincere or lively passion for any one of them.

By the time Christianity appeared in the world, even these faint and imperfect impressions had lost much of their original power. Human reason — which by its unassisted strength is incapable of perceiving the mysteries of faith — had already won an easy triumph over the folly of paganism; and when Tertullian or Lactantius set about exposing its falsehood and extravagance, they are obliged to transcribe the eloquence of Cicero or the wit of Lucian. The contagion of these skeptical writings had spread far beyond the circle of their readers. The fashion of unbelief passed from the philosopher to the man of pleasure or business, from the noble to the plebeian, and from the master to the household slave who waited at his table and listened eagerly to the freedom of his conversation. On public occasions the philosophic part of mankind made a show of treating the religious institutions of their country with respect and decency; but their secret contempt showed through the thin and clumsy disguise. And when the common people discovered that their deities were rejected and derided by the very men whose rank or intellect they were accustomed to revere, even they were filled with doubts and misgivings about the truth of doctrines to which they had given the most implicit belief. The decline of ancient prejudice exposed a very large portion of humankind to the danger of a painful and comfortless condition. A state of skepticism and suspense may entertain a few inquisitive minds; but the practice of superstition is so congenial to the multitude that, if forcibly awakened, they still regret the loss of their pleasing dream. Their love of the marvellous and supernatural, their curiosity about future events, and their strong propensity to extend their hopes and fears beyond the limits of the visible world were the principal causes that had favored the establishment of polytheism. So urgent, among the common people, is the necessity of believing, that the fall of any system of mythology will most probably be followed by the introduction of some other form of superstition. Some deities of a newer and more fashionable cast might soon have occupied the deserted temples of Jupiter and Apollo, if, at the decisive moment, the wisdom of Providence had not interposed a genuine revelation — one fitted to inspire the most rational esteem and conviction, while at the same time adorned with everything that could attract the curiosity, the wonder, and the veneration of the people. In their actual state of mind — largely disengaged from their artificial prejudices, yet just as susceptible to, and desirous of, a devout attachment — an object far less deserving would have sufficed to fill the vacant place in their hearts and gratify the restless eagerness of their passions. Those inclined to pursue this reflection, instead of marvelling at the rapid progress of Christianity, will perhaps be surprised that its success was not still more rapid and still more universal.

It has been observed, with truth as well as propriety, that the conquests of Rome prepared and facilitated those of Christianity. In the second chapter of this work we tried to explain how the most civilized provinces of Europe, Asia, and Africa were united under the dominion of one sovereign, and gradually bound together by the most intimate ties of law, manners, and language. The Jews of Palestine, who had fondly expected a temporal deliverer, gave so cold a reception to the miracles of the divine prophet that it was found unnecessary to publish — or at least to preserve — any Hebrew gospel. The authentic histories of the actions of Christ were composed in Greek, at a considerable distance from Jerusalem, and only after the Gentile converts had grown extremely numerous. As soon as those histories were translated into Latin, they were perfectly intelligible to all the subjects of Rome, with the sole exception of the peasants of Syria and Egypt, for whose benefit special versions were later made. The public highways, built for the use of the legions, opened an easy passage for the Christian missionaries from Damascus to Corinth, and from Italy to the farthest reaches of Spain or Britain; nor did those spiritual conquerors meet any of the obstacles that usually delay or prevent the introduction of a foreign religion into a distant country. There is the strongest reason to believe that before the reigns of Diocletian and Constantine the faith of Christ had been preached in every province and in all the great cities of the empire; but the founding of the individual congregations, the numbers of the faithful who composed them, and their proportion to the unbelieving multitude are now buried in obscurity, or disguised by fiction and declamation. Such imperfect particulars as have reached us concerning the increase of the Christian name in Asia and Greece, in Egypt, in Italy, and in the West, we shall now proceed to relate — without neglecting the real or imaginary conquests that lay beyond the frontiers of the Roman empire.

The rich provinces stretching from the Euphrates to the Ionian Sea were the principal theatre on which the apostle of the Gentiles displayed his zeal and piety. The seeds of the gospel he scattered in that fertile soil were diligently cultivated by his disciples, and it appears that during the first two centuries the most considerable body of Christians was contained within those limits. Among the societies established in Syria, none were more ancient or more illustrious than those of Damascus, of Berea or Aleppo, and of Antioch. The prophetic introduction to the Apocalypse has described and immortalized the seven churches of Asia — Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamus, Thyatira, Sardes, Laodicea, and Philadelphia — and their colonies soon spread across that populous country. At a very early period the islands of Cyprus and Crete and the provinces of Thrace and Macedonia gave a favorable reception to the new religion, and Christian republics were soon founded in the cities of Corinth, of Sparta, and of Athens. The antiquity of the Greek and Asiatic churches allowed ample time for their growth and multiplication; and even the swarms of Gnostics and other heretics serve to display the flourishing condition of the orthodox church, since the name of heretic has always been applied to the less numerous party. To these domestic testimonies we may add the confessions, the complaints, and the apprehensions of the Gentiles themselves. From the writings of Lucian — a philosopher who had studied mankind and who describes their manners in the most vivid colors — we learn that under the reign of Commodus his native country of Pontus was filled with Epicureans and *Christians*. Within eighty years of the death of Christ, the humane Pliny was lamenting the magnitude of an evil he vainly attempted to eradicate. In his very detailed letter to the emperor Trajan, he affirms that the temples were almost deserted, that the sacrificial victims scarcely found any purchasers, and that the superstition had not only infected the cities but had spread even into the villages and open countryside of Pontus and Bithynia.
