# Chapter XVI, Part 1 — The Puzzle of Persecution: Why a Tolerant Empire Turned on the Christians

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XVI (The Conduct of the Roman Government Towards the Christians, from the Reign of Nero to That of Constantine), Part 1 of 5
>
> [← Chapter XV, Part 5](ch15-part-5.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Chapter XVI, Part 2 →](ch16-part-2.md)

## Overview

This opening section of Chapter XVI sets up the puzzle the whole chapter is built to solve: how did an empire that tolerated "a thousand forms of religion" come to punish the most inoffensive of sects? Gibbon frames the question with studied innocence — if the Christians were as pure as their own accounts insist, and the Romans as tolerant as chapter II established, then the persecutions should never have happened — and announces his program: to extract "a few authentic facts" about the persecutions from "an undigested mass of fiction and error." The dig at church historians who exaggerated pagan cruelty while imitating it once in power signals that this chapter will be as skeptical of the martyrologies as Chapter XV was of the miracle stories.

Gibbon's first move is a comparison with the Jews. Despite bloody revolts from Nero to Hadrian (including Barchochebas's two-year war), the Jews were restored to their privileges and left to practice their "unsocial religion" in peace. Why, then, were the Christians treated worse? His answer turns on a distinction that mattered enormously to the ancient mind: the Jews were a *nation* following ancestral custom, which everyone conceded they had a right — indeed a duty — to do; the Christians were a *sect* of deserters who had renounced the religions of their families, cities, and provinces without the excuse of ancestry. To pagan eyes this was apostasy against the human race itself.

From this root Gibbon traces the specific accusations: the Christians looked like atheists, since their invisible, imageless God was undetectable by pagan standards of worship; they worshipped a recently executed provincial rather than the hero-gods who had civilized the world; they formed secret, sometimes nocturnal assemblies of exactly the kind Roman policy always distrusted; and their deliberate secrecy invited the lurid slanders — ritual infanticide, cannibal feasts, incestuous orgies — that Gibbon quotes at length, noting acidly that Christian apologists undercut their own defense by hurling identical charges at Gnostic heretics.

The section closes with Gibbon's characteristic reversal: the persecuting emperors, he argues, were *less* guilty than modern Christian persecutors like Charles V or Louis XIV, because they acted as legislators rather than bigots. From this he derives the four propositions that structure the rest of the chapter — the government was slow to notice the Christians, reluctant to convict them, moderate in punishing them, and the church enjoyed long intervals of peace. He then begins proving proposition I, showing how the church's early disguise as a Jewish sect protected it, doubting the martyrdom traditions of the apostles, and arriving at the first genuine persecution: Nero's, introduced by the great fire of Rome in AD 64.

## Key Concepts

- **The paradox of pagan intolerance** — Christian purity plus Roman toleration should have produced protection, not persecution; the chapter exists to explain why it didn't.
- **Nation versus sect** — the Jews inherited their exclusive religion and so were tolerated; the Christians *chose* theirs by deserting their ancestral gods, an "unnatural and unpardonable offence" by ancient standards.
- **The charge of atheism** — a God without image, altar, or sacrifice was invisible to pagan religious categories, so the most pious of men were condemned as impious.
- **Fear of secret association** — Rome's standing distrust of private clubs made the Christians' concealed, nocturnal meetings look like conspiracy, and their "inflexible obstinacy" (Pliny) like defiance of the magistrate.
- **The atrocity slanders** — secrecy modeled on the Eleusinian mysteries backfired, breeding tales of infanticide, cannibalism, and incest that apologists then redirected against Gnostic rivals.
- **Legislators, not bigots** — Gibbon's mitigating claim that the emperors persecuted from temperate policy rather than zeal, making them less criminal than modern Christian persecutors.
- **The four propositions** — government attention came late; convictions were cautious and reluctant; punishments were moderate; the church enjoyed long stretches of peace.
- **The Jewish disguise** — the earliest church sheltered under the legal toleration of Judaism, protected by Roman "ignorance and contempt," until Nero's persecution of AD 64.

## Modern Translation

If we take seriously the purity of the Christian religion, the sanctity of its moral teachings, and the innocent as well as austere lives of most of those who embraced the faith of the Gospel in its first centuries, we would naturally expect that so benevolent a doctrine would have been received with due reverence even by the unbelieving world; that the learned and the cultivated, however much they mocked the miracles, would have esteemed the virtues of the new sect; and that the magistrates, instead of persecuting, would have protected an order of men who gave the most passive obedience to the laws, even while declining the active duties of war and government. If, on the other hand, we recall the universal toleration of polytheism — invariably upheld by the faith of the people, the skepticism of the philosophers, and the policy of the Roman senate and emperors — we are at a loss to discover what new offense the Christians had committed, what new provocation could exasperate the mild indifference of antiquity, and what new motives could drive the Roman princes — who watched without concern as a thousand forms of religion subsisted in peace under their gentle rule — to inflict severe punishment on any portion of their subjects who had chosen for themselves an unusual but inoffensive mode of faith and worship.

The religious policy of the ancient world seems to have taken on a sterner and more intolerant character precisely in order to oppose the progress of Christianity. About eighty years after the death of Christ, his innocent disciples were being punished with death by the sentence of a proconsul of the most amiable and philosophical character, under the laws of an emperor distinguished by the wisdom and justice of his general administration. The apologies repeatedly addressed to Trajan's successors are filled with the most moving complaints that the Christians — who obeyed the dictates of conscience and asked only for its liberty — were, alone among all the subjects of the Roman empire, excluded from the common benefits of that fortunate government. The deaths of a few eminent martyrs have been recorded with care; and from the moment Christianity was invested with supreme power, the governors of the church have been no less diligently employed in publicizing the cruelty of their pagan adversaries than in imitating their conduct. The design of the present chapter is to separate (if that is possible) a few authentic and interesting facts from an undigested mass of fiction and error, and to relate, in a clear and rational manner, the causes, the extent, the duration, and the most important circumstances of the persecutions to which the first Christians were exposed.

The adherents of a persecuted religion — weighed down by fear, animated by resentment, and perhaps heated by fanatical zeal — are seldom in the right frame of mind to investigate calmly, or assess candidly, the motives of their enemies; motives which often escape even the impartial and discerning eyes of observers standing at a safe distance from the flames of persecution. One explanation for the emperors' conduct toward the early Christians has been proposed which may seem all the more plausible because it is drawn from the acknowledged character of polytheism. It has already been observed that the religious harmony of the world rested chiefly on the implicit assent and reverence that the nations of antiquity showed for their respective traditions and ceremonies. One might therefore expect them to unite in indignation against any sect or people that separated itself from the communion of mankind and — claiming exclusive possession of divine knowledge — disdained every form of worship except its own as impious and idolatrous. The rights of toleration were held by mutual indulgence, and they were justly forfeited by refusing the customary tribute. Since the Jews, and the Jews alone, inflexibly refused to pay that tribute, an examination of how the Roman magistrates treated them will show how far these speculations are borne out by the facts, and will lead us to discover the true causes of the persecution of Christianity.

Without repeating what has already been said about the reverence of Roman princes and governors for the temple of Jerusalem, we will observe only that the destruction of the temple and the city was accompanied and followed by every circumstance that could inflame the minds of the conquerors and lend religious persecution the most plausible cover of political justice and public safety. From the reign of Nero to that of Antoninus Pius, the Jews showed a fierce impatience with Roman rule which repeatedly erupted in the most furious massacres and insurrections. Humanity is shocked at the recital of the horrific cruelties they committed in the cities of Egypt, Cyprus, and Cyrene, where they had lived in treacherous friendship with the unsuspecting natives; and we are tempted to applaud the severe retaliation carried out by the arms of the legions against a race of fanatics whose dire and credulous superstition seemed to make them the implacable enemies not only of the Roman government but of the human race. The zealotry of the Jews was sustained by the conviction that it was unlawful for them to pay taxes to an idolatrous master, and by the flattering promise, drawn from their ancient oracles, that a conquering Messiah would soon arise, destined to break their chains and to invest the favorites of heaven with the empire of the earth. It was by announcing himself as this long-expected deliverer, and by calling on all the descendants of Abraham to assert the hope of Israel, that the famous Barchochebas gathered a formidable army, with which he resisted the power of the emperor Hadrian for two years.

Despite these repeated provocations, the resentment of the Roman princes died with the victory, and their fears did not outlast the period of war and danger. Through the general indulgence of polytheism, and the mild temper of Antoninus Pius, the Jews were restored to their ancient privileges and once more obtained permission to circumcise their children — with the easy restriction that they must never confer that distinguishing mark of the Hebrew race on any foreign convert. The numerous remnants of that people, though still barred from the precincts of Jerusalem, were permitted to form and maintain considerable communities both in Italy and in the provinces, to acquire Roman citizenship, to enjoy municipal honors, and at the same time to obtain exemption from the burdensome and expensive public offices of society. The moderation, or the contempt, of the Romans gave legal sanction to the form of ecclesiastical governance instituted by the vanquished sect. The patriarch, who had fixed his residence at Tiberias, was empowered to appoint his subordinate ministers and apostles, to exercise a domestic jurisdiction, and to receive an annual contribution from his dispersed brethren. New synagogues went up regularly in the principal cities of the empire; and the sabbaths, fasts, and festivals — whether commanded by the Mosaic law or enjoined by the traditions of the rabbis — were celebrated in the most solemn and public manner. Such gentle treatment gradually softened the stern temper of the Jews. Awakened from their dream of prophecy and conquest, they took on the behavior of peaceable and industrious subjects. Their irreconcilable hatred of mankind, instead of flaming out in acts of blood and violence, evaporated in less dangerous gratifications: they seized every opportunity to outmaneuver the idolaters in trade, and they pronounced secret and ambiguous curses against the haughty kingdom of Edom.

Since the Jews, who rejected with abhorrence the deities adored by their sovereign and their fellow subjects, nevertheless enjoyed the free exercise of their unsociable religion, there must have been some other cause that exposed the disciples of Christ to severities from which the descendants of Abraham were exempt. The difference between them is simple and obvious, but by the standards of antiquity it was of the highest importance. The Jews were a *nation*; the Christians were a *sect*; and if it was natural for every community to respect the sacred institutions of its neighbors, it was obligatory for each to persevere in those of its ancestors. The voice of the oracles, the precepts of the philosophers, and the authority of the laws unanimously enforced this national obligation. By their lofty claim to superior sanctity the Jews might provoke the polytheists to regard them as an odious and impure race; by disdaining contact with other nations they might earn their contempt. The laws of Moses might be for the most part frivolous or absurd; yet, since a large society had accepted them for many centuries, his followers were justified by the example of mankind, and it was universally acknowledged that they had a right to practice what it would have been criminal for them to neglect. But this principle, which protected the Jewish synagogue, offered no favor or security to the primitive church. By embracing the faith of the Gospel, the Christians incurred the supposed guilt of an unnatural and unpardonable offense. They dissolved the sacred ties of custom and upbringing, violated the religious institutions of their country, and presumptuously despised whatever their fathers had believed true or revered as sacred. Nor was this apostasy (if we may use the expression) of a merely partial or local kind: the pious deserter who withdrew from the temples of Egypt or Syria would just as scornfully refuse asylum in those of Athens or Carthage. Every Christian rejected with contempt the superstitions of his family, his city, and his province. The whole body of Christians unanimously refused any communion with the gods of Rome, of the empire, and of mankind. In vain did the oppressed believer assert the inalienable rights of conscience and private judgment. Though his situation might stir pity, his arguments could never reach the understanding of either the philosophical or the believing part of the pagan world. To their minds, that individuals should have scruples about complying with the established worship was no less astonishing than if they had conceived a sudden loathing for the manners, the dress, or the language of their native country.

The surprise of the pagans soon gave way to resentment, and the most pious of men found themselves exposed to the unjust but dangerous charge of impiety. Malice and prejudice combined to portray the Christians as a society of atheists who, by the most audacious attack on the religious constitution of the empire, had earned the severest punishment of the civil magistrate. They had separated themselves — they gloried in the confession — from every mode of superstition accepted anywhere on the globe by the varied temper of polytheism; but it was not so clear what deity, or what form of worship, they had substituted for the gods and temples of antiquity. The pure and sublime idea they held of the Supreme Being escaped the coarse understanding of the pagan multitude, who could make nothing of a spiritual and solitary God who was neither represented by any bodily figure or visible symbol, nor worshipped with the customary pomp of libations and festivals, altars and sacrifices. The sages of Greece and Rome who had raised their minds to contemplate the existence and attributes of the First Cause were induced, whether by reason or by vanity, to reserve the privilege of this philosophical devotion for themselves and their chosen disciples. They were far from accepting the prejudices of mankind as the standard of truth, but they regarded those prejudices as flowing from the original constitution of human nature; and they supposed that any popular form of faith and worship that presumed to dispense with the aid of the senses would, in exact proportion as it retreated from superstition, prove incapable of restraining the wanderings of the imagination and the visions of fanaticism. The careless glance that men of wit and learning condescended to cast on the Christian revelation served only to confirm their hasty opinion, and to persuade them that the principle of Divine Unity — which they might otherwise have revered — was defaced by the wild enthusiasm, and annihilated by the airy speculations, of the new sectaries. The author of a celebrated dialogue attributed to Lucian, while he affects to treat the mysterious subject of the Trinity in a style of ridicule and contempt, betrays his own ignorance of the weakness of human reason and of the inscrutable nature of the divine perfections.

It might seem less surprising that the founder of Christianity was not only revered by his disciples as a sage and a prophet, but adored as a God. The polytheists were disposed to adopt any article of faith that offered even a distant or imperfect resemblance to the popular mythology, and the legends of Bacchus, Hercules, and Aesculapius had to some degree prepared their imagination for the appearance of the Son of God in human form. But they were astonished that the Christians should abandon the temples of those ancient heroes who, in the infancy of the world, had invented the arts, established laws, and vanquished the tyrants or monsters that infested the earth — in order to choose, as the exclusive object of their religious worship, an obscure teacher who, in a recent age and among a barbarous people, had fallen victim either to the malice of his own countrymen or to the jealousy of the Roman government. The pagan multitude, reserving their gratitude for temporal benefits alone, rejected the inestimable gift of life and immortality offered to mankind by Jesus of Nazareth. His gentle constancy amid cruel and voluntary sufferings, his universal benevolence, and the sublime simplicity of his actions and character were not enough, in the opinion of those worldly men, to compensate for his want of fame, of empire, and of success; and while they refused to acknowledge his stupendous triumph over the powers of darkness and of the grave, they misrepresented or insulted the questionable birth, the wandering life, and the ignominious death of the divine Author of Christianity.

The personal guilt that every Christian contracted by preferring his private opinion to the national religion was aggravated, to a very high degree, by the number and the unity of the offenders. It is well known — and has already been observed — that Roman policy viewed any association among its subjects with the utmost jealousy and distrust, and that the privileges of private corporations, even those formed for the most harmless or beneficial purposes, were granted with a very sparing hand. The religious assemblies of the Christians, who had separated themselves from the public worship, appeared far less innocent than that: they were illegal in principle and might become dangerous in their consequences; nor were the emperors conscious of violating justice when, for the peace of society, they prohibited those secret and sometimes nocturnal meetings. The pious disobedience of the Christians made their conduct — or perhaps their designs — appear in a far more serious and criminal light; and the Roman princes, who might have let themselves be disarmed by prompt submission, considered their honor bound up in the execution of their commands, and sometimes attempted by rigorous punishments to subdue this independent spirit that boldly acknowledged an authority superior to the magistrate's. The extent and duration of this spiritual conspiracy seemed to make it daily more deserving of official punishment. We have already seen how the active and successful zeal of the Christians had gradually spread them through every province and almost every city of the empire. The new converts seemed to renounce family and country in order to bind themselves, in an indissoluble union, to a peculiar society that everywhere assumed a character different from the rest of mankind. Their gloomy and austere appearance, their abhorrence of the common business and pleasures of life, and their frequent predictions of impending calamities filled the pagans with apprehension of some danger from the new sect — all the more alarming for being obscure. "Whatever the principle of their conduct may be," says Pliny, "their inflexible obstinacy appeared deserving of punishment."

The precautions with which the disciples of Christ performed their religious offices were at first dictated by fear and necessity, but they were continued by choice. By imitating the awe-inspiring secrecy that reigned in the Eleusinian mysteries, the Christians had flattered themselves that they would make their sacred institutions more respectable in the eyes of the pagan world. But the outcome, as often happens with subtle policy, defeated their wishes and expectations. The conclusion drawn was that they concealed only what they would have blushed to disclose. Their misjudged prudence gave malice the opportunity to invent, and suspicious credulity the opportunity to believe, the horrid tales that described the Christians as the most wicked of the human race — people who practiced, in their dark hideaways, every abomination a depraved imagination could suggest, and who courted the favor of their unknown God by sacrificing every moral virtue. There were many who pretended to confess or to describe the ceremonies of this abhorred society. It was asserted,

> "that a newborn infant, entirely covered over with flour, was presented, like some mystic symbol of initiation, to the knife of the new convert, who unknowingly inflicted many a secret and mortal wound on the innocent victim of his error; that as soon as the cruel deed was done, the sectaries drank up the blood, greedily tore apart the quivering limbs, and pledged themselves to eternal secrecy by a shared consciousness of guilt. It was as confidently affirmed that this inhuman sacrifice was followed by a suitable banquet, at which drunkenness served as a spur to brutal lust; until, at the appointed moment, the lights were suddenly extinguished, shame was banished, nature was forgotten, and, as chance might direct, the darkness of the night was polluted by the incestuous couplings of sisters and brothers, of sons and of mothers."

But a reading of the ancient apologies was enough to remove even the slightest suspicion from the mind of any fair-minded adversary. The Christians, with the intrepid confidence of innocence, appeal from the voice of rumor to the equity of the magistrates. They acknowledge that if any proof can be produced of the crimes that slander has imputed to them, they deserve the severest punishment. They invite the punishment, and they demand the proof. At the same time they urge, with equal truth and propriety, that the charge is as improbable as it is unsupported by evidence. They ask whether anyone can seriously believe that the pure and holy precepts of the Gospel — which so often restrain even the most lawful enjoyments — could inculcate the practice of the most abominable crimes; that a large society would resolve to dishonor itself in the eyes of its own members; and that a great number of persons of both sexes, of every age and character, indifferent to the fear of death or infamy, would consent to violate the principles that nature and upbringing had imprinted most deeply in their minds. Nothing, one would think, could weaken the force or destroy the effect of so unanswerable a defense — except the ill-judged conduct of the apologists themselves, who betrayed the common cause of religion in order to gratify their devout hatred of the church's domestic enemies. It was sometimes faintly insinuated, and sometimes boldly asserted, that the same bloody sacrifices and the same incestuous festivals so falsely ascribed to orthodox believers were in reality celebrated by the Marcionites, the Carpocratians, and several other Gnostic sects — who, however far they might have strayed into the paths of heresy, were still moved by the feelings of human beings and still governed by the precepts of Christianity. Accusations of the same kind were flung back at the church by the schismatics who had departed from its communion; and it was admitted on all sides that the most scandalous licentiousness of conduct prevailed among great numbers of those who claimed the name of Christian. A pagan magistrate, possessing neither the leisure nor the ability to discern the almost imperceptible line dividing the orthodox faith from heretical depravity, might easily have concluded that their mutual hostility had wrung from them the confession of a shared guilt. It was fortunate for the peace — or at least for the reputation — of the first Christians that the magistrates sometimes proceeded with more temper and moderation than religious zeal usually allows, and that they reported, as the impartial result of their judicial inquiry, that the sectaries who had deserted the established worship appeared to them sincere in their professions and blameless in their conduct — however much they might, by their absurd and excessive superstition, incur the censure of the laws.

History, which undertakes to record the transactions of the past for the instruction of future ages, would ill deserve that honorable office if she stooped to plead the cause of tyrants or to justify the maxims of persecution. It must nevertheless be acknowledged that the conduct of the emperors who appeared least favorable to the primitive church is by no means as criminal as that of modern sovereigns who have employed the arm of violence and terror against the religious opinions of any part of their subjects. From their own reflections, or even from their own feelings, a Charles V or a Louis XIV might have acquired a just understanding of the rights of conscience, the obligation of faith, and the innocence of error. But the princes and magistrates of ancient Rome were strangers to the principles that inspired and authorized the inflexible obstinacy of the Christians in the cause of truth; nor could they discover in their own hearts any motive that would have prompted them to refuse a legal — and, as it were, natural — submission to the sacred institutions of their country. The same consideration that lightens their guilt must also have moderated the rigor of their persecutions. Since they acted not from the furious zeal of bigots but from the temperate policy of legislators, contempt must often have relaxed, and humanity must frequently have suspended, the execution of the laws they enacted against the humble and obscure followers of Christ. From this general view of their character and motives we might naturally conclude: I. That a considerable time passed before they regarded the new sectaries as an object deserving the attention of government. II. That in convicting any of their subjects accused of so very peculiar a crime, they proceeded with caution and reluctance. III. That they were moderate in the use of punishments. And IV. That the afflicted church enjoyed many intervals of peace and tranquility. Notwithstanding the careless indifference that even the fullest and most detailed of the pagan writers have shown toward the affairs of the Christians, it may still be within our power to confirm each of these probable suppositions by the evidence of authentic facts.

I. By the wise dispensation of Providence, a mysterious veil was cast over the infancy of the church which, until the faith of the Christians had matured and their numbers had multiplied, served to protect them not only from the malice but even from the knowledge of the pagan world. The slow and gradual abandonment of the Mosaic ceremonies provided the earliest converts to the Gospel with a safe and innocent disguise. Since the great majority of them were of the race of Abraham, they bore the distinctive mark of circumcision, offered their devotions in the Temple of Jerusalem until its final destruction, and received both the Law and the Prophets as the genuine inspirations of the Deity. The Gentile converts, who by spiritual adoption had been joined to the hope of Israel, were likewise indistinguishable under the garb and appearance of Jews; and since the polytheists paid less attention to articles of faith than to external worship, the new sect — which carefully concealed, or only faintly announced, its future greatness and ambition — was allowed to shelter under the general toleration granted to an ancient and celebrated people of the Roman empire. It was probably not long before the Jews themselves, animated by a fiercer zeal and a more jealous faith, perceived the gradual separation of their Nazarene brethren from the doctrine of the synagogue; and they would gladly have extinguished the dangerous heresy in the blood of its adherents. But the decrees of Heaven had already disarmed their malice. Though they might sometimes exercise the lawless privilege of riot, they no longer controlled the administration of criminal justice; nor did they find it easy to infuse the rancor of their own zeal and prejudice into the calm breast of a Roman magistrate. The provincial governors declared themselves ready to hear any accusation that might affect public safety; but as soon as they learned that the question was one not of facts but of words — a dispute concerning only the interpretation of the Jewish laws and prophecies — they judged it beneath the majesty of Rome to seriously examine the obscure quarrels that might arise among a barbarous and superstitious people. The innocence of the first Christians was protected by ignorance and contempt, and the tribunal of the pagan magistrate often proved their surest refuge against the fury of the synagogue. If we were inclined to accept the traditions of a too credulous antiquity, we might recount the distant journeys, the wonderful achievements, and the various deaths of the twelve apostles; but a more careful inquiry will lead us to doubt whether any of those who had witnessed the miracles of Christ were permitted, beyond the borders of Palestine, to seal the truth of their testimony with their blood. From the ordinary span of human life it may very naturally be presumed that most of them had died before the discontent of the Jews erupted into that furious war which ended only with the ruin of Jerusalem. Through that long period, from the death of Christ to that memorable rebellion, we can find no trace of Roman intolerance — unless it lies in the sudden, transient, but cruel persecution that Nero inflicted on the Christians of the capital, thirty-five years after the first of those great events and only two years before the second. The character of the philosophic historian to whom we owe most of our knowledge of this singular episode would by itself be enough to commend it to our most attentive consideration.

In the tenth year of the reign of Nero (AD 64), the capital of the empire was afflicted by a fire that raged beyond the memory or example of any former age. The monuments of Greek art and of Roman virtue, the trophies of the Punic and Gallic wars, the most holy temples and the most splendid palaces were swept up in one common destruction. Of the fourteen regions or quarters into which Rome was divided, only four survived intact; three were leveled to the ground; and the remaining seven, having felt the fury of the flames, presented a melancholy prospect of ruin and desolation. The vigilance of the government appears to have neglected none of the precautions that might soften the impact of so dreadful a calamity. The imperial gardens were thrown open to the distressed multitude, temporary buildings were erected for their accommodation, and a plentiful supply of grain and provisions was distributed at a very moderate price. The most generous policy seemed to have dictated the edicts regulating the layout of the streets and the construction of private houses; and, as usually happens in an age of prosperity, the conflagration of Rome produced within a few years a new city, more regular and more beautiful than the old. But all the prudence and humanity that Nero displayed on this occasion could not shield him from popular suspicion. Every crime could be imputed to the assassin of his own wife and mother; nor could a prince who prostituted his person and dignity on the stage be thought incapable of the most extravagant folly. The voice of rumor accused the emperor of being the arsonist of his own capital; and since the most incredible stories are the best suited to the temper of an enraged people, it was gravely reported, and firmly believed, that Nero, enjoying the calamity he had caused, amused himself by singing the destruction of ancient Troy to his lyre. To deflect a suspicion that all the power of despotism could not suppress, the emperor resolved to substitute fictitious criminals in his own place.
