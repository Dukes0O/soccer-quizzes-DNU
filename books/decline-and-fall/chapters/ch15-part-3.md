# Chapter XV, Part 3 — The Virtues of the Primitive Christians and the Rise of Church Government

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XV (The Progress of the Christian Religion, and the Sentiments, Manners, Numbers, and Condition of the Primitive Christians), Part 3 of 5
>

## Overview

This part carries forward Gibbon's famous inquiry into the five "secondary causes" of Christianity's rapid growth. It opens by finishing his treatment of the third cause — the church's claim to miraculous powers — with a characteristically double-edged observation: early Christians lived on "mystic ground," so saturated in wonders that believing the gospel miracles cost them nothing, whereas modern piety harbors an involuntary skepticism. He then develops the fourth cause at length: the pure and austere morals of the early believers, which he attributes not only to revelation but to two very human motives — the convert's remorse for past sins and the small sect's anxious concern for its collective reputation.

Gibbon's admiration for early Christian virtue is real but relentlessly qualified. He argues that even their faults were "an excess of virtue": the fathers, reading Christ's precepts too literally, pushed self-denial to heights that no one could sustain, condemning music, fine clothes, warm baths, white bread, and shaving. His long discussion of the fathers' hostility to sexuality — marriage grudgingly tolerated, second marriages branded "legal adultery," celibacy exalted, and the scandalous experiment of virgins sharing beds with clergy to prove their purity — traces "the early traces of monastic principles." He likewise notes the Christians' refusal to serve as soldiers or magistrates, and the awkward secret behind their apologists' evasions: they expected the world itself to end soon.

The final section begins the fifth cause: the union and discipline of the "Christian republic." Human nature reasserted itself, and the believers' suppressed love of action found an outlet in church government. Gibbon narrates the evolution from the loose, egalitarian congregations of the first century — guided by prophets, then by interchangeable bishops-and-presbyters — to a permanent episcopal presidency, then to provincial synods that fused the churches into "a great federative republic," and finally to the third-century prelates who converted exhortation into command and claimed a divine authority above that of princes. The trajectory from humble servant to lordly shepherd, exemplified by the politically gifted Cyprian of Carthage, is the part's closing irony.

## Key Concepts

- **Credulity as an asset** — Constant exposure to visions, demons, and prophecies disposed early Christians to accept the gospel miracles effortlessly; this "deep impression of supernatural truths" is what was celebrated as faith, ranked by rigid theologians above moral virtue itself.
- **Two human motives for Christian austerity** — Repentance for past sins (the most eminent saints had often been the most abandoned sinners) and the small sect's need to protect its collective reputation under hostile scrutiny.
- **Excessive virtue as the fathers' characteristic fault** — Literal readings of Scripture drove self-mortification, censures of luxury, and demands for purity beyond what human nature can sustain.
- **Hostility to pleasure and to marriage** — Sensual enjoyment marked as abuse, marriage tolerated only as a defect, second marriages condemned, celibacy exalted — the seedbed of monasticism.
- **Withdrawal from civic life** — Christians refused oaths, office, and military service, quietly expecting the imminent end of the world; Gibbon notes their scruples conveniently matched their situation.
- **Evolution of church government** — From independent, egalitarian congregations with prophets, to bishops as lifelong presidents, to provincial synods and a federated "Catholic church."
- **Episcopal usurpation** — Third-century bishops claimed divine authority, monopolized ordination, demanded a shepherd's obedience from their "flock," and stigmatized democratic resistance as faction and schism.

## Modern Translation

Whatever one thinks of the miracles claimed by the early church after the apostles' time, this unresisting softness of mind, so conspicuous among believers of the second and third centuries, turned out to be an accidental benefit to the cause of truth and religion. In modern times, a latent and even involuntary skepticism clings to the most pious temperaments. Their acceptance of supernatural truths is much less an active consent than a cold, passive acquiescence. Long accustomed to observing and respecting the regular order of Nature, our reason — or at least our imagination — is not adequately prepared to withstand the visible action of the Deity. But in the first ages of Christianity, humanity's situation was entirely different. The most curious, or the most credulous, among the Pagans were often persuaded to join a society that asserted an active, present claim to miraculous powers. The primitive Christians walked perpetually on mystic ground, and their minds were trained by the habit of believing the most extraordinary events. They felt — or they fancied — that on every side they were incessantly assaulted by demons, comforted by visions, instructed by prophecy, and astonishingly delivered from danger, sickness, and death itself by the prayers of the church. The real or imaginary prodigies of which they so frequently believed themselves the objects, the instruments, or the spectators disposed them, very conveniently, to accept with the same ease — but with far greater justice — the authentic wonders of the gospel narrative. And so miracles that did not exceed the measure of their own experience inspired them with the liveliest assurance of mysteries acknowledged to surpass the limits of their understanding. It is this deep impression of supernatural truths that has been so widely celebrated under the name of faith: a state of mind described as the surest pledge of divine favor and of future happiness, and recommended as the first, or perhaps the only, merit of a Christian. According to the stricter theologians, the moral virtues — which infidels can practice just as well — have no value or efficacy whatever in the work of our justification.

IV. But the primitive Christian demonstrated his faith by his virtues, and it was quite reasonably supposed that the divine persuasion which enlightened or subdued the understanding must at the same time purify the heart and direct the believer's actions. The first apologists of Christianity, defending the innocence of their brethren, and the writers of a later period, celebrating the sanctity of their ancestors, paint in the most vivid colors the reformation of morals that the preaching of the gospel introduced into the world. Since my intention is to note only such human causes as were permitted to reinforce the influence of revelation, I will briefly mention two motives that might naturally have made the lives of the primitive Christians much purer and more austere than those of their Pagan contemporaries or their degenerate successors: repentance for their past sins, and the commendable desire to uphold the reputation of the society they had joined.

It is a very old reproach, suggested by the ignorance or malice of unbelievers, that the Christians recruited into their party the most atrocious criminals, who, as soon as they felt a twinge of remorse, were easily persuaded to wash away in the water of baptism the guilt of past conduct for which the temples of the gods refused them any expiation. But this reproach, once cleared of misrepresentation, contributes as much to the honor of the church as it did to its growth. The friends of Christianity may admit without a blush that many of the most eminent saints had been, before their baptism, the most abandoned sinners. People who in ordinary life had followed, however imperfectly, the dictates of benevolence and propriety derived such calm satisfaction from the sense of their own rectitude that they were far less susceptible to the sudden surges of shame, grief, and terror that have produced so many remarkable conversions. Following the example of their Divine Master, the missionaries of the gospel did not disdain the company of men — and especially of women — oppressed by the consciousness, and very often by the consequences, of their vices. As these converts emerged from sin and superstition into the glorious hope of immortality, they resolved to devote themselves to a life not merely of virtue but of penitence. The desire for perfection became the ruling passion of their souls; and it is well known that while reason settles for a cold mediocrity, our passions hurl us with rapid violence across the whole distance between the most opposite extremes.

Once the new converts had been enrolled among the faithful and admitted to the sacraments of the church, they were restrained from relapsing into their old disorders by another consideration — less spiritual, but entirely innocent and respectable. Any small society that has broken away from the great body of the nation or religion to which it belonged immediately becomes the object of universal and resentful scrutiny. The smaller its numbers, the more its collective character is shaped by the virtues and vices of its individual members; and every member is bound to watch with the most vigilant attention over his own behavior and that of his brethren, since he must expect to bear a share of the common disgrace and may hope to enjoy a share of the common reputation. When the Christians of Bithynia were brought before the tribunal of the younger Pliny, they assured the proconsul that, far from being engaged in any unlawful conspiracy, they were bound by a solemn obligation to abstain from the crimes that disturb the private or public peace of society: theft, robbery, adultery, perjury, and fraud. Nearly a century later, Tertullian could boast with honest pride that very few Christians had died at the executioner's hand except on account of their religion. Their serious, secluded way of life, averse to the gay luxury of the age, trained them in chastity, temperance, frugality, and all the sober domestic virtues. Since most of them practiced some trade or profession, it fell to them, by the strictest integrity and fairest dealing, to dispel the suspicions that the worldly are all too ready to harbor against the appearance of sanctity. The world's contempt schooled them in the habits of humility, meekness, and patience. The more they were persecuted, the more closely they clung to one another. Their mutual charity and unsuspecting trust were noted by unbelievers — and were too often abused by treacherous friends.

It is a very honorable circumstance for the morals of the primitive Christians that even their faults — or rather their errors — derived from an excess of virtue. The bishops and teachers of the church, whose testimony attests, and whose authority might shape, the professions, principles, and even the practice of their contemporaries, had studied the Scriptures with less skill than devotion, and they often took in the most literal sense those rigid precepts of Christ and the apostles to which the prudence of later commentators has applied a looser and more figurative interpretation. Eager to exalt the perfection of the gospel above the wisdom of philosophy, the zealous fathers carried the duties of self-mortification, purity, and patience to a height scarcely possible to attain, much less to maintain, in our present state of weakness and corruption. So extraordinary and sublime a doctrine could not fail to command the veneration of the people; but it was poorly calculated to win the approval of those worldly philosophers who, in the conduct of this transitory life, consult only the feelings of nature and the interests of society.

There are two very natural propensities that we can distinguish in even the most virtuous and generous temperaments: the love of pleasure and the love of action. When the former is refined by art and learning, improved by the charms of social intercourse, and corrected by a due regard for economy, health, and reputation, it produces the greater part of the happiness of private life. The love of action is a principle of a much stronger and more dubious character. It often leads to anger, ambition, and revenge; but when it is guided by a sense of propriety and benevolence, it becomes the parent of every virtue — and if those virtues are matched by equal abilities, a family, a state, or an empire may owe its safety and prosperity to the undaunted courage of a single man. To the love of pleasure, then, we may ascribe most of the agreeable qualities; to the love of action, most of the useful and respectable ones. The character in which both are united and harmonized would seem to constitute the most perfect idea of human nature. The numb and inactive disposition, supposedly destitute of both, would be rejected by the common consent of mankind as utterly incapable of producing any happiness for the individual or any public benefit for the world. But it was not in *this* world that the primitive Christians wished to make themselves either agreeable or useful.

The acquisition of knowledge, the exercise of reason or imagination, and the cheerful flow of unguarded conversation may occupy the leisure of a cultivated mind. Such amusements, however, were rejected with abhorrence, or admitted only with the utmost caution, by the severity of the fathers, who despised all knowledge that was not useful for salvation and who regarded all levity of speech as a criminal abuse of the gift of language. In our present state of existence, the body is so inseparably joined to the soul that it would seem to be in our interest to taste, with innocence and moderation, the enjoyments of which that faithful companion is capable. Very different was the reasoning of our devout predecessors. Vainly aspiring to imitate the perfection of angels, they disdained — or affected to disdain — every earthly and bodily delight. Some of our senses are indeed necessary for our preservation, others for our subsistence, and others again for our information, and to that extent it was impossible to reject their use. But the first sensation of pleasure was marked down as the first moment of their abuse. The unfeeling candidate for Heaven was instructed not only to resist the grosser allurements of taste and smell, but even to shut his ears against the profane harmony of sounds, and to look with indifference on the most finished productions of human art. Fine clothes, magnificent houses, and elegant furniture were held to combine the double guilt of pride and sensuality; a simple, mortified appearance better suited the Christian who was certain of his sins and doubtful of his salvation. In their censures of luxury the fathers are extremely minute and detailed; among the various articles that arouse their pious indignation we may list false hair, garments of any color except white, musical instruments, vases of gold or silver, downy pillows (Jacob, after all, rested his head on a stone), white bread, foreign wines, greetings exchanged in public, the use of warm baths, and the practice of shaving the beard — which, in Tertullian's phrase, is a lie against our own faces and an impious attempt to improve on the works of the Creator. When Christianity spread among the rich and the polished, the observance of these singular laws was left, as it would be today, to the few with ambitions of superior sanctity. But it is always easy, as well as agreeable, for the lower ranks of mankind to claim merit for despising the pomp and pleasure that fortune has placed beyond their reach. The virtue of the primitive Christians, like that of the first Romans, was very frequently guarded by poverty and ignorance.

The chaste severity of the fathers in everything relating to relations between the sexes flowed from the same principle: their abhorrence of every enjoyment that might gratify the sensual, and degrade the spiritual, nature of man. It was their favorite opinion that if Adam had kept his obedience to the Creator, he would have lived forever in a state of virgin purity, and that some harmless mode of vegetation might have peopled paradise with a race of innocent and immortal beings. The use of marriage was permitted only to his fallen posterity, as a necessary expedient for continuing the human species and as a restraint, however imperfect, on the natural licentiousness of desire. The hesitation of the orthodox casuists on this interesting subject betrays the perplexity of men unwilling to approve an institution they were compelled to tolerate. A list of the very whimsical rules they imposed, in the most circumstantial detail, on the marriage bed would draw a smile from the young and a blush from the fair. It was their unanimous view that a first marriage was adequate to all the purposes of nature and society. The sensual connection was refined into a resemblance of the mystic union of Christ with his church, and was pronounced indissoluble by either divorce or death. The practice of second marriage was branded with the name of legal adultery, and those guilty of so scandalous an offense against Christian purity were soon excluded from the honors, and even from the alms, of the church. Since desire was counted a crime, and marriage tolerated only as a defect, it was consistent with the same principles to regard celibacy as the nearest approach to divine perfection. Ancient Rome could barely sustain the institution of six vestal virgins; but the primitive church was filled with great numbers of both sexes who had devoted themselves to the profession of perpetual chastity. A few of these — among whom we may count the learned Origen — judged it most prudent to disarm the tempter. Some were insensible to the assaults of the flesh, and some were invincible against them. Disdaining an ignominious flight, the virgins of the warm climate of Africa met the enemy in the closest possible engagement: they permitted priests and deacons to share their beds, and gloried, amid the flames, in their unsullied purity. But insulted Nature sometimes vindicated her rights, and this new species of martyrdom served only to introduce a new scandal into the church. Among the Christian ascetics, however (a name they soon acquired from their painful exercises), many, being less presumptuous, were probably more successful. The loss of sensual pleasure was supplied and compensated by spiritual pride. Even the Pagan multitude was inclined to estimate the merit of the sacrifice by its apparent difficulty; and it was in praise of these chaste spouses of Christ that the fathers poured out the troubled stream of their eloquence. Such are the early traces of monastic principles and institutions, which in a later age have counterbalanced all the temporal advantages of Christianity.

The Christians were no less averse to the business than to the pleasures of this world. They did not know how to reconcile the defense of person and property with the patient doctrine that enjoined unlimited forgiveness of past injuries and commanded them to invite the repetition of fresh insults. Their simplicity was offended by the use of oaths, by the pomp of magistracy, and by the active contention of public life; nor could their humane ignorance be convinced that it was ever lawful to shed the blood of our fellow creatures, whether by the sword of justice or the sword of war — even when criminal or hostile designs threatened the peace and safety of the whole community. It was acknowledged that under a less perfect law, the powers of the Jewish constitution had been exercised, with Heaven's approval, by inspired prophets and anointed kings. The Christians felt and confessed that such institutions might be necessary for the present system of the world, and they cheerfully submitted to the authority of their Pagan governors. But while they preached the maxims of passive obedience, they refused to take any active part in the civil administration or the military defense of the empire. Some indulgence might perhaps be extended to those who, before their conversion, were already engaged in such violent and bloody occupations; but it was impossible for Christians, without renouncing a more sacred duty, to assume the character of soldiers, magistrates, or princes. This indolent, or even criminal, disregard for the public welfare exposed them to the contempt and reproaches of the Pagans, who frequently asked: what would become of the empire, attacked on every side by barbarians, if all mankind adopted the cowardly sentiments of the new sect? To this insulting question the Christian apologists returned obscure and ambiguous answers, since they were unwilling to reveal the secret ground of their confidence: the expectation that before the conversion of mankind was complete, war, government, the Roman empire, and the world itself would exist no longer. It may be observed that here too the situation of the first Christians coincided very happily with their religious scruples, and that their aversion to an active life served rather to excuse them from the service of the state and army than to exclude them from its honors.

V. But the human character, however it may be exalted or depressed by a temporary enthusiasm, gradually returns to its proper and natural level and resumes the passions best suited to its present condition. The primitive Christians were dead to the business and pleasures of the world; but their love of action, which could never be entirely extinguished, soon revived and found a new occupation in the government of the church. A separate society that attacked the established religion of the empire was obliged to adopt some form of internal organization and to appoint a sufficient number of ministers entrusted not only with spiritual functions but with the temporal direction of the Christian commonwealth. The safety of that society, its honor, its aggrandizement, produced even in the most pious minds a spirit of patriotism such as the first Romans had felt for the republic — and sometimes a similar indifference about the means that might advance so desirable an end. The ambition of raising themselves or their friends to the honors and offices of the church was disguised by the laudable intention of devoting to the public benefit the power and standing which, for that purpose alone, it became their duty to seek. In the exercise of their functions they were frequently called upon to detect the errors of heresy or the arts of faction, to counter the designs of treacherous brethren, to brand their characters with deserved infamy, and to expel them from the bosom of a society whose peace and happiness they had tried to disturb. The ecclesiastical governors of the Christians were taught to unite the wisdom of the serpent with the innocence of the dove; but as the former was refined, the latter was imperceptibly corrupted, by the habits of government. In the church as in the world, those placed in any public station made themselves considerable by their eloquence and firmness, their knowledge of mankind, and their dexterity in business; and while they concealed from others — and perhaps from themselves — the secret motives of their conduct, they too frequently relapsed into all the turbulent passions of active life, tinctured with an additional degree of bitterness and obstinacy from the infusion of spiritual zeal.

The government of the church has often been the subject, as well as the prize, of religious contention. The hostile disputants of Rome, Paris, Oxford, and Geneva have all struggled to reduce the primitive and apostolic model to the standards of their own respective systems. The few who have pursued this inquiry with more candor and impartiality are of the opinion that the apostles declined the office of legislation, choosing rather to endure some partial scandals and divisions than to deprive the Christians of future ages of the liberty of varying their forms of ecclesiastical government according to changing times and circumstances. The scheme of organization adopted, with their approval, for the use of the first century can be discovered from the practice of Jerusalem, Ephesus, or Corinth. The societies established in the cities of the Roman empire were united only by the ties of faith and charity. Independence and equality formed the basis of their internal constitution. The lack of discipline and formal learning was supplied by the occasional assistance of the *prophets*, who were called to that function without distinction of age, sex, or natural ability, and who, whenever they felt the divine impulse, poured forth the effusions of the Spirit in the assembly of the faithful. But these extraordinary gifts were frequently abused or misapplied by the prophetic teachers. They displayed them at improper moments, presumptuously disturbed the service of the assembly, and by their pride or misguided zeal introduced — particularly into the apostolic church of Corinth — a long and melancholy train of disorders. As the institution of prophets became useless and even pernicious, their powers were withdrawn and their office abolished. The public functions of religion were entrusted solely to the established ministers of the church, the *bishops* and the *presbyters* — two titles which, in their original use, appear to have designated the same office and the same order of persons. The name of Presbyter expressed their age, or rather their gravity and wisdom. The title of Bishop denoted their oversight of the faith and morals of the Christians committed to their pastoral care. According to the numbers of the faithful in each place, a larger or smaller number of these *episcopal presbyters* guided each infant congregation with equal authority and united counsels.

But the most perfect equality of freedom still requires the directing hand of a superior magistrate, and the orderly conduct of public deliberations soon introduces the office of a president, invested at least with the authority to collect the sentiments and execute the resolutions of the assembly. Concern for the public tranquility — which annual or occasional elections would so frequently have disturbed — led the primitive Christians to establish an honorable and permanent magistracy, and to choose one of the wisest and holiest of their presbyters to perform, for life, the duties of their ecclesiastical governor. It was in these circumstances that the lofty title of Bishop began to rise above the humble appellation of presbyter; and while the latter remained the natural designation for the members of every Christian senate, the former was appropriated to the dignity of its new president. The advantages of this episcopal form of government — introduced, it appears, before the end of the first century — were so obvious, and so important for the future greatness as well as the present peace of Christianity, that it was adopted without delay by all the societies already scattered across the empire. It acquired at a very early period the sanction of antiquity, and it is still revered by the most powerful churches of both East and West as a primitive and even a divine establishment. It hardly needs saying that the pious and humble presbyters first dignified with the episcopal title could not have possessed, and would probably have rejected, the power and pomp that now encircle the tiara of the Roman pontiff or the mitre of a German prelate. But we can define in a few words the narrow limits of their original jurisdiction, which was chiefly spiritual, though in some instances temporal, in nature. It consisted in administering the sacraments and discipline of the church; superintending the religious ceremonies, which imperceptibly grew in number and variety; consecrating the ecclesiastical ministers, to whom the bishop assigned their respective functions; managing the public fund; and deciding all such disputes as the faithful were unwilling to lay before the tribunal of an idolatrous judge. These powers, for a short period, were exercised according to the advice of the college of presbyters and with the consent and approval of the assembly of Christians. The primitive bishops were considered merely the first among their equals and the honorable servants of a free people. Whenever the episcopal chair fell vacant by death, a new president was chosen from among the presbyters by the vote of the whole congregation, every member of which considered himself invested with a sacred and priestly character.

Such was the mild and equal constitution by which the Christians were governed for more than a hundred years after the death of the apostles. Every society formed within itself a separate and independent republic; and although the most distant of these little states maintained a mutual and friendly exchange of letters and delegations, the Christian world was not yet connected by any supreme authority or legislative assembly. As the numbers of the faithful gradually multiplied, they discovered the advantages that might flow from a closer union of their interests and designs. Toward the end of the second century, the churches of Greece and Asia adopted the useful institution of provincial synods, and they may fairly be supposed to have borrowed the model of a representative council from the celebrated examples of their own country: the Amphictyons, the Achaean league, or the assemblies of the Ionian cities. It was soon established, as both custom and law, that the bishops of the independent churches should meet in the provincial capital at the fixed seasons of spring and autumn. Their deliberations were assisted by the advice of a few distinguished presbyters and moderated by the presence of a listening multitude. Their decrees, called Canons, regulated every important controversy of faith and discipline; and it was natural to believe that a liberal effusion of the Holy Spirit would be poured out upon the united assembly of the delegates of the Christian people. The institution of synods suited private ambition and public interest so well that within a few years it was adopted throughout the whole empire. A regular correspondence was established between the provincial councils, which mutually communicated and approved one another's proceedings; and the Catholic church soon assumed the form, and acquired the strength, of a great federative republic.

As the legislative authority of the individual churches was imperceptibly superseded by the use of councils, the bishops obtained through their alliance a much larger share of executive and arbitrary power; and as soon as they were connected by a sense of their common interest, they were able to attack, with united vigor, the original rights of their clergy and people. The prelates of the third century imperceptibly changed the language of exhortation into the language of command, scattered the seeds of future usurpations, and made up for their deficiency of force and of reason with scriptural allegories and declamatory rhetoric. They exalted the unity and power of the church as embodied in the episcopal office, of which every bishop enjoyed an equal and undivided portion. Princes and magistrates, it was often repeated, might boast an earthly claim to a transitory dominion; episcopal authority alone derived from the Deity, and extended over this world and the next. The bishops were the vicegerents of Christ, the successors of the apostles, and the mystic substitutes of the high priest of the Mosaic law. Their exclusive privilege of conferring the priestly character encroached on the freedom of both clerical and popular elections; and if, in the administration of the church, they still consulted the judgment of the presbyters or the inclinations of the people, they were most careful to emphasize the merit of such voluntary condescension. The bishops acknowledged the supreme authority residing in the assembly of their brethren; but in the government of his own diocese, each of them exacted from his *flock* the same implicit obedience as if that favorite metaphor had been literally true, and as if the shepherd were of a nature more exalted than that of his sheep. This obedience, however, was not imposed without some effort on one side and some resistance on the other. The democratic part of the constitution was, in many places, very warmly defended by the zealous or self-interested opposition of the inferior clergy. But their patriotism was rewarded with the insulting epithets of faction and schism; and the episcopal cause owed its rapid progress to the labors of many active prelates who, like Cyprian of Carthage, could combine the arts of the most ambitious statesman with the Christian virtues that seem suited to the character of a saint and martyr.
