# Chapter XV, Part 2 — The War on Idolatry, the Promise of Immortality, and the Claim of Miracles

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XV (The Progress of the Christian Religion, and the Sentiments, Manners, Numbers, and Condition of the Primitive Christians), Part 2 of 5
>

## Overview

This part continues Gibbon's famous inquiry into the "secondary causes" of Christianity's rapid growth. It opens by finishing the first cause — the inflexible zeal inherited from Judaism — with a vivid tour of how thoroughly paganism saturated Roman life: business, weddings, funerals, dinner parties, the arts, even everyday speech. Because every corner of society was entangled with the gods, the Christian's duty to shun idolatry forced a constant, conspicuous separation from ordinary life, and Gibbon suggests that this perpetual friction actually hardened the believers' zeal rather than wearing it down.

The second cause then takes center stage: the doctrine of a future life. Gibbon surveys the failure of classical philosophy to establish the soul's immortality with any conviction, the three defects that made pagan religion useless on the question, and — with studied astonishment at the "mysterious dispensations of Providence" — the striking omission of the doctrine from the law of Moses. Only with the Pharisees, and finally with the authority and example of Christ, did the promise of immortality acquire binding force. To this promise the early church added two powerful accelerants: the universal belief that the end of the world was imminent, and the doctrine of the Millennium, a thousand-year earthly kingdom pictured in gratifyingly material terms — a scaffolding, Gibbon dryly notes, that the church discarded once the building was finished. The threatened counterpart, the fiery destruction of Rome as the mystic Babylon and the eternal torture of the entire unbelieving world, supplied terror where hope did not suffice; Tertullian's gloating vision of the damned is quoted at length before Gibbon draws a veil over it.

The third cause — the miraculous powers claimed by the primitive church — closes the part. Gibbon catalogues the gifts of tongues, visions, exorcisms, healings, and even resurrections, then poses the devastating structural question raised by Conyers Middleton's recent inquiry: since the chain of attested miracles runs unbroken from the apostles to the popes, at what link may it honestly be cut? Whatever date is chosen for the cessation of miracles, the Christians of that era never noticed the loss — a fact Gibbon leaves to speak for itself.

## Key Concepts

- **The totality of pagan observance** — polytheism was woven into every transaction, festival, art form, and idiom of Roman life, so that avoiding idolatry meant withdrawing from society itself; each forced act of refusal further fortified Christian zeal.
- **Philosophy's weak case for immortality** — the ablest ancients could offer only desire, hope, or at most probability; the doctrine had no hold on actual conduct and was openly derided by educated Romans.
- **The three defects of pagan religion on the afterlife** — no solid proof, an underworld disfigured by poetic fictions, and a providence concerned with states in this world rather than souls in the next.
- **The Mosaic omission and the Pharisees** — the immortality of the soul is absent from the law of Moses and only dimly hinted by the prophets; it entered Judaism through the Pharisees as a borrowed "tradition" and prevailed only shortly before Christ.
- **Imminent apocalypse and the Millennium** — the primitive church universally expected the world's end within living memory and a thousand-year earthly reign of Christ in a jeweled New Jerusalem; the doctrine, once useful, was later downgraded to allegory and finally condemned as heresy.
- **The general conflagration** — Rome, branded the mystic Babylon, was to perish in fire, a belief conveniently seconded by Stoic philosophy and Italy's volcanoes.
- **Damnation as an instrument of conversion** — the church consigned the greater part of humanity to eternal torture, and the terror of that sentence, Gibbon argues, was often what first subdued the careless polytheist.
- **The problem of the cessation of miracles** — miracle claims continue in an unbroken succession through church history, so any chosen cutoff date is arbitrary; the believers alive at the supposed cutoff never perceived that the gift had been withdrawn.

## Modern Translation

Because of this conviction, a Christian's first — and most difficult — duty was to keep himself pure and untainted by any practice of idolatry. The religion of the nations was not just a speculative doctrine professed in the schools or preached in the temples. The countless deities and rites of polytheism were closely woven into every circumstance of business and pleasure, of public and private life; and it seemed impossible to avoid observing them without at the same time renouncing all human contact and every office and amusement of society. The great transactions of peace and war were opened or concluded with solemn sacrifices, at which the magistrate, the senator, and the soldier were obliged to preside or take part. The public spectacles were an essential part of the Pagans' cheerful devotion, and the gods were believed to accept, as the most welcome offering, the games that prince and people celebrated in honor of their particular festivals. The Christian who shrank with pious horror from the abomination of the circus or the theater found himself surrounded by infernal snares at every dinner party, whenever his friends, invoking the gods of hospitality, poured out libations to one another's happiness. When the bride, struggling with well-rehearsed reluctance, was carried in wedding pomp over the threshold of her new home, or when the sad procession of the dead moved slowly toward the funeral pyre, the Christian on these momentous occasions was compelled to abandon the people dearest to him rather than contract the guilt inherent in those impious ceremonies. Every art and every trade with even the slightest connection to the making or decorating of idols was polluted by the stain of idolatry — a severe sentence, since it consigned to eternal misery the far greater part of the community, employed as it is in the liberal and mechanical professions. If we survey the abundant remains of antiquity, we find that, beyond the direct representations of the gods and the sacred instruments of their worship, the elegant forms and charming fictions consecrated by the Greek imagination served as the richest ornaments of Pagan houses, dress, and furniture. Even the arts of music and painting, of eloquence and poetry, flowed from the same impure source. In the language of the fathers, Apollo and the Muses were mouthpieces of the infernal spirit, Homer and Virgil were the most eminent of his servants, and the beautiful mythology that pervades and animates their works of genius exists to celebrate the glory of the demons. Even the everyday speech of Greece and Rome teemed with familiar but impious expressions, which the careless Christian might too thoughtlessly utter, or too patiently hear.

The dangerous temptations that lay in ambush on every side to surprise the unguarded believer attacked with redoubled force on the days of solemn festivals. These were framed and distributed through the year so artfully that superstition always wore the appearance of pleasure, and often of virtue. Some of the most sacred festivals in the Roman calendar were designed to greet the new calends of January with vows of public and private happiness; to indulge the pious remembrance of the dead and the living; to fix the inviolable boundaries of property; to hail, at the return of spring, the genial powers of fertility; to commemorate the two great eras of Rome, the founding of the city and of the republic; and to restore, during the humane license of the Saturnalia, the primitive equality of mankind. Some idea of the Christians' abhorrence for such impious ceremonies can be gathered from the scrupulous delicacy they showed on a far less alarming occasion. On days of general festivity, it was the custom of the ancients to decorate their doors with lamps and laurel branches, and to crown their heads with garlands of flowers. This innocent and elegant practice might perhaps have been tolerated as a purely civil institution. But it most unluckily happened that the doors were under the protection of the household gods, that the laurel was sacred to the lover of Daphne, and that garlands of flowers, though often worn simply as a sign of joy or mourning, had originally been dedicated to the service of superstition. The trembling Christians who were persuaded, in this one instance, to comply with the fashion of their country and the commands of the magistrate labored under the gloomiest apprehensions — from the reproaches of their own conscience, the censures of the church, and the threats of divine vengeance.

Such was the anxious vigilance required to guard the purity of the gospel from the infectious breath of idolatry. The followers of the established religion performed its public and private rites carelessly, out of upbringing and habit. But every such occasion gave the Christians an opportunity to declare and confirm their zealous opposition. These constant protestations continually fortified their attachment to the faith; and as their zeal grew, they fought with all the more ardor and success in the holy war they had undertaken against the empire of the demons.

II. The writings of Cicero display in the most vivid colors the ignorance, the errors, and the uncertainty of the ancient philosophers regarding the immortality of the soul. When they want to arm their disciples against the fear of death, they teach, as an obvious though melancholy proposition, that the fatal stroke of our dissolution releases us from the calamities of life, and that those who no longer exist can no longer suffer. Yet there were a few sages of Greece and Rome who had formed a loftier and, in some respects, a truer idea of human nature — though it must be admitted that in this sublime inquiry their reason was often guided by their imagination, and their imagination prompted by their vanity. When they contemplated with satisfaction the range of their own mental powers; when they exercised the faculties of memory, of fancy, and of judgment in the most profound speculations or the most important labors; and when they reflected on the desire for fame, which carried them into future ages far beyond the boundary of death and the grave — they were unwilling to class themselves with the beasts of the field, or to suppose that a being whose dignity they so sincerely admired could be confined to a patch of earth and a few years of existence. With this favorable bias they called to their aid the science — or rather the vocabulary — of metaphysics. They soon discovered that since none of the properties of matter can account for the operations of the mind, the human soul must be a substance distinct from the body: pure, simple, and spiritual, incapable of dissolution, and capable of a far higher degree of virtue and happiness once released from its bodily prison. From these plausible and noble principles, the philosophers who walked in Plato's footsteps drew a wholly unwarranted conclusion: they asserted not only the future immortality but the past eternity of the human soul, which they were too ready to regard as a portion of the infinite and self-existing spirit that pervades and sustains the universe. A doctrine so far removed from the senses and the experience of mankind might serve to amuse the leisure of a philosophic mind, or, in the silence of solitude, it might occasionally lend a ray of comfort to despairing virtue; but the faint impression received in the schools was soon erased by the commerce and business of active life. We know enough about the eminent figures who flourished in the age of Cicero and the first Caesars — their actions, their characters, and their motives — to be certain that their conduct in this life was never governed by any serious conviction of the rewards or punishments of a life to come. At the Roman bar and in the senate, the ablest orators had no fear of offending their audience by exposing that doctrine as an idle and extravagant notion, rejected with contempt by every man of liberal education and understanding.

Since, then, the most sublime efforts of philosophy can go no further than feebly pointing out the desire, the hope, or at most the probability of a future state, nothing except a divine revelation can establish the existence, or describe the condition, of the invisible country destined to receive the souls of men after their separation from the body. But the popular religions of Greece and Rome suffered from several inherent defects that made them quite unequal to so demanding a task. First, the general system of their mythology rested on no solid proofs, and the wisest of the Pagans had already repudiated its usurped authority. Second, the description of the infernal regions had been abandoned to the fancy of painters and poets, who populated them with so many phantoms and monsters, and who distributed their rewards and punishments with so little equity, that a solemn truth most congenial to the human heart was smothered and disgraced by an absurd tangle of the wildest fictions. Third, among the devout polytheists of Greece and Rome the doctrine of a future state was hardly regarded as a fundamental article of faith. The providence of the gods, concerned with public communities rather than private individuals, was displayed chiefly on the visible stage of the present world. The petitions offered at the altars of Jupiter or Apollo expressed their worshippers' anxiety for earthly happiness, and their ignorance or indifference about a life to come. The important truth of the immortality of the soul was taught with more diligence, and more success, in India, in Assyria, in Egypt, and in Gaul; and since we cannot credit the barbarians with superior knowledge, we must attribute the difference to the influence of an established priesthood, which used the motives of virtue as the instrument of its ambition.

We might naturally expect that a principle so essential to religion would have been revealed in the plainest terms to the chosen people of Palestine, and that it could safely have been entrusted to the hereditary priesthood of Aaron. It becomes our duty to adore the mysterious dispensations of Providence when we discover that the doctrine of the immortality of the soul is omitted from the law of Moses; it is only darkly hinted at by the prophets, and during the long interval between the Egyptian and the Babylonian servitudes, the hopes as well as the fears of the Jews appear to have been confined within the narrow compass of the present life. After Cyrus permitted the exiled nation to return to the promised land, and after Ezra restored the ancient records of their religion, two celebrated sects gradually arose at Jerusalem: the Sadducees and the Pharisees. The former, drawn from the wealthier and more distinguished ranks of society, held strictly to the literal sense of the Mosaic law, and they piously rejected the immortality of the soul as an opinion that received no support from the Divine book, which they revered as the only rule of their faith. To the authority of Scripture the Pharisees added that of tradition, and under the name of traditions they accepted various speculative tenets from the philosophy or religion of the eastern nations. The doctrines of fate or predestination, of angels and spirits, and of a future state of rewards and punishments were among these new articles of belief; and since the Pharisees, by the austerity of their manners, had drawn the mass of the Jewish people into their party, the immortality of the soul became the prevailing sentiment of the synagogue under the reign of the Asmonaean princes and pontiffs. The temperament of the Jews could not content itself with the cold and languid assent that might satisfy the mind of a polytheist; as soon as they admitted the idea of a future state, they embraced it with the zeal that has always been the mark of the nation. Their zeal, however, added nothing to its evidence, or even its probability; and it still remained necessary that the doctrine of life and immortality — dictated by nature, approved by reason, and received by superstition — should obtain the sanction of Divine truth from the authority and example of Christ.

When eternal happiness was offered to mankind on condition of adopting the faith and observing the precepts of the gospel, it is no wonder that so advantageous an offer was accepted by great numbers from every religion, every rank, and every province of the Roman empire. The ancient Christians were animated by a contempt for their present existence and by a well-grounded confidence in immortality, of which the doubtful and imperfect faith of modern ages can give us no adequate notion. In the primitive church the influence of truth was powerfully reinforced by an opinion that, whatever respect its usefulness and antiquity may deserve, has not been found to agree with experience: it was universally believed that the end of the world and the kingdom of heaven were at hand. The near approach of this wondrous event had been predicted by the apostles; the tradition of it was preserved by their earliest disciples; and those who took the words of Christ himself literally were bound to expect the second and glorious coming of the Son of Man in the clouds before the total extinction of that generation which had seen his humble condition on earth, and which might still witness the calamities of the Jews under Vespasian or Hadrian. The passage of seventeen centuries has taught us not to press the mysterious language of prophecy and revelation too closely; but as long as this error was permitted, for wise purposes, to subsist in the church, it produced the most salutary effects on the faith and practice of Christians, who lived in awed expectation of the moment when the globe itself, and every race of mankind, would tremble at the appearance of their divine Judge.

The ancient and popular doctrine of the Millennium was intimately connected with the second coming of Christ. Just as the work of creation had been finished in six days, so its duration in its present state was fixed — according to a tradition attributed to the prophet Elijah — at six thousand years. By the same analogy it was inferred that this long period of labor and strife, now almost expired, would be followed by a joyful Sabbath of a thousand years, and that Christ, with the triumphant band of saints and of the elect who had escaped death or been miraculously revived, would reign on earth until the time appointed for the last and general resurrection. So pleasing was this hope to the minds of believers that the *New Jerusalem*, the seat of this blissful kingdom, was quickly painted in all the gayest colors of the imagination. A happiness consisting only of pure and spiritual pleasure would have seemed too refined for its inhabitants, who were still assumed to possess their human nature and senses. A garden of Eden, with the amusements of pastoral life, no longer suited the advanced state of society that prevailed under the Roman empire. A city was therefore erected of gold and precious stones, and a supernatural abundance of grain and wine was bestowed on the surrounding territory, in the free enjoyment of whose spontaneous produce this happy and benevolent people would never be restrained by any jealous laws of exclusive property. The assurance of such a Millennium was carefully taught by a succession of fathers, from Justin Martyr and Irenaeus, who had conversed with the immediate disciples of the apostles, down to Lactantius, who was tutor to the son of Constantine. Though it may not have been universally received, it appears to have been the reigning sentiment of the orthodox believers; and it seems so well suited to the desires and anxieties of mankind that it must have contributed very considerably to the progress of the Christian faith. But when the edifice of the church was nearly complete, the temporary scaffolding was taken down. The doctrine of Christ's reign on earth was first treated as a profound allegory, was gradually reduced to a doubtful and useless opinion, and was at last rejected as the absurd invention of heresy and fanaticism. A mysterious prophecy, which still forms part of the sacred canon but which was thought to favor the discredited belief, only narrowly escaped proscription by the church.

While the happiness and glory of a temporal reign were promised to the disciples of Christ, the most dreadful calamities were pronounced against an unbelieving world. The building of the new Jerusalem was to advance step for step with the destruction of the mystic Babylon; and as long as the emperors who reigned before Constantine persisted in the profession of idolatry, the epithet of Babylon was applied to the city and the empire of Rome. A regular sequence was drawn up of all the moral and physical evils that can afflict a flourishing nation: internal discord and the invasion of the fiercest barbarians from the unknown regions of the North; pestilence and famine, comets and eclipses, earthquakes and floods. All these were only so many preparatory and alarming signs of the great catastrophe of Rome, when the country of the Scipios and Caesars would be consumed by a flame from Heaven, and the city of the seven hills, with her palaces, her temples, and her triumphal arches, would be buried in a vast lake of fire and brimstone. It might, however, offer some consolation to Roman vanity that the end of their empire would also be the end of the world itself — which, having once perished by the element of water, was destined to suffer a second and speedy destruction by the element of fire. In the expectation of a general conflagration, the faith of the Christian happily coincided with the tradition of the East, the philosophy of the Stoics, and the analogy of Nature; and even the country which, for religious reasons, had been chosen as the origin and principal scene of the conflagration was the best fitted for the purpose by natural and physical causes — by its deep caverns, its beds of sulphur, and its numerous volcanoes, of which Aetna, Vesuvius, and Lipari offer only a very imperfect sample. The calmest and most intrepid sceptic could not refuse to admit that the destruction of the present system of the world by fire was, in itself, extremely probable. The Christian, who based his belief far less on the fallacious arguments of reason than on the authority of tradition and the interpretation of Scripture, awaited it with terror and confidence as a certain and approaching event; and since his mind was perpetually filled with this solemn idea, he regarded every disaster that befell the empire as an infallible symptom of an expiring world.

The condemnation of the wisest and most virtuous of the Pagans, on account of their ignorance or disbelief of the divine truth, seems to offend the reason and the humanity of the present age. But the primitive church, whose faith was of much firmer consistency, delivered over to eternal torture, without hesitation, the far greater part of the human species. A charitable hope might perhaps be indulged in favor of Socrates, or a few other sages of antiquity who had consulted the light of reason before that of the gospel had risen. But it was unanimously affirmed that those who, since the birth or the death of Christ, had obstinately persisted in the worship of the demons neither deserved nor could expect a pardon from the offended justice of the Deity. These rigid sentiments, unknown to the ancient world, appear to have infused a spirit of bitterness into a system of love and harmony. The ties of blood and friendship were frequently torn apart by differences of religious faith; and the Christians, who in this world found themselves oppressed by the power of the Pagans, were sometimes seduced by resentment and spiritual pride into taking delight in the prospect of their future triumph.

> "You are fond of spectacles," exclaims the stern Tertullian; "expect the greatest of all spectacles, the last and eternal judgment of the universe. How I shall admire, how I shall laugh, how I shall rejoice, how I shall exult, when I behold so many proud monarchs, so many imagined gods, groaning in the lowest abyss of darkness; so many magistrates who persecuted the name of the Lord, liquefying in fiercer fires than any they ever kindled against the Christians; so many sage philosophers blushing in red-hot flames alongside their deluded pupils; so many celebrated poets trembling before the tribunal not of Minos but of Christ; so many tragedians, more tuneful now in voicing their own sufferings; so many dancers—"

But the humanity of the reader will permit me to draw a veil over the rest of this infernal description, which the zealous African pursues through a long series of labored and unfeeling witticisms.

There were doubtless many among the primitive Christians whose temper better matched the meekness and charity of their profession. Many felt sincere compassion for the danger of their friends and countrymen, and exerted the most benevolent zeal to save them from the impending destruction. The careless polytheist, assailed by new and unexpected terrors against which neither his priests nor his philosophers could offer any sure protection, was very frequently frightened into submission by the threat of eternal tortures. His fears might assist the progress of his faith and reason; and if he could once bring himself to suspect that the Christian religion might possibly be true, it became an easy task to convince him that it was the safest and most prudent side he could possibly take.

III. The supernatural gifts ascribed to the Christians, even in this life, above the rest of mankind must have contributed to their own comfort, and very frequently to the conviction of unbelievers. Beyond the occasional prodigies that might sometimes be worked by the direct intervention of the Deity when he suspended the laws of Nature in the service of religion, the Christian church, from the time of the apostles and their first disciples, has claimed an uninterrupted succession of miraculous powers: the gift of tongues, of vision, and of prophecy; the power of expelling demons, of healing the sick, and of raising the dead. Knowledge of foreign languages was frequently bestowed on the contemporaries of Irenaeus — though Irenaeus himself was left to struggle with the difficulties of a barbarous dialect while he preached the gospel to the natives of Gaul. Divine inspiration, whether it came as a waking or a sleeping vision, is described as a favor liberally granted to all ranks of the faithful — to women as well as elders, to boys as well as bishops. When their devout minds had been sufficiently prepared by a regimen of prayer, fasting, and vigils to receive the extraordinary impulse, they were transported out of their senses and delivered in ecstasy whatever was inspired, being mere organs of the Holy Spirit, just as a pipe or flute is of the one who blows into it. We may add that the purpose of these visions was, for the most part, either to disclose the future history of the church or to guide its present administration. The expulsion of demons from the bodies of those unfortunate persons whom they had been permitted to torment was regarded as a signal, though routine, triumph of religion, and is repeatedly cited by the ancient apologists as the most convincing evidence of the truth of Christianity. The awe-inspiring ceremony was usually performed in public, before a large crowd of spectators; the patient was relieved by the power or skill of the exorcist, and the vanquished demon was heard to confess that he was one of the fabled gods of antiquity, who had impiously usurped the adoration of mankind. But the miraculous cure of diseases of the most stubborn or even supernatural kind can no longer occasion any surprise when we recall that in the days of Irenaeus, around the end of the second century, the resurrection of the dead was far from being considered an uncommon event; that the miracle was frequently performed, when the occasion required it, by great fasting and the joint supplication of the church of the place; and that the persons thus restored by their prayers lived among them for many years afterward. At such a period, when faith could boast so many wonderful victories over death, it seems hard to account for the scepticism of those philosophers who still rejected and mocked the doctrine of the resurrection. A noble Greek staked the entire controversy on this decisive ground, and promised Theophilus, bishop of Antioch, that if he could be gratified with the sight of a single person who had actually been raised from the dead, he would immediately embrace the Christian religion. It is somewhat remarkable that the prelate of the first church of the East, however anxious for the conversion of his friend, thought it proper to decline this fair and reasonable challenge.

The miracles of the primitive church, after enjoying the sanction of ages, have lately been attacked in a very free and ingenious inquiry, which, though it has met with the most favorable reception from the public, appears to have caused general scandal among the divines of our own and of the other Protestant churches of Europe. Our differing views on this subject will be shaped much less by any particular arguments than by our habits of study and reflection — above all, by the degree of evidence we have accustomed ourselves to demand as proof of a miraculous event. The duty of a historian does not require him to interpose his private judgment in this delicate and important controversy; but he ought not to conceal the difficulty of framing a theory that reconciles the interest of religion with that of reason, of applying that theory properly, and of defining with precision the limits of that happy period, exempt from error and from deceit, to which we might be inclined to extend the gift of supernatural powers. From the first of the fathers to the last of the popes, a succession of bishops, of saints, of martyrs, and of miracles continues without interruption; and the progress of superstition was so gradual, so nearly imperceptible, that we do not know at which particular link we ought to break the chain of tradition. Every age bears testimony to the wonderful events that distinguished it, and its testimony appears no less weighty and respectable than that of the generation before, until we are gradually led to accuse our own inconsistency if, in the eighth or the twelfth century, we deny to the venerable Bede, or to the holy Bernard, the same degree of confidence that in the second century we had so liberally granted to Justin or to Irenaeus. If the truth of any of these miracles is to be judged by their apparent use and propriety, every age had unbelievers to convince, heretics to confute, and idolatrous nations to convert; and sufficient motives could always be produced to justify the intervention of Heaven. And yet, since every friend of revelation is persuaded of the reality of miraculous powers, and every reasonable man is convinced of their cessation, there must evidently have been *some period* in which they were withdrawn, suddenly or gradually, from the Christian church. Whatever era is chosen for that purpose — the death of the apostles, the conversion of the Roman empire, or the extinction of the Arian heresy — the obliviousness of the Christians who lived at the time will equally furnish just cause for surprise. They continued to assert their pretensions after they had lost their power. Credulity performed the office of faith; fanaticism was permitted to assume the language of inspiration; and the effects of accident or contrivance were ascribed to supernatural causes. The recent experience of genuine miracles should have instructed the Christian world in the ways of Providence, and habituated their eye (if we may use so inadequate an expression) to the style of the divine artist. Should the most skilful painter of modern Italy presume to sign his feeble imitations with the name of Raphael or of Correggio, the insolent fraud would soon be detected and indignantly rejected.
