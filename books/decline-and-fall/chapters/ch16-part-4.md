# Chapter XVI, Part 4 — From Toleration to the Great Persecution: Diocletian's Edicts of 303

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XVI (The Conduct of the Roman Government Towards the Christians, from the Reign of Nero to That of Constantine), Part 4 of 5
>

## Overview

This part carries the chapter to its turning point: the outbreak of the Great Persecution under Diocletian in AD 303. Gibbon first finishes the story of Paul of Samosata, the heretical bishop of Antioch whose deposition ended up being enforced by the pagan emperor Aurelian — a trial Gibbon treats as proof that by the 270s the empire's magistrates recognized the Christian church as a real institution with property, privileges, and internal government. He then paints the church's remarkable prosperity during the first eighteen-plus years of Diocletian's reign: Christian empresses and palace eunuchs, Christians in high office, new and grander churches — and, in a characteristic twist, cites the church's own moral corruption (lamented by Eusebius) as evidence of just how much liberty it enjoyed.

Beneath the calm, Gibbon traces the gathering storm. Two centuries of religious polemic had finally stung the pagans into taking their own gods seriously; the Neoplatonist philosophers allied themselves with the priests against the Christians; and Diocletian's junior colleagues Maximian and Galerius — unlettered soldiers — nursed an implacable hatred of the faith, fed by provocations like the Christian conscript Maximilianus and the centurion Marcellus, who renounced his arms at a public festival.

The core of the part is the winter conference at Nicomedia, where Galerius extracted from a reluctant Diocletian the decision to destroy Christianity. Gibbon narrates the demolition of the great church of Nicomedia on the feast of the Terminalia (February 23, 303), the sweeping first edict — churches razed, scriptures burned, property confiscated, Christians stripped of legal protection — the defiant Christian who tore the edict down and was roasted alive, the suspicious palace fires that hardened Diocletian, and the escalating later edicts that imprisoned the clergy and unleashed a general persecution. Along the way he notes the "Traditors" who surrendered their scriptures, the bishop Felix who died rather than comply, and the Phrygian town whose whole Christian population was burned in its church.

The part closes by framing the final section of the chapter: after Diocletian's abdication, the persecution's severity varied by region for ten years, and in the West the humane Constantius — and then his son Constantine, the first openly Christian emperor — sheltered the church. Throughout, Gibbon's skepticism about his sources (partisan ecclesiastical historians, improbable stories) and his eye for political rather than religious motives remain on full display.

## Key Concepts

- **The trial of Paul of Samosata** — Aurelian's willingness to adjudicate a church dispute (by deferring to the bishops of Italy) shows the empire had de facto recognized the Christian church's existence, property, and internal government, even before any law did.
- **Prosperity as evidence of toleration** — Gibbon argues, with pointed irony, that the corruption, ambition, and infighting of the clergy under Diocletian prove how much freedom the Christians "enjoyed and abused."
- **The pagan revival** — two hundred years of religious controversy taught the polytheists to believe in and defend their own mythology; even philosophy, once religion's most dangerous enemy, became its ally through the Neoplatonists.
- **The distinct republic** — the argument that decided Diocletian: the Christians formed an independent state within the state, with its own laws, magistrates, treasury, and obedient congregations bound together by councils of bishops.
- **The first edict of 303** — churches demolished, secret worship punishable by death, scriptures surrendered and burned, church property confiscated, and Christians placed outside the protection of the law: able to be sued but not to sue.
- **Traditors** — clergy who saved their lives by handing over the scriptures, earning a badge of infamy that would breed lasting schism in the African church.
- **Persecution by unequal enforcement** — the edicts' effect varied with geography and personality: savage under Galerius's influence, softened almost to nothing in Gaul and Britain under Constantius, whose son Constantine would make Christianity the empire's reigning religion.

## Modern Translation

Despite these scandalous vices, if Paul of Samosata had kept the orthodox faith intact, his reign over the capital of Syria would have ended only with his life — and if a well-timed persecution had come along, one act of courage might have placed him among the saints and martyrs. But certain subtle and hair-splitting errors concerning the doctrine of the Trinity, which he rashly adopted and stubbornly defended, roused the zeal and indignation of the Eastern churches. From Egypt to the Black Sea, the bishops were up in arms and on the move. (AD 270) Councils were held, refutations published, excommunications pronounced; ambiguous compromise formulas were accepted and then rejected in turn; treaties were concluded and broken. At last Paul of Samosata was stripped of his episcopal office by the sentence of seventy or eighty bishops assembled for that purpose at Antioch — who then, without consulting the rights of either clergy or people, appointed a successor on their own authority. The obvious irregularity of this proceeding swelled the ranks of the disaffected faction, and since Paul, who was no stranger to the arts of courts, had worked his way into the favor of Zenobia, he kept possession of the episcopal house and office for more than four years. Aurelian's victory changed the face of the East, and the two contending parties — each calling the other schismatic and heretical — were either commanded or permitted to plead their cause before the conqueror's tribunal. This public and highly unusual trial offers convincing proof that the existence, property, privileges, and internal government of the Christians were acknowledged, if not by the laws of the empire, then at least by its magistrates. As a pagan and a soldier, Aurelian could hardly be expected to weigh whether Paul's opinions or those of his adversaries better matched the true standard of orthodox belief. His decision, however, rested on general principles of fairness and reason. (AD 274) He regarded the bishops of Italy as the most impartial and respectable judges among the Christians, and as soon as he learned that they had unanimously approved the council's sentence, he accepted their opinion and immediately ordered that Paul be forced to give up the temporal possessions attached to an office of which, in the judgment of his own brethren, he had been lawfully deprived. But while we applaud Aurelian's justice, we should not overlook his policy: he wanted to restore and cement the provinces' dependence on the capital by every means that could engage the interests or the prejudices of any group of his subjects.

(AD 284–303) Amid the empire's frequent revolutions, the Christians continued to flourish in peace and prosperity; and although a celebrated "era of the martyrs" has been dated from the accession of Diocletian, the new system of government that this prince's wisdom introduced and maintained continued, for more than eighteen years, to breathe the mildest and most liberal spirit of religious toleration. Diocletian's own mind was, admittedly, less suited to speculative inquiry than to the active labors of war and government. His prudence made him averse to any great innovation, and though his temperament had little room for zeal or enthusiasm, he always kept up a habitual regard for the ancient gods of the empire. But the leisure of the two empresses — his wife Prisca and his daughter Valeria — allowed them to listen with more attention and respect to the truths of Christianity, which in every age has acknowledged its great debt to female devotion. The principal eunuchs — Lucian and Dorotheus, Gorgonius and Andrew — who attended Diocletian's person, enjoyed his favor, and ran his household, used their powerful influence to protect the faith they had embraced. Their example was followed by many of the most important officers of the palace, who in their various posts had charge of the imperial regalia, the robes, the furniture, the jewels, and even the privy purse; and though they were sometimes obliged to accompany the emperor when he sacrificed in the temple, they enjoyed — along with their wives, children, and slaves — the free exercise of the Christian religion. Diocletian and his colleagues frequently conferred the most important offices on men who openly avowed their abhorrence of the worship of the gods but had shown abilities useful to the state. The bishops held an honorable rank in their provinces and were treated with distinction and respect not only by the people but by the magistrates themselves. In almost every city the old churches proved too small to hold the growing crowds of converts, and grander, more spacious buildings were erected in their place for public worship. The corruption of morals and principles that Eusebius laments so forcefully can be seen not only as a consequence of the liberty the Christians enjoyed and abused under Diocletian, but as proof of it. Prosperity had slackened the nerves of discipline. Fraud, envy, and malice prevailed in every congregation. Presbyters schemed for the office of bishop, which every day became a more tempting prize for their ambition. The bishops, competing with one another for ecclesiastical preeminence, behaved as if they were claiming a secular and tyrannical power within the church; and the lively faith that still set Christians apart from the Gentiles showed itself far less in their lives than in their polemical writings.

Despite this apparent security, an attentive observer might have detected symptoms threatening the church with a more violent persecution than any it had yet endured. The zeal and rapid progress of the Christians shook the polytheists out of their lazy indifference toward the gods whom custom and education had taught them to revere. The mutual provocations of a religious war that had already lasted more than two hundred years sharpened the animosity on both sides. The pagans were incensed at the effrontery of a recent and obscure sect that presumed to accuse their countrymen of error and to consign their ancestors to eternal misery. The habit of defending the popular mythology against the invectives of an implacable enemy produced in their minds a certain faith and reverence for a system they had been used to regarding with the most careless levity. The supernatural powers claimed by the church inspired terror and rivalry at once. The followers of the established religion dug themselves in behind a similar rampart of prodigies: they invented new modes of sacrifice, expiation, and initiation; tried to revive the credit of their dying oracles; and listened with eager credulity to every impostor who flattered their prejudices with a tale of wonders. Each party seemed to grant the truth of the miracles claimed by the other; and since both were content to ascribe them to the arts of magic and the power of demons, they cooperated in restoring and entrenching the reign of superstition. Philosophy, superstition's most dangerous enemy, was now converted into its most useful ally. The groves of the Academy, the gardens of Epicurus, and even the portico of the Stoics stood almost deserted, dismissed as so many schools of skepticism or impiety; and many Romans wanted the writings of Cicero condemned and suppressed by authority of the senate. The fashionable sect of the new Platonists judged it prudent to ally themselves with the priests — whom they perhaps despised — against the Christians, whom they had reason to fear. These fashionable philosophers pursued their project of extracting allegorical wisdom from the fictions of the Greek poets; instituted mysterious rites of devotion for their chosen disciples; recommended the worship of the ancient gods as emblems or ministers of the Supreme Deity; and composed against the faith of the Gospel many elaborate treatises, which the prudence of orthodox emperors has since committed to the flames.

Although Diocletian's policy and Constantius's humanity inclined them both to keep the maxims of toleration inviolate, it soon became clear that their two associates, Maximian and Galerius, harbored the most implacable hatred for the name and religion of the Christians. The minds of those princes had never been enlightened by learning; education had never softened their tempers. They owed their greatness to their swords, and at the height of their fortune they still retained the superstitious prejudices of soldiers and peasants. In the general administration of the provinces they obeyed the laws their benefactor had established; but within their own camps and palaces they found frequent occasions for a quiet persecution — for which the imprudent zeal of the Christians sometimes supplied the most plausible pretexts. A death sentence was carried out on Maximilianus, an African youth whose own father had presented him to the magistrate as a fit and lawful recruit, but who stubbornly insisted that his conscience would not allow him to take up the profession of a soldier. And it could hardly be expected that any government would let the act of Marcellus the centurion pass unpunished. On the day of a public festival, that officer threw down his belt, his weapons, and the insignia of his office, and cried out in a loud voice that he would obey no one but Jesus Christ the eternal King, and that he renounced forever the use of carnal weapons and the service of an idolatrous master. As soon as the soldiers recovered from their astonishment, they seized Marcellus. He was examined in the city of Tingi by the president of that part of Mauritania, and since he stood convicted by his own confession, he was condemned and beheaded — for the crime of desertion. Examples of this kind smack much less of religious persecution than of military or even civil law; but they served to alienate the minds of the emperors, to justify the severity of Galerius, who dismissed a great number of Christian officers from their posts, and to lend authority to the opinion that a sect of fanatics professing principles so contrary to public safety must either remain useless subjects of the empire or soon become dangerous ones.

After the success of the Persian war had raised Galerius's hopes and reputation, he spent a winter with Diocletian in the palace of Nicomedia, and the fate of Christianity became the subject of their secret consultations. The experienced emperor was still inclined toward lenient measures; and though he readily agreed to exclude Christians from any employment in the household or the army, he urged in the strongest terms both the danger and the cruelty of shedding the blood of those deluded fanatics. Galerius at length extracted from him permission to summon a council composed of a few of the most distinguished figures in the civil and military departments of the state. The great question was debated in their presence, and those ambitious courtiers easily saw that their job was to lend their eloquence to the Caesar's importunate violence. We may presume they pressed every argument that could engage their sovereign's pride, piety, or fears in the destruction of Christianity. Perhaps they argued that the glorious work of delivering the empire remained unfinished as long as an independent people was allowed to survive and multiply in the heart of the provinces. The Christians — so it might plausibly be alleged — having renounced the gods and the institutions of Rome, had set up a distinct republic: one that could still be suppressed before it acquired any military force, but which was already governed by its own laws and magistrates, possessed a public treasury, and was intimately connected in all its parts by the frequent assemblies of the bishops, to whose decrees their numerous and wealthy congregations yielded implicit obedience. Arguments like these may seem to have determined Diocletian's reluctant mind to adopt a new system of persecution. But though we may suspect, we cannot narrate, the secret intrigues of the palace — the private ambitions and resentments, the jealousy of women or eunuchs, and all those trifling but decisive causes that so often shape the fate of empires and the counsels of the wisest monarchs.

The emperors' pleasure was at last made known to the Christians, who through that gloomy winter had waited anxiously for the outcome of so many secret consultations. (AD 303) The twenty-third of February, which happened to coincide with the Roman festival of the Terminalia, was appointed — whether by accident or by design — to set bounds to the progress of Christianity. At the first light of dawn the Praetorian prefect, accompanied by several generals, tribunes, and revenue officers, went to the principal church of Nicomedia, which stood on a rise in the most populous and beautiful quarter of the city. The doors were instantly broken open; the officials rushed into the sanctuary; and having searched in vain for any visible object of worship, they had to content themselves with burning the volumes of Holy Scripture. Diocletian's ministers were followed by a large body of guards and pioneers, who marched in battle order equipped with all the instruments used in demolishing fortified cities. By their unrelenting labor, a sacred building that towered above the imperial palace — and had long excited the indignation and envy of the Gentiles — was within a few hours leveled to the ground.

The next day the general edict of persecution was published. Diocletian, still averse to bloodshed, had moderated the fury of Galerius, who had proposed that anyone refusing to sacrifice should be burned alive on the spot; even so, the penalties inflicted on Christian obstinacy could be considered rigorous and effective enough. The edict ordered that their churches, in every province of the empire, be demolished to their foundations, and it decreed the death penalty for anyone who presumed to hold secret assemblies for religious worship. The philosophers, who now took on the unworthy office of directing the blind zeal of persecution, had studied the nature and character of the Christian religion with some care; and knowing that the speculative doctrines of the faith were held to be contained in the writings of the prophets, the evangelists, and the apostles, it was most probably they who suggested the order that bishops and presbyters surrender all their sacred books to the magistrates — who were commanded, under the severest penalties, to burn them publicly and ceremonially. By the same edict, the property of the church was confiscated at a stroke; its various assets were sold to the highest bidder, absorbed into the imperial domain, bestowed on cities and corporations, or granted to the solicitations of grasping courtiers. Having taken such effective measures to abolish the worship and dissolve the government of the Christians, the emperors thought it necessary to impose the most intolerable hardships on those perverse individuals who still rejected the religion of Nature, of Rome, and of their ancestors. Persons of free birth were declared incapable of holding any honors or offices; slaves were forever cut off from the hope of freedom; and the whole body of the Christian people was placed outside the protection of the law. Judges were authorized to hear and decide every suit brought against a Christian; but Christians were not permitted to complain of any injury done to them — so those unfortunate sectaries were exposed to the full severity of public justice while being shut out from all its benefits. This new species of martyrdom — so painful and lingering, so obscure and ignominious — was perhaps the best calculated to wear down the constancy of the faithful; and there can be no doubt that human passions and human interests stood ready, on this occasion, to second the designs of the emperors. Still, the policy of a well-ordered government must sometimes have intervened on behalf of the oppressed Christians; the Roman princes could not entirely remove the fear of punishment, or wink at every act of fraud and violence, without exposing their own authority and the rest of their subjects to the most alarming dangers.

This edict had scarcely been posted in the most conspicuous place in Nicomedia before it was torn down by the hands of a Christian, who at the same time expressed, in the bitterest invectives, his contempt as well as his abhorrence for such impious and tyrannical rulers. His offense, under even the mildest laws, amounted to treason and deserved death; and if it is true that he was a man of rank and education, those circumstances could only aggravate his guilt. He was burned — or rather roasted — over a slow fire; and his executioners, eager to avenge the personal insult offered to the emperors, exhausted every refinement of cruelty without being able to subdue his patience, or to alter the steady, insulting smile that he kept on his face even in his dying agonies. The Christians, though they admitted that his conduct had not been strictly in keeping with the rules of prudence, admired the divine fervor of his zeal; and the extravagant praise they lavished on the memory of their hero and martyr helped fix a deep impression of terror and hatred in the mind of Diocletian.

His fears were soon inflamed by a danger from which he escaped very narrowly. Within fifteen days, the palace of Nicomedia — and even Diocletian's own bedchamber — twice caught fire; and though both blazes were put out without serious damage, the strange repetition of the fire was reasonably taken as clear proof that it was no accident or negligence. Suspicion naturally fell on the Christians, and it was suggested, with some degree of plausibility, that those desperate fanatics — provoked by their present sufferings and dreading worse to come — had conspired with their faithful brethren, the eunuchs of the palace, against the lives of two emperors whom they detested as the irreconcilable enemies of the church of God. Jealousy and resentment filled every breast, above all Diocletian's. A great number of persons distinguished by the offices they had held or the favor they had enjoyed were thrown into prison. Every mode of torture was applied, and the court, like the city, was polluted with many bloody executions. But since it proved impossible to extract any disclosure of this mysterious affair, we are obliged either to presume the innocence of the sufferers or to admire their resolution. A few days later Galerius left Nicomedia in haste, declaring that if he delayed his departure from that doomed palace he would fall a victim to the rage of the Christians. The ecclesiastical historians — our only source for even a partial and imperfect knowledge of this persecution — are at a loss to explain the emperors' fears and dangers. Two of these writers, a prince and a rhetorician, were eyewitnesses to the fire of Nicomedia. One ascribes it to lightning and the wrath of God; the other insists it was set by the malice of Galerius himself.

Since the edict against the Christians was intended as a general law for the whole empire, and since Diocletian and Galerius — though they might not wait for the consent of the Western princes — were assured of their concurrence, it would seem more consistent with our ideas of statecraft for the governors of all the provinces to have received secret instructions to publish this declaration of war on one and the same day, each in his own department. One would at least expect that the convenience of the public highways and the established post system would have let the emperors transmit their orders with the utmost speed from the palace of Nicomedia to the far ends of the Roman world — and that they would not have allowed fifty days to pass before the edict was published in Syria, and nearly four months before it was communicated to the cities of Africa. The delay may perhaps be put down to the cautious temper of Diocletian, who had given only reluctant consent to the measures of persecution and wanted to try the experiment under his own eye before giving free rein to the disorders and discontent it was bound to provoke in the distant provinces. At first, indeed, the magistrates were restrained from shedding blood, but every other severity was permitted — even recommended — to their zeal; and the Christians, though they cheerfully gave up the ornaments of their churches, could not bring themselves to interrupt their religious assemblies or to consign their sacred books to the flames. The pious obstinacy of Felix, an African bishop, appears to have embarrassed the subordinate officials of the government. The curator of his city sent him in chains to the proconsul; the proconsul forwarded him to the Praetorian prefect of Italy; and Felix, who scorned even to give an evasive answer, was at length beheaded at Venusia in Lucania — a place on which the birth of Horace has conferred fame. This precedent, and perhaps some imperial rescript issued in consequence of it, seemed to authorize provincial governors to punish with death the Christians' refusal to surrender their sacred books. There were, no doubt, many who seized this opportunity to win the crown of martyrdom; but there were also too many who purchased an ignominious life by disclosing and betraying the Holy Scripture into the hands of the infidels. A great number even of bishops and presbyters earned, by this criminal compliance, the opprobrious label of *Traditors* ("handers-over," betrayers); and their offense produced much scandal at the time, and much discord afterward, in the African church.

Copies and translations of Scripture had already multiplied so widely across the empire that even the most rigorous inquisition could no longer do fatal damage; and even the sacrifice of those volumes kept in every congregation for public use required the connivance of some treacherous and unworthy Christians. The ruin of the churches, on the other hand, was easily accomplished by the authority of the government and the labor of the pagans. In some provinces, however, the magistrates contented themselves with closing the places of worship. In others they complied more literally with the terms of the edict: after removing the doors, the benches, and the pulpit, which they burned as if on a funeral pyre, they completely demolished what remained of the building. It is perhaps to this melancholy occasion that we should attach a very remarkable story — told with so many varying and improbable details that it excites our curiosity rather than satisfies it. In a small town in Phrygia, whose name and location we are not told, it appears that the magistrates and the general body of the people had embraced the Christian faith; and since resistance to the edict's execution was anticipated, the provincial governor came supported by a large detachment of legionaries. At their approach the citizens shut themselves inside the church, resolved either to defend that sacred building by force of arms or to perish in its ruins. They indignantly rejected the notice — and the permission — given them to withdraw, until the soldiers, provoked by their obstinate refusal, set fire to the building on every side and consumed, in this extraordinary kind of martyrdom, a great number of Phrygians, together with their wives and children.

Some minor disturbances in Syria and on the Armenian frontier — suppressed almost as soon as they broke out — gave the enemies of the church a very plausible occasion to insinuate that those troubles had been secretly fomented by the intrigues of the bishops, who had already forgotten their ostentatious professions of passive and unlimited obedience. Diocletian's resentment, or his fears, at last carried him beyond the bounds of the moderation he had until then preserved, and in a series of cruel edicts he declared his intention of abolishing the Christian name. By the first of these edicts, provincial governors were directed to arrest all persons of the ecclesiastical order, and the prisons intended for the vilest criminals soon filled with a multitude of bishops, presbyters, deacons, readers, and exorcists. A second edict commanded the magistrates to employ every method of severity that might reclaim them from their odious superstition and force them back to the established worship of the gods. A subsequent edict extended this rigorous order to the whole body of Christians, who were now exposed to a violent and general persecution. In place of the old salutary safeguards, which had required the direct and formal testimony of an accuser, it now became the duty — and the interest — of imperial officers to seek out, hunt down, and torment the most exposed among the faithful. Heavy penalties were decreed against anyone who presumed to shield a proscribed sectary from the just indignation of the gods and the emperors. Yet despite the severity of this law, the courageous conduct of many pagans in hiding their friends or relatives offers honorable proof that the rage of superstition had not extinguished in their minds the sentiments of nature and humanity.

(AD 303–311) No sooner had Diocletian published his edicts against the Christians than — as if he wished to commit the work of persecution to other hands — he laid down the imperial purple. The character and situation of his colleagues and successors sometimes urged them to enforce these rigorous laws and sometimes inclined them to suspend their execution; and we cannot form a just and distinct idea of this important period of church history unless we consider separately the state of Christianity in the different parts of the empire during the ten years between Diocletian's first edicts and the final peace of the church.

The mild and humane temper of Constantius was averse to oppressing any portion of his subjects. The principal offices of his palace were held by Christians. He loved them personally, valued their fidelity, and had no dislike for their religious principles. But as long as Constantius remained in the subordinate rank of Caesar, it was not in his power openly to reject the edicts of Diocletian or to disobey the commands of Maximian. His authority did, however, help to ease sufferings that he pitied and abhorred. He consented, reluctantly, to the destruction of the churches, but he ventured to protect the Christians themselves from the fury of the mob and the rigor of the laws. The provinces of Gaul (among which we may probably include those of Britain) owed the singular tranquility they enjoyed to the gentle intervention of their sovereign. But Datianus, the president or governor of Spain, moved either by zeal or by calculation, chose to execute the emperors' public edicts rather than to divine the secret intentions of Constantius; and it can scarcely be doubted that his provincial administration was stained with the blood of a few martyrs. Constantius's elevation to the supreme and independent dignity of Augustus gave free scope to the exercise of his virtues, and the shortness of his reign did not prevent him from establishing a system of toleration of which he left both the precept and the example to his son Constantine. That fortunate son, declaring himself the protector of the church from the first moment of his accession, at length earned the title of the first emperor who publicly professed and established the Christian religion. The motives of his conversion — which may be variously traced to benevolence, to policy, to conviction, or to remorse — and the progress of the revolution which, under his powerful influence and that of his sons, made Christianity the reigning religion of the Roman empire, will form a very interesting and important chapter of this history. For now it is enough to observe that every victory of Constantine brought some relief or benefit to the church.
