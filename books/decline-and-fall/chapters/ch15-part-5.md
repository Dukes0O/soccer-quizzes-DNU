# Chapter XV, Part 5 — Counting the Christians, and the Strange Silence of the Pagan World

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XV (The Progress of the Christian Religion, and the Sentiments, Manners, Numbers, and Condition of the Primitive Christians), Part 5 of 5
>

## Overview

This final part of Chapter XV completes Gibbon's fifth cause of Christianity's growth — its rapid spread — by asking the blunt question the pious sources never answer: how many Christians were there, actually? Working from the one hard number that survives (Chrysostom's figure of 100,000 Christians at Antioch under Theodosius), Gibbon reasons backward through Antioch, Alexandria, Rome, and the western provinces, and arrives at a deliberately deflating estimate: before Constantine's conversion, at most one-twentieth of the empire's subjects had "enlisted under the banner of the cross." Along the way he notes how thin the evidence really is — the origins of the western churches are so poorly recorded that history has to be padded out with monkish legends, of which the transformation of St. James the fisherman into a Moor-slaying Spanish knight serves as his choice specimen.

Gibbon then turns from the numbers to the condition of the converts. He accepts, with a show of even-handedness, the pagan charge that the new sect drew chiefly on the poor, the uneducated, women, children, and slaves — softening it as the "innocent and natural" consequence of a religion addressed to the whole human race, listing the educated exceptions (Justin, Clement, Tertullian, Origen, Cyprian), and then piously observing that the lower we set the first Christians' social standing, the more we must admire their success. The mock-devotional register here is doing exactly the opposite of what it pretends.

The chapter closes with two of the most quietly devastating passages Gibbon ever wrote. First: the great minds of the age — Seneca, the Plinys, Tacitus, Plutarch, Galen, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius — all overlooked or dismissed Christianity, and the apologists who might have persuaded them argued instead from Hebrew prophecies that meant nothing to a Gentile reader, propping up their case with pious forgeries. Second, and most famously: the age of Christ was, by the church's own account, an age of continual public miracle — yet the pagan world somehow failed to notice. Seneca and the elder Pliny, tireless catalogers of every earthquake, comet, and eclipse, both omit the three-hour supernatural darkness at the Passion. Gibbon never says the miracles did not happen; he merely reports, with perfect gravity, that no one at the time appears to have seen them. The insinuation, left hanging at the chapter's end, sets up the treatment of persecution in Chapter XVI.

## Key Concepts

- **The one-twentieth estimate** — extrapolating from Antioch and Rome, Gibbon concludes that no more than 5 percent of the empire's population was Christian before Constantine, against the inflated figures produced "by fear on the one side and by devotion on the other."
- **The Antioch calculation** — Chrysostom's 100,000 faithful, set against a city of at least half a million, yields a Christian fifth even in the East, even after sixty years of imperial favor; the persecuted western church must have been far smaller.
- **The census of the Roman clergy** — an authentic mid-third-century record (one bishop, 46 presbyters, 7 deacons, and lesser orders, supporting 1,500 dependents) lets Gibbon estimate about 50,000 Christians in a city of a million.
- **The laggard West** — Gaul, Spain, and Britain show no reliable trace of Christianity before the Antonines, and their true origins were later buried under monastic legend (St. James of Compostella as the extreme case); Africa alone converted eagerly.
- **The social composition of the church** — the pagan sneer that Christians were peasants, mechanics, women, and slaves is granted as substantially true, then reframed with mock piety as grounds for greater admiration.
- **The failure of the apologists** — defending Christianity by Hebrew prophecy (and forged Sibylline oracles) could edify believers but not persuade philosophers who neither understood nor respected the prophetic style.
- **The silence of the sages** — the era's greatest observers of nature recorded every eclipse and comet but never the miracles of the Gospel age, including the darkness at the Passion: Gibbon's climactic, unstated indictment of the miracle tradition.

## Modern Translation

Rather than minutely scrutinizing the language and motives of the writers who either celebrate or lament Christianity's progress in the East, we can observe one thing in general: none of them gives us any basis for a sound estimate of the actual number of the faithful in those provinces. One piece of evidence, however, has fortunately survived, and it throws a somewhat clearer light on this obscure but interesting subject. Under the reign of Theodosius — after Christianity had basked for more than sixty years in the sunshine of imperial favor — the ancient and illustrious church of Antioch numbered one hundred thousand persons, three thousand of whom were supported out of the public offerings. The splendor and dignity of the queen of the East, the acknowledged populousness of Caesarea, Seleucia, and Alexandria, and the death of two hundred and fifty thousand souls in the earthquake that struck Antioch under the elder Justin, all point convincingly to a total population of at least half a million — meaning that the Christians, however multiplied by zeal and by power, made up no more than a fifth of that great city. How much smaller a proportion must we assume when we compare the persecuted church with the triumphant one, the West with the East, remote villages with populous towns, and lands only recently converted with the very place where believers first received the name of Christians! It should not be concealed, however, that in another passage Chrysostom — to whom we owe this useful information — reckons the multitude of the faithful as actually larger than that of the Jews and pagans combined. But the solution to this apparent difficulty is easy and obvious. The eloquent preacher was drawing a parallel between the civil and the ecclesiastical registers of Antioch: between the list of Christians who had gained Heaven through baptism and the list of citizens entitled to a share of the public largesse. Slaves, foreigners, and infants were counted on the first list; they were excluded from the second.

Alexandria's extensive commerce, and its proximity to Palestine, gave the new religion an easy entrance. It was first embraced in large numbers by the Therapeutae, or Essenes of Lake Mareotis, a Jewish sect that had let go of much of its reverence for the Mosaic ceremonies. The austere life of the Essenes — their fasts and excommunications, their community of goods, their love of celibacy, their zeal for martyrdom, and the warmth (if not the purity) of their faith — already presented a vivid image of the primitive Christian discipline. It was in the school of Alexandria that Christian theology appears to have first taken on a regular and systematic form; and when Hadrian visited Egypt, he found a church of Jews and Greeks substantial enough to attract the notice of that inquisitive prince. But for a long time the progress of Christianity remained confined within a single city — itself a foreign colony — and until the end of the second century the predecessors of Demetrius were the only bishops of the Egyptian church. Demetrius consecrated three bishops with his own hands, and his successor Heraclas raised the number to twenty. The native population, a people marked by a sullen inflexibility of temper, received the new doctrine coldly and reluctantly; even in Origen's day it was rare to meet an Egyptian who had overcome his early prejudices in favor of his country's sacred animals. But the moment Christianity ascended the throne, the zeal of those barbarians fell in with the prevailing current: the cities of Egypt filled with bishops, and the deserts of the Thebaid swarmed with hermits.

A perpetual stream of strangers and provincials poured into the capacious bosom of Rome. Whatever was strange or odious, whoever was guilty or suspect, could hope to escape the vigilance of the law in the obscurity of that immense capital. Amid such a mixed confluence of nations, any teacher of truth or of falsehood, any founder of a virtuous or a criminal association, could easily multiply his disciples or his accomplices. Tacitus represents the Christians of Rome, at the time of Nero's incidental persecution, as already amounting to "a very great multitude" — and the language of that great historian closely resembles the style Livy uses in relating the introduction and suppression of the rites of Bacchus. After the Bacchanals had aroused the severity of the senate, it was likewise feared that a very great multitude — as it were, *another people* — had been initiated into those abhorred mysteries. A more careful inquiry soon showed that the offenders numbered no more than seven thousand: alarming enough, certainly, when considered as the object of public justice. We should apply the same fair-minded discount when interpreting the vague expressions of Tacitus, and in an earlier instance those of Pliny, when they exaggerate the crowds of deluded fanatics who had forsaken the established worship of the gods. The church of Rome was undoubtedly the first and most populous in the empire, and we possess an authentic record attesting the state of religion in that city around the middle of the third century, after a peace of thirty-eight years. The clergy at that time consisted of a bishop, forty-six presbyters, seven deacons, an equal number of sub-deacons, forty-two acolytes, and fifty readers, exorcists, and porters. The widows, the infirm, and the poor maintained by the offerings of the faithful numbered fifteen hundred. From reasoning, as well as from the analogy of Antioch, we may venture to estimate the Christians of Rome at about fifty thousand. The population of that great capital cannot perhaps be fixed exactly, but the most modest calculation will surely not put it below a million inhabitants — of whom the Christians can have made up, at most, a twentieth part.

The western provincials appear to have derived their knowledge of Christianity from the same source that had spread among them the language, the sentiments, and the manners of Rome. In this more important respect, Africa, like Gaul, was gradually shaped by the imitation of the capital. Yet despite the many favorable occasions that might have invited Roman missionaries to visit their Latin provinces, it was late before they crossed either the sea or the Alps; nor can we discover in those great countries any reliable traces of either faith or persecution earlier than the reign of the Antonines. The gospel's slow progress in the cold climate of Gaul stands in sharp contrast with the eagerness with which it seems to have been received on the burning sands of Africa. The African Christians soon became one of the principal branches of the primitive church. The practice, introduced in that province, of appointing bishops to the most insignificant towns and often to the most obscure villages served to multiply the splendor and importance of their religious societies, which during the third century were animated by the zeal of Tertullian, directed by the abilities of Cyprian, and adorned by the eloquence of Lactantius. Turn instead toward Gaul, and we must content ourselves with finding, in the time of Marcus Antoninus, the feeble and united congregations of Lyons and Vienna; and even as late as the reign of Decius we are assured that only in a few cities — Arles, Narbonne, Toulouse, Limoges, Clermont, Tours, and Paris — were scattered churches sustained by the devotion of a small number of Christians. Silence, admittedly, is quite consistent with devotion; but since it is seldom compatible with zeal, we may perceive, and lament, the languid state of Christianity in those provinces that had exchanged the Celtic tongue for the Latin — for during the first three centuries they did not produce a single ecclesiastical writer. From Gaul, which claimed a just preeminence in learning and authority over all the countries on this side of the Alps, the light of the gospel was reflected still more faintly onto the remote provinces of Spain and Britain; if we may credit the vehement assertions of Tertullian, they had already received the first rays of the faith by the time he addressed his apology to the magistrates of the emperor Severus. But the obscure and imperfect origins of the western churches of Europe were recorded so negligently that, if we wish to relate the time and manner of their foundation, we must fill the silence of antiquity with the legends that greed or superstition later dictated to the monks in the lazy gloom of their convents. Of these holy romances, only that of the apostle St. James deserves mention — for its singular extravagance. From a peaceful fisherman of the Lake of Gennesareth, he was transformed into a valorous knight who charged at the head of the Spanish chivalry in their battles against the Moors. The gravest historians have celebrated his exploits; the miraculous shrine of Compostella displayed his power; and the sword of a military order, backed by the terrors of the Inquisition, proved sufficient to dispose of every objection of profane criticism.

The progress of Christianity was not confined to the Roman empire; and according to the primitive fathers, who interpret facts by prophecy, the new religion had visited every part of the globe within a century of its divine author's death. "There exists not," says Justin Martyr, "a people, whether Greek or Barbarian, or any other race of men, by whatsoever appellation or manners they may be distinguished, however ignorant of arts or agriculture, whether they dwell under tents, or wander about in covered wagons, among whom prayers are not offered up in the name of a crucified Jesus to the Father and Creator of all things." But this splendid exaggeration — which even today it would be extremely difficult to reconcile with the actual state of mankind — can only be taken as the rash outburst of a devout but careless writer, the measure of whose belief was set by the measure of his wishes. Yet neither the belief nor the wishes of the fathers can alter the truth of history. It remains an undoubted fact that the barbarians of Scythia and Germany, who later overthrew the Roman monarchy, were sunk in the darkness of paganism; and that even the conversion of Iberia, of Armenia, and of Ethiopia was not attempted with any success until the scepter was in the hands of an orthodox emperor. Before that time, the various accidents of war and commerce might indeed spread an imperfect knowledge of the gospel among the tribes of Caledonia and among the peoples bordering the Rhine, the Danube, and the Euphrates. Beyond that last river, Edessa was distinguished by its firm and early adherence to the faith. From Edessa the principles of Christianity passed easily into the Greek and Syrian cities that obeyed the successors of Artaxerxes; but they do not appear to have made any deep impression on the minds of the Persians, whose religious system had been constructed, by the labors of a well-disciplined order of priests, with far more art and solidity than the uncertain mythology of Greece and Rome.

From this impartial, though imperfect, survey of Christianity's progress, it seems probable that the number of its converts has been greatly inflated — by fear on one side and by devotion on the other. On the irreproachable testimony of Origen, the proportion of the faithful was very small compared with the multitude of an unbelieving world; but since we are left without any precise information, it is impossible to determine, and difficult even to guess, the real numbers of the primitive Christians. The most generous calculation that can be drawn from the examples of Antioch and Rome will not allow us to imagine that more than a twentieth of the empire's subjects had enlisted under the banner of the cross before the momentous conversion of Constantine. Their habits of faith, of zeal, and of union, however, made their numbers seem greater than they were; and the same causes that would drive their future increase also made their actual strength appear more visible and more formidable.

Civil society is so constituted that, while a few persons are distinguished by riches, honors, and knowledge, the mass of the people is condemned to obscurity, ignorance, and poverty. The Christian religion, which addressed itself to the whole human race, was therefore bound to gather far more converts from the lower ranks of life than from the higher. This innocent and natural circumstance has been worked up into a very odious accusation — one which the apologists of the faith seem to deny less strenuously than its adversaries press it: that the new sect of Christians was composed almost entirely of the dregs of the populace, of peasants and laborers, of boys and women, of beggars and slaves — the last of whom might sometimes introduce the missionaries into the rich and noble households to which they belonged. These obscure teachers (so ran the charge of malice and unbelief) are as mute in public as they are talkative and dogmatic in private. While they carefully avoid the dangerous encounter with philosophers, they mingle with the rough and illiterate crowd and worm their way into those minds which age, sex, or lack of education has best prepared to receive the impression of superstitious terrors.

This unflattering picture, though not without a faint resemblance to its subject, betrays by its dark coloring and distorted features the brush of an enemy. As the humble faith of Christ spread through the world, it was embraced by a number of persons who possessed some standing through the advantages of nature or fortune. Aristides, who presented an eloquent apology to the emperor Hadrian, was an Athenian philosopher. Justin Martyr had sought divine knowledge in the schools of Zeno, of Aristotle, of Pythagoras, and of Plato before he was fortunately accosted by the old man — or rather the angel — who turned his attention to the study of the Jewish prophets. Clement of Alexandria had acquired wide reading in Greek, and Tertullian in Latin. Julius Africanus and Origen possessed a very considerable share of the learning of their times; and although the style of Cyprian differs greatly from that of Lactantius, one could almost tell that both writers had been public teachers of rhetoric. Even the study of philosophy was at length introduced among the Christians — though it did not always produce the most wholesome effects: knowledge was as often the parent of heresy as of devotion, and the description aimed at the followers of Artemon may with equal justice be applied to the various sects that resisted the successors of the apostles:

> "They presume to alter the Holy Scriptures, to abandon the ancient rule of faith, and to form their opinions according to the subtle precepts of logic. The science of the church is neglected for the study of geometry, and they lose sight of Heaven while they are employed in measuring the earth. Euclid is perpetually in their hands. Aristotle and Theophrastus are the objects of their admiration; and they express an uncommon reverence for the works of Galen. Their errors are derived from the abuse of the arts and sciences of the infidels, and they corrupt the simplicity of the Gospel by the refinements of human reason."

Nor can it truthfully be claimed that the advantages of birth and fortune were always absent from the profession of Christianity. Several Roman citizens were brought before Pliny's tribunal, and he soon discovered that a great number of persons of *every rank* in Bithynia had deserted the religion of their ancestors. His unbiased testimony deserves more credit, in this instance, than the bold challenge of Tertullian, who — addressing himself to the fears as much as to the humanity of the proconsul of Africa — assured him that if he persisted in his cruel intentions he would have to decimate Carthage, and that he would find among the guilty many persons of his own rank: senators and matrons of the noblest extraction, and the friends or relatives of his closest friends. It appears, however, that some forty years later the emperor Valerian was persuaded of the truth of this claim, since in one of his rescripts he plainly assumes that senators, Roman knights, and ladies of quality were involved in the Christian sect. The church continued to grow in outward splendor as it lost its internal purity; and by the reign of Diocletian, the palace, the courts of justice, and even the army concealed a multitude of Christians who sought to reconcile the interests of the present life with those of the life to come.

And yet these exceptions are either too few in number, or too late in time, to remove entirely the charge of ignorance and obscurity so arrogantly cast on the first converts of Christianity. Rather than employing the fictions of later ages in our defense, it will be more prudent to convert this occasion of scandal into a subject of edification. Our serious reflections will remind us that the apostles themselves were chosen by Providence from among the fishermen of Galilee, and that the lower we set the temporal condition of the first Christians, the more reason we shall find to admire their merit and their success. It is our duty to remember diligently that the kingdom of heaven was promised to the poor in spirit, and that minds afflicted by calamity and by the contempt of mankind listen cheerfully to the divine promise of future happiness — while the fortunate, by contrast, are satisfied with the possession of this world, and the wise squander in doubt and dispute their vain superiority of reason and knowledge.

We stand in need of such reflections to console us for the loss of certain illustrious characters who might have seemed, in our eyes, the most worthy of the heavenly gift. The names of Seneca, of the elder and the younger Pliny, of Tacitus, of Plutarch, of Galen, of the slave Epictetus, and of the emperor Marcus Antoninus adorn the age in which they flourished and exalt the dignity of human nature. They filled their respective stations, in active or in contemplative life, with glory; their excellent understandings were improved by study; philosophy had purified their minds of the prejudices of popular superstition; and their days were spent in the pursuit of truth and the practice of virtue. Yet all these sages — it is a matter of no less surprise than concern — overlooked or rejected the perfection of the Christian system. Their language and their silence alike reveal their contempt for the growing sect, which in their time had spread across the Roman empire. Those among them who condescended to mention the Christians at all treated them merely as obstinate and perverse fanatics who demanded implicit submission to their mysterious doctrines without being able to produce a single argument that could engage the attention of men of sense and learning.

It is at least doubtful whether any of these philosophers ever read the apologies that the primitive Christians repeatedly published on behalf of themselves and their religion; but it is much to be regretted that such a cause was not defended by abler advocates. They expose the extravagance of polytheism with superfluous wit and eloquence. They engage our compassion by displaying the innocence and sufferings of their injured brethren. But when they set out to demonstrate the divine origin of Christianity, they lean far more heavily on the predictions that announced the Messiah's appearance than on the miracles that accompanied it. Their favorite argument might serve to edify a Christian or to convert a Jew, since both acknowledge the authority of those prophecies and both are bound, with devout reverence, to search out their meaning and their fulfillment. But this mode of persuasion loses much of its weight and influence when addressed to people who neither understand nor respect the Mosaic dispensation and the prophetic style. In the unskillful hands of Justin and the apologists who followed him, the sublime meaning of the Hebrew oracles evaporates into far-fetched types, strained conceits, and cold allegories; and even their authenticity was made suspect to an unenlightened Gentile by their mixture with pious forgeries — texts foisted on him under the names of Orpheus, Hermes, and the Sibyls as being of equal value with the genuine inspirations of Heaven. The adoption of fraud and sophistry in defense of revelation too often reminds us of the ill-judged conduct of those poets who load their *invulnerable* heroes with a useless weight of cumbersome and brittle armor.

But how shall we excuse the passive inattention of the pagan and philosophic world to those evidences which the hand of Omnipotence presented not to their reason, but to their senses? During the age of Christ, of his apostles, and of their first disciples, the doctrine they preached was confirmed by innumerable prodigies. The lame walked, the blind saw, the sick were healed, the dead were raised, demons were expelled, and the laws of Nature were frequently suspended for the benefit of the church. But the sages of Greece and Rome turned away from the awesome spectacle and, going about the ordinary business of life and study, appeared unaware of any alteration in the moral or physical government of the world. Under the reign of Tiberius, the whole earth — or at least a celebrated province of the Roman empire — was wrapped in a supernatural darkness of three hours. Even this miraculous event, which ought to have excited the wonder, the curiosity, and the devotion of mankind, passed without notice in an age of science and history. It happened during the lifetime of Seneca and the elder Pliny, who must have experienced the immediate effects of the prodigy or received the earliest reports of it. Each of these philosophers, in a laborious work, recorded all the great phenomena of Nature — earthquakes, meteors, comets, and eclipses — that his indefatigable curiosity could collect. Both alike have omitted to mention the greatest phenomenon to which the mortal eye has been witness since the creation of the globe. A distinct chapter of Pliny is devoted to eclipses of an extraordinary nature and unusual duration; but he contents himself with describing the singular failure of light that followed the murder of Caesar, when for the greater part of a year the orb of the sun appeared pale and without splendor. That season of obscurity, which surely cannot be compared with the preternatural darkness of the Passion, had already been celebrated by most of the poets and historians of that memorable age.
