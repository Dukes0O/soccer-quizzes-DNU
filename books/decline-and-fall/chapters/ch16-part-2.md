# Chapter XVI, Part 2 — Nero's Scapegoats, Domitian's Suspicions, and the Rules of Trajan

> Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*, vol. 1 (1776) · Chapter XVI (The Conduct of the Roman Government Towards the Christians, from the Reign of Nero to That of Constantine), Part 2 of 5
>

## Overview

This part opens with the most famous document in the history of the early persecutions: Tacitus's account of Nero blaming the Christians for the Great Fire of Rome and torturing them to death in his Vatican gardens — the very ground, Gibbon notes with relish, on which St. Peter's now stands. Gibbon then subjects the passage to a four-point critical examination. He defends its authenticity, but argues that Tacitus, writing some sixty years after the event, colored his account with the assumptions of Hadrian's age rather than Nero's; he ventures the conjecture that Nero's real targets should have been the Jewish zealots who followed Judas the Gaulonite, and that the innocent disciples of Jesus were confused with them under the shared label "Galilaeans." Whatever the truth of that guess, he insists the persecution was confined to the city of Rome and never made Christian belief itself a crime.

The narrative then follows the sect's legal exposure through the Flavian period: the humiliating Jewish poll tax after the destruction of the Temple, which caught Christians in its net; the touching examination of the grandsons of St. Jude, poor farmers dismissed by the authorities "with compassion and contempt"; and Domitian's execution of his own cousin Flavius Clemens on the charge of "atheism and Jewish manners" — the thin evidence on which the church built its "second persecution."

The centerpiece of the part is the correspondence between the younger Pliny, governor of Bithynia, and the emperor Trajan (AD 112). From Pliny's confessed ignorance of how to try Christians, Gibbon extracts a crucial negative fact: no general anti-Christian laws yet existed. Trajan's reply established two rules — punish the convicted, but do not seek Christians out, and never accept anonymous accusations — which Gibbon presents as genuine protections that made formal prosecution rare and risky. The real danger came instead from mob fury at the public games, which the edicts of Hadrian and Antoninus Pius refused to admit as legal evidence.

Finally, Gibbon turns to what happened after conviction. The magistrates, he argues, were humane men who used torture perversely to extract denial rather than confession, preferred lesser penalties to death, and executed only bishops at one extreme and slaves at the other. Against the later legends of a "formidable army of martyrs" — and the monks' prurient inventions about assaults on Christian virgins — he sets Origen's testimony that the martyrs were few, and Dionysius's count of only seventeen at Alexandria under Decius. The part closes by introducing Cyprian of Carthage, whose prudent flight from the Decian persecution, and eventual death eight years later, will supply the case study that follows.

## Key Concepts

- **The authenticity of Tacitus's account** — Gibbon accepts both the fact of Nero's persecution (confirmed by Suetonius) and the genuineness of the Tacitus passage, precisely because it is hostile to the Christians and attributes no miracles to them — no pious forger would have written it.
- **Anachronism in Tacitus** — writing under Hadrian about events sixty years past, Tacitus described the Christians according to the knowledge and prejudices of his own day, not Nero's.
- **The Galilaean confusion** — Gibbon's conjecture (offered as no more than that) that two utterly opposite groups, the peaceful disciples of Jesus and the violent zealots of Judas the Gaulonite, were lumped together as "Galilaeans," letting the guilt of the extinct rebels fall on the surviving Christians.
- **The Jewish tax as a trap** — the capitation tax diverted from the Temple of Jerusalem to the Capitoline Jupiter caught Christians who had long sheltered "under the shade of the synagogue," since circumcision, not doctrine, was the magistrate's test.
- **Domitian's "second persecution"** — the execution of Flavius Clemens and exile of Domitilla for "atheism and Jewish manners," a charge Gibbon reads as probably meaning Christianity, but a persecution brief, dynastic in motive, and reversed under Nerva.
- **The Pliny–Trajan correspondence** — proof by negative inference that in AD 112 no general laws or senatorial decrees against the Christians existed; Trajan's rescript punished the convicted but forbade inquisition and anonymous charges.
- **Mob violence vs. imperial legality** — the real engine of persecution was the crowd at the games ("if the Tiber had, or if the Nile had not, risen beyond its banks"), which the emperors' edicts refused to recognize as evidence.
- **The scarcity of martyrs** — torture aimed at recantation, magistrates preferred milder penalties, and the authentic record (Origen; Dionysius's ten men and seven women at Alexandria) demolishes the "formidable army of martyrs" of later legend.

## Modern Translation

> "With this in view," Tacitus continues, "he inflicted the most exquisite tortures on those men who, under the common name of Christians, were already branded with deserved infamy. They took their name and origin from Christ, who, in the reign of Tiberius, had been put to death by sentence of the procurator Pontius Pilate. For a while this dreadful superstition was checked; but it broke out again, and spread not only over Judea, the original home of this pernicious sect, but even into Rome itself, the common refuge that receives and protects everything impure and everything atrocious. The confessions of those who were seized revealed a great multitude of their accomplices, and they were all convicted — not so much for the crime of setting fire to the city as for their hatred of the human race. They died in torments, and their torments were sharpened by insult and mockery. Some were nailed to crosses; others were sewn into the skins of wild beasts and exposed to the fury of dogs; others still, smeared with combustible materials, were used as torches to light up the darkness of the night. Nero's gardens were given over to this melancholy spectacle, which came with a horse race and was honored by the presence of the emperor himself, who mingled with the crowd in the dress and posture of a charioteer. The guilt of the Christians did indeed deserve the most exemplary punishment; but public abhorrence turned into pity, from the feeling that these unhappy wretches were being sacrificed not so much to the public welfare as to the cruelty of a jealous tyrant."

Anyone who surveys the revolutions of human affairs with a curious eye may observe that the gardens and circus of Nero on the Vatican, once polluted with the blood of the first Christians, have been made still more famous by the triumph — and by the abuse — of the persecuted religion. On that same spot a temple far surpassing the ancient glories of the Capitol has since been raised by the Christian pontiffs, who, deriving their claim to universal dominion from a humble fisherman of Galilee, have succeeded to the throne of the Caesars, dictated laws to the barbarian conquerors of Rome, and extended their spiritual jurisdiction from the coast of the Baltic to the shores of the Pacific Ocean.

But it would be wrong to leave this account of Nero's persecution without making a few observations that may clear away the difficulties that surround it, and throw some light on the later history of the church.

1. Even the most skeptical criticism is forced to respect both the truth of this extraordinary event and the integrity of this celebrated passage of Tacitus. The fact itself is confirmed by the diligent and accurate Suetonius, who mentions the punishment Nero inflicted on the Christians — a sect of men, as he puts it, who had embraced a new and criminal superstition. The genuineness of the passage can be proved by the agreement of the oldest manuscripts; by the inimitable character of Tacitus's style; by his reputation, which protected his text from the interpolations of pious fraud; and by the drift of his narrative, which accused the first Christians of the most atrocious crimes without ever hinting that they possessed any miraculous, or even magical, powers beyond the rest of mankind. 2. Although Tacitus was probably born a few years before the fire of Rome, he could know of an event that happened in his infancy only from reading and conversation. Before offering himself to the public, he calmly waited until his genius had reached full maturity, and he was more than forty years old when a grateful regard for the memory of the virtuous Agricola drew from him the earliest of those historical works that will delight and instruct the most distant posterity. After trying his strength in the life of Agricola and the description of Germany, he conceived, and at length carried out, a more ambitious project: a history of Rome, in thirty books, from the fall of Nero to the accession of Nerva. The administration of Nerva ushered in an age of justice and decency, which Tacitus had intended as the occupation of his old age; but when he looked more closely at his subject — judging, perhaps, that it was a more honorable, or at least a less risky, task to record the vices of dead tyrants than to celebrate the virtues of a reigning monarch — he chose instead to relate, in the form of annals, the actions of the four immediate successors of Augustus. To collect, arrange, and polish the record of eighty years into an immortal work, every sentence of which is loaded with the deepest observation and the most vivid imagery, was an undertaking large enough to occupy even the genius of Tacitus for the greater part of his life. In the last years of Trajan's reign, while that victorious monarch was extending the power of Rome beyond its ancient limits, the historian was describing, in the second and fourth books of his annals, the tyranny of Tiberius; and Hadrian must already have succeeded to the throne before Tacitus, in the regular progress of his work, could reach the fire of the capital and Nero's cruelty toward the unfortunate Christians. At a distance of sixty years it was the annalist's duty to adopt the narratives of contemporaries; but it was natural for the philosopher to indulge himself in describing the origin, the progress, and the character of the new sect — not so much according to the knowledge or the prejudices of the age of Nero as according to those of the age of Hadrian. 3. Tacitus very often trusts to the curiosity or reflection of his readers to supply the intermediate circumstances and ideas that his extreme conciseness leads him to suppress. We may therefore allow ourselves to imagine some probable cause that could have directed Nero's cruelty against the Christians of Rome, whose obscurity, no less than their innocence, ought to have shielded them from his anger and even from his notice. The Jews, who were numerous in the capital and oppressed in their own country, were a far more plausible object for the suspicions of emperor and people alike; nor did it seem unlikely that a conquered nation, which had already shown its hatred of the Roman yoke, might resort to the most atrocious means of gratifying its implacable revenge. But the Jews had very powerful advocates in the palace, and even in the heart of the tyrant: his wife and mistress, the beautiful Poppaea, and a favorite actor of the race of Abraham, who had already used their influence on behalf of that vulnerable people. In their place some other victims had to be offered up; and it could easily be suggested that although the genuine followers of Moses were innocent of the fire of Rome, there had arisen among them a new and pernicious sect of Galilaeans capable of the most horrid crimes. Under the name of Galilaeans, two kinds of men were confused who were the most opposite to each other in manners and principles: the disciples who had embraced the faith of Jesus of Nazareth, and the zealots who had followed the standard of Judas the Gaulonite. The former were the friends of the human race; the latter were its enemies; and the only resemblance between them lay in the same inflexible constancy which, in defense of their cause, made them insensible to death and torture. The followers of Judas, who drove their countrymen into rebellion, were soon buried under the ruins of Jerusalem, while those of Jesus, known by the more celebrated name of Christians, spread themselves across the Roman empire. How natural it was for Tacitus, writing in the time of Hadrian, to attach to the Christians the guilt and the sufferings he might, with far greater truth and justice, have attributed to a sect whose odious memory was by then almost extinct! 4. Whatever one thinks of this conjecture (and it is no more than a conjecture), it is evident that the effect, as well as the cause, of Nero's persecution was confined to the walls of Rome; that the religious beliefs of the Galilaeans, or Christians, were never made a subject of punishment or even of inquiry; and that, since the memory of their sufferings was long associated with the memory of cruelty and injustice, later princes were the more inclined to spare a sect that had been oppressed by a tyrant whose rage had usually been directed against virtue and innocence.

It is somewhat remarkable that the flames of war consumed, at almost the same moment, the temple of Jerusalem and the Capitol of Rome; and it appears no less strange that the tribute which devotion had destined for the former should have been converted, by the power of a conquering enemy, to restoring and adorning the splendor of the latter. The emperors levied a general poll tax on the Jewish people; and although the sum assessed on each individual was trifling, the purpose for which it was designed, and the severity with which it was collected, were felt as an intolerable grievance. Since the revenue officers stretched their unjust claim to many people who were strangers to the blood and religion of the Jews, it was impossible for the Christians — who had so often sheltered under the shade of the synagogue — to escape this rapacious form of persecution. Anxious as they were to avoid the slightest taint of idolatry, their conscience forbade them to contribute to the honor of that demon who had assumed the character of the Capitoline Jupiter. And since a very numerous, though declining, party among the Christians still adhered to the law of Moses, their efforts to disguise their Jewish origin were exposed by the decisive test of circumcision; nor were the Roman magistrates at leisure to inquire into the finer points of their religious opinions. Among the Christians brought before the tribunal of the emperor — or, as seems more probable, before that of the procurator of Judea — two persons are said to have appeared whose descent was more truly noble than that of the greatest monarchs. These were the grandsons of St. Jude the apostle, himself the brother of Jesus Christ. Their natural claim to the throne of David might perhaps have attracted the respect of the people and excited the jealousy of the governor; but the meanness of their clothing and the simplicity of their answers soon convinced him that they were neither eager nor able to disturb the peace of the Roman empire. They frankly confessed their royal origin and their close relation to the Messiah; but they disclaimed any worldly ambitions, and declared that his kingdom, which they devoutly awaited, was of a purely spiritual and angelic nature. When they were questioned about their fortune and their occupation, they showed hands hardened by daily labor, and stated that their whole livelihood came from cultivating a farm near the village of Cocaba, about twenty-four English acres in extent and worth nine thousand drachms, or three hundred pounds sterling. The grandsons of St. Jude were dismissed with compassion and contempt.

But although the obscurity of the house of David might protect its members from a tyrant's suspicions, the present greatness of his own family alarmed the timid temper of Domitian, which could be soothed only by the blood of those Romans he feared, or hated, or esteemed. **AD 95** Of the two sons of his uncle Flavius Sabinus, the elder was soon convicted of treasonable designs; the younger, who bore the name of Flavius Clemens, owed his safety to his lack of courage and ability. For a long time the emperor honored so harmless a kinsman with his favor and protection, gave him his own niece Domitilla in marriage, adopted the children of that union as heirs to the succession, and invested their father with the honors of the consulship. But Clemens had barely finished his year in office when, on a slight pretext, he was condemned and executed; Domitilla was banished to a desolate island off the coast of Campania; and sentences of death or confiscation were pronounced against a great number of persons implicated in the same accusation. The guilt laid to their charge was that of *atheism* and *Jewish manners* — a strange pairing of ideas which cannot with any propriety be applied to anyone but the Christians, as they were dimly and imperfectly perceived by the magistrates and writers of that period. On the strength of this plausible interpretation — and accepting a little too eagerly the suspicions of a tyrant as proof of their honorable crime — the church has placed both Clemens and Domitilla among its first martyrs, and has branded Domitian's cruelty with the name of the second persecution. But this persecution (if it deserves the name) did not last long. A few months after the death of Clemens and the banishment of Domitilla, Stephen, a freedman of hers who had enjoyed the favor, but had certainly not embraced the faith, of his mistress, assassinated the emperor in his palace. The memory of Domitian was condemned by the senate; his acts were rescinded; his exiles were recalled; and under the gentle administration of Nerva, while the innocent were restored to their rank and fortunes, even the most guilty either obtained pardon or escaped punishment.

II. About ten years later, in the reign of Trajan, the younger Pliny was entrusted by his friend and master with the government of Bithynia and Pontus. He soon found himself unable to determine by what rule of justice or of law he should carry out an office so repugnant to his humane character. Pliny had never taken part in any judicial proceedings against the Christians, with whose very name he seems barely acquainted; he was entirely uninformed about the nature of their guilt, the method of convicting them, or the proper degree of their punishment. In this perplexity he fell back on his usual expedient of submitting to Trajan's wisdom an impartial and, in some respects, favorable account of the new superstition, and asking the emperor to condescend to resolve his doubts and instruct his ignorance. Pliny's life had been spent in acquiring learning and in the business of the world. From the age of nineteen he had pleaded with distinction in the courts of Rome, held a seat in the senate, been invested with the honors of the consulship, and formed extensive connections with every class of men, in Italy and in the provinces alike. From *his* ignorance, therefore, we can draw some useful information. We may be confident that when he accepted the government of Bithynia there were no general laws or senatorial decrees in force against the Christians; that neither Trajan nor any of his virtuous predecessors, whose edicts had been absorbed into the civil and criminal law, had publicly declared their intentions concerning the new sect; and that whatever proceedings had previously been taken against the Christians, none carried enough weight and authority to establish a precedent for the conduct of a Roman magistrate.

**AD 112** Trajan's answer, to which the Christians of the following century frequently appealed, shows as much regard for justice and humanity as could be reconciled with his mistaken notions of religious policy. Instead of displaying the implacable zeal of an inquisitor, eager to hunt down the smallest particles of heresy and exulting in the number of his victims, the emperor shows far more concern to protect the security of the innocent than to prevent the escape of the guilty. He admitted the difficulty of fixing any general rule; but he laid down two salutary principles which often brought relief and support to the distressed Christians. Though he directs the magistrates to punish persons who are legally convicted, he forbids them — with a very humane inconsistency — from making any inquiries after the supposed criminals. Nor was the magistrate permitted to act on every kind of information. Anonymous charges the emperor rejected as too repugnant to the equity of his government; and for the conviction of anyone charged with the guilt of Christianity, he strictly required the positive evidence of a fair and open accuser. It is likely, too, that those who took on so odious a role were obliged to state the grounds of their suspicions, to specify — as to both time and place — the secret assemblies their Christian adversary had attended, and to disclose a great number of details that were guarded with the most vigilant jealousy from the eyes of outsiders. If accusers succeeded in their prosecution, they exposed themselves to the resentment of a considerable and active party, to the censure of the more liberal part of mankind, and to the disgrace which, in every age and country, has attached to the character of an informer. If, on the other hand, they failed to prove their case, they incurred the severe — perhaps capital — penalty which, under a law published by the emperor Hadrian, fell on those who falsely accused their fellow citizens of the crime of Christianity. The violence of personal or superstitious hatred might sometimes overcome the natural fear of disgrace and danger; but it can hardly be imagined that accusations of so unpromising a kind were undertaken either lightly or often by the pagan subjects of the Roman empire.

The expedient that was used to evade the caution of the laws is itself sufficient proof of how effectively they frustrated the malicious designs of private spite or superstitious zeal. In a large and turbulent crowd, the restraints of fear and shame, so powerful over the minds of individuals, lose most of their force. The pious Christian, whether he longed to obtain the glory of martyrdom or to escape it, awaited the regular return of the public games and festivals with impatience or with terror. On those occasions the inhabitants of the great cities of the empire gathered in the circus or the theater, where every feature of the place, as of the ceremony, served to inflame their devotion and to extinguish their humanity. While the crowds of spectators — crowned with garlands, perfumed with incense, purified with the blood of victims, and surrounded by the altars and statues of their guardian deities — gave themselves up to pleasures they regarded as an essential part of their religious worship, they remembered that the Christians alone abhorred the gods of mankind, and by their absence and their gloom on these solemn festivals seemed either to insult or to lament the public happiness. If the empire had suffered any recent calamity — a plague, a famine, an unsuccessful war; if the Tiber had risen beyond its banks, or if the Nile had not; if the earth had shaken, or the temperate order of the seasons had been disturbed — the superstitious pagans were convinced that the crimes and impiety of the Christians, spared as they were by the excessive leniency of the government, had at last provoked divine justice. It was not among a lawless and inflamed populace that the forms of legal procedure could be observed; it was not in an amphitheater stained with the blood of wild beasts and gladiators that the voice of compassion could be heard. The impatient clamor of the multitude denounced the Christians as the enemies of gods and men, doomed them to the severest tortures, and, venturing to name some of the most distinguished of the new sectaries, demanded with irresistible vehemence that they be instantly seized and thrown to the lions. The provincial governors and magistrates who presided over the public spectacles were usually inclined to gratify the wishes, and appease the rage, of the people by sacrificing a few unpopular victims. But the wisdom of the emperors protected the church from the danger of these tumultuous clamors and irregular accusations, which they justly condemned as repugnant both to the firmness and to the equity of their administration. The edicts of Hadrian and of Antoninus Pius expressly declared that the voice of the multitude should never be admitted as legal evidence to convict or punish those unfortunate persons who had embraced the enthusiasm of the Christians.

III. Punishment was not the inevitable consequence of conviction; and the Christians whose guilt was most clearly established by the testimony of witnesses, or even by their own voluntary confession, still held in their own hands the choice of life or death. It was not so much the past offense as the present resistance that provoked the magistrate's indignation. He was persuaded that he was offering them an easy pardon: if they consented to cast a few grains of incense on the altar, they were dismissed from the tribunal in safety and with applause. It was considered the duty of a humane judge to try to reclaim, rather than to punish, these deluded enthusiasts. Adapting his tone to the age, the sex, or the situation of the prisoners, he would often stoop to set before their eyes every consideration that could make life more attractive or death more terrible, and to urge — indeed, to beg — them to show some compassion for themselves, their families, and their friends. If threats and persuasion failed, he often resorted to violence: the scourge and the rack were called in to make up for the deficiencies of argument, and every art of cruelty was employed to subdue an obstinacy so inflexible and, as it appeared to the pagans, so criminal. The ancient apologists of Christianity have censured, with equal truth and severity, the irregular conduct of their persecutors, who, contrary to every principle of judicial procedure, employed torture to obtain not a confession of the crime under investigation, but a denial of it. The monks of later ages, who in their peaceful solitudes amused themselves by multiplying and varying the deaths and sufferings of the primitive martyrs, have often invented torments of a far more refined and ingenious kind. In particular, it has pleased them to suppose that the zeal of the Roman magistrates, disdaining every consideration of moral virtue or public decency, tried to seduce those whom they could not conquer, and ordered the most brutal violence against those whom they found it impossible to seduce. It is related that pious women, prepared to despise death, were sometimes condemned to a harder trial, and made to decide whether they set a higher value on their religion or on their chastity. The young men to whose lustful embraces they were surrendered received a solemn exhortation from the judge to exert their most strenuous efforts to uphold the honor of Venus against the impious virgin who refused to burn incense on her altars. Their violence, however, was usually foiled, and the timely intervention of some miraculous power preserved the chaste brides of Christ from the dishonor of even an involuntary defeat. We should not fail to note, however, that the older and more authentic records of the church are seldom stained with these extravagant and indecent fictions.

The total disregard of truth and probability in the depiction of these primitive martyrdoms arose from a very natural mistake. The church writers of the fourth and fifth centuries attributed to the magistrates of Rome the same implacable and unrelenting zeal that filled their own breasts against the heretics or idolaters of their own day. It is not improbable that some of those raised to the dignities of the empire had absorbed the prejudices of the populace, and that the cruel disposition of others was occasionally sharpened by greed or personal resentment. But it is certain — and we may appeal to the grateful admissions of the first Christians themselves — that most of the magistrates who exercised the authority of the emperor or the senate in the provinces, and to whose hands alone the power of life and death was entrusted, behaved like men of polished manners and liberal education, who respected the rules of justice and were familiar with the precepts of philosophy. They frequently declined the odious task of persecution, dismissed the charge with contempt, or suggested to the accused Christian some legal evasion by which he might slip past the severity of the laws. Whenever they held discretionary power, they used it far less to oppress than to relieve and benefit the afflicted church. They were far from condemning all the Christians accused before their tribunals, and very far from putting to death all those convicted of an obstinate attachment to the new superstition. Contenting themselves, for the most part, with the milder punishments of imprisonment, exile, or slavery in the mines, they left the unhappy victims of their justice some reason to hope that a happy event — the accession, the marriage, or the triumph of an emperor — might soon restore them, by a general pardon, to their former condition. The martyrs whom the Roman magistrates sent to immediate execution appear to have been chosen from the two opposite extremes. They were either bishops and presbyters — the persons most distinguished among the Christians by rank and influence, whose example might strike terror into the whole sect — or else they were the lowest and most abject among them, particularly slaves, whose lives were counted of little value and whose sufferings the ancients viewed with a too careless indifference. The learned Origen, who from experience as well as reading was intimately acquainted with the history of the Christians, declares in the most explicit terms that the number of martyrs was very small. His authority alone would be enough to annihilate that formidable army of martyrs whose relics, drawn for the most part from the catacombs of Rome, have stocked so many churches, and whose marvelous exploits have filled so many volumes of holy romance. But Origen's general assertion can be explained and confirmed by the particular testimony of his friend Dionysius, who, in the immense city of Alexandria, and under the rigorous persecution of Decius, counts only ten men and seven women who suffered for the profession of the Christian name.

During this same period of persecution, the zealous, eloquent, and ambitious Cyprian governed the church not only of Carthage but of all Africa. He possessed every quality that could win the reverence of the faithful or provoke the suspicion and resentment of the pagan magistrates. His character, no less than his station, seemed to mark out that holy prelate as the most conspicuous target of envy and danger. Yet the actual experience of Cyprian's life is enough to prove that our imagination has exaggerated the perils of a Christian bishop's position: the dangers he faced were less pressing than those which worldly ambition is always prepared to run in the pursuit of honors. In the space of the ten years during which the bishop of Carthage guided the councils of the African church by his authority and eloquence, four Roman emperors, with their families, their favorites, and their followers, perished by the sword. It was only in the third year of his administration that he had reason, for a few months, to fear the severe edicts of Decius, the vigilance of the magistrate, and the clamor of the multitude, who loudly demanded that Cyprian, the leader of the Christians, be thrown to the lions. Prudence suggested the necessity of a temporary retreat, and the voice of prudence was obeyed. He withdrew into an obscure hiding place, from which he could keep up a constant correspondence with the clergy and people of Carthage; and by concealing himself until the storm had passed, he preserved his life without giving up either his power or his reputation. His extreme caution did not, however, escape the censure of the stricter Christians, who lamented it, or the reproaches of his personal enemies, who mocked it, as a cowardly and criminal desertion of the most sacred of duties. The reasons offered in his defense were the propriety of preserving himself for the future needs of the church, the example of several holy bishops, and the divine warnings which, as he himself declares, he frequently received in visions and ecstasies. But his best apology is the cheerful resolution with which, about eight years later, he suffered death in the cause of religion. The authentic history of his martyrdom has been recorded with unusual candor and impartiality. A short summary of its most important circumstances will therefore give the clearest picture of the spirit, and of the forms, of the Roman persecutions.
