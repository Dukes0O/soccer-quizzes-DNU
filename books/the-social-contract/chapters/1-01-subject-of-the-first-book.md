# Book I, Chapter I — Subject of the First Book

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book I, Chapter I
>
> [Book index](../README.md) · [I.II The First Societies →](1-02-the-first-societies.md)

## Overview

Book I opens with a short personal note in which Rousseau states his whole project: to ask whether civil society can rest on a rule of government that is both dependable and legitimate, "taking men as they are and laws as they might be" — that is, without pretending human nature can be perfected, but without settling for whatever institutions happen to exist. He disarms the objection that only rulers should write about politics: he writes precisely because he is neither prince nor legislator, and because as a citizen of a free state with a vote, studying public affairs is his duty.

The chapter proper then delivers the book's most famous sentence — "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains" — and immediately narrows the question. Rousseau will not try to explain historically how freedom was lost; he asks instead what could make political subjection *legitimate*. He rules out force as an answer: if might made right, then a people that breaks its chains would be equally within its rights, and the whole notion of legitimacy would collapse into whoever happens to be stronger. Since the social order is nonetheless the foundation of all other rights, and since it cannot come from nature, it must rest on conventions — agreements. Proving that claim is the announced task of the chapters that follow.

## Key Concepts

- **Legitimacy versus fact** — Rousseau brackets the historical question (how did men come to be "in chains"?) and pursues the normative one: what could justify political authority at all.
- **Force cannot found right** — an argument by symmetry: if compulsion justified obedience, then successful rebellion would be justified by the very same "right," so force yields no genuine obligation either way.
- **The social order as the basis of all rights** — political society is not one right among others but the sacred foundation on which every other right depends.
- **Convention as the source of the social order** — because this founding right does not come from nature, it can only arise from agreements among men; this sets up the social contract itself.
- **The citizen's duty to inquire** — membership in the Sovereign (the collective citizen body) with a right to vote carries an obligation to study public affairs, however small one's individual influence.

## Archaic Terms

Words and usages that have fallen out of use or shifted meaning (in Cole's translation or Rousseau's terminology).

| Term | Meaning here |
|---|---|
| rule of administration | principle or standard of government |
| the Sovereign | Rousseau's technical term: the whole body of citizens acting as the supreme political authority — not a monarch |
| what wants doing | what needs to be done |
| hold my peace | stay silent |
| yoke | the burden of subjection; domination by a master |
| right (as in "a sacred right") | a rightful entitlement or lawful foundation, not merely a moral claim |
| conventions | deliberate agreements or covenants among people — not customs or habits |

## Modern Translation

#### Book I — Introductory Note

I want to ask whether, in the civil order, there can be any rule of government that is both reliable and legitimate, taking men as they are and laws as they might be. In this inquiry I will try always to join what right permits with what interest requires, so that justice and utility are never pulled apart.

I take up my task without arguing for the importance of the subject. I will be asked whether I am a prince or a legislator, that I should write about politics. I answer that I am neither — and that is exactly why I write. If I were a prince or a legislator, I would not waste my time saying what needs to be done; I would do it, or keep quiet.

Since I was born a citizen of a free state and a member of the Sovereign, I feel that however feeble my voice's influence on public affairs may be, the right to vote on them makes it my duty to study them. And whenever I reflect on governments, I am happy to find that my inquiries always give me new reasons to love the government of my own country.

#### Chapter I

Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains. One man thinks himself the master of others, and yet remains a greater slave than they are. How did this change come about? I do not know. What can make it legitimate? That question I think I can answer.

If I considered only force, and the effects that flow from it, I would say: "As long as a people is compelled to obey, and does obey, it does well; as soon as it can shake off the yoke, and does shake it off, it does even better. For in recovering its liberty by the same right that was used to take it away, either the people is justified in taking it back, or there was no justification for taking it away in the first place." But the social order is a sacred right that is the basis of all other rights. Yet this right does not come from nature, and so it must be founded on conventions. Before coming to those, I have to prove what I have just asserted.
