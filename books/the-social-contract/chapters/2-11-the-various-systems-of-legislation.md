# Book II, Chapter XI — The Various Systems of Legislation

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter XI
>
> [← II.X The People, Continued Again](2-10-the-people-continued-2.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.XII The Division of the Laws →](2-12-the-division-of-the-laws.md)

## Overview

Rousseau asks what every system of legislation should ultimately aim at, and answers: the greatest good of all, which comes down to two things — liberty and equality. Liberty, because any private dependence of one person on another subtracts force from the State; equality, because liberty cannot survive without it. He is careful to define equality not as identical wealth and power for everyone, but as limits: power must never be strong enough to become violence, and no citizen should be rich enough to buy another or poor enough to have to sell himself. To the objection that such equality is a fantasy, he gives one of the book's sharpest replies: it is precisely because circumstances constantly work to destroy equality that legislation must constantly work to maintain it.

The second half of the chapter qualifies these universal goals. Every country must adapt them to its own terrain, climate, and national character: barren or overcrowded land calls for industry and trade; fertile, underpopulated land calls for agriculture; a good coastline calls for shipping and commerce; a rocky, harborless coast is better left to a simple fishing life. Each nation has something distinctive that should shape its laws — religion for the Jews and Arabs, letters for Athens, commerce for Carthage and Tyre, war for Sparta, virtue for Rome — and Rousseau credits Montesquieu (*The Spirit of the Laws*) with showing how a legislator steers a constitution toward such objects. A constitution lasts only when law works with these natural tendencies rather than against them; a legislator who fights nature will see his laws lose their grip until the State is destroyed or transformed and nature reclaims her sway.

## Key Concepts

- **Liberty and equality as the twin ends of legislation** — every good system of law reduces to these two objects; liberty because private dependence weakens the State, equality because liberty cannot exist without it.
- **Equality as bounded inequality** — not identical wealth and power for all, but power that never becomes violence and wealth gaps never wide enough for one citizen to buy another or force another to sell himself.
- **Legislation as counterforce** — since the pressure of circumstances always tends to destroy equality, the force of law must always push back to preserve it; the inevitability of abuse is an argument for regulation, not against it.
- **Adapting laws to local conditions** — the universal ends of legislation must be tailored to each country's soil, geography, population, and character, giving each nation a system of institutions best not in itself but for that particular State.
- **Working with nature, not against it** — a constitution is solid only when laws confirm and accompany the natural relations of the country; a legislator who chooses a principle contrary to circumstances (servitude where they point to liberty, riches where they point to population, peace where they point to conquest) dooms the State to unrest.

## Modern Translation

If we ask what exactly the greatest good of all consists in — the good that should be the goal of every system of legislation — we will find it comes down to two main objects: liberty and equality. Liberty, because every private dependence of one person on another means that much force taken away from the body of the State; and equality, because liberty cannot exist without it.

I have already defined civil liberty. By equality we should understand, not that everyone must have exactly the same degree of power and wealth, but that power should never be great enough to become violence, and should only ever be exercised by virtue of rank and law; and that, as for wealth, no citizen should ever be rich enough to buy another, and none poor enough to be forced to sell himself. This implies moderation in property and influence on the part of the great, and moderation in greed and covetousness on the part of ordinary people.

> **Rousseau's note:** If you want to give the State stability, bring the two extremes as close together as possible; tolerate neither rich men nor beggars. These two conditions, which naturally go together, are equally fatal to the common good: from one come the friends of tyranny, from the other come tyrants. It is always between them that public liberty is put up for auction — one buys it, and the other sells it.

Such equality, we are told, is an impractical ideal that cannot actually exist. But even if its abuse is inevitable, does it follow that we shouldn't at least regulate it? It is precisely because the force of circumstances is always working to destroy equality that the force of legislation should always be working to maintain it.

But these general aims of every good legislative system need to be adjusted in each country to fit the local situation and the character of the inhabitants; and those circumstances should determine, in each case, the particular system of institutions that is best — best not perhaps in itself, but for the State it is meant for. If, for example, the soil is barren and unproductive, or the land too crowded for its population, the people should turn to industry and the crafts, and trade what they produce for the goods they lack. If, on the other hand, a people lives on rich plains and fertile slopes, or has good land but too few inhabitants, it should devote all its attention to agriculture, which makes populations grow, and should drive out the crafts, which would only depopulate the country by clustering its few inhabitants in a handful of places. If a nation occupies a long and convenient coastline, let it cover the sea with ships and cultivate commerce and navigation: its life will be short and glorious. If the sea along its coasts washes nothing but nearly inaccessible rocks, let it stay primitive and live on fish: its life will be quieter, perhaps better, and certainly happier. In short, beyond the principles common to all, every nation contains something of its own that gives those principles a particular application and makes its legislation distinctively its own. That is why, long ago among the Jews and more recently among the Arabs, the chief object was religion; among the Athenians, letters; at Carthage and Tyre, commerce; at Rhodes, shipping; at Sparta, war; at Rome, virtue. The author of *The Spirit of the Laws* has shown, with many examples, the art by which the legislator directs the constitution toward each of these objects.

> **Rousseau's note:** "Any branch of foreign commerce," says M. d'Argenson, "creates on the whole only an apparent advantage for the kingdom as a whole. It may enrich a few individuals, or even a few towns, but the nation as a whole gains nothing from it, and the people are no better off."

What makes the constitution of a State truly solid and lasting is keeping to what is fitting — so that natural relations and the laws agree on every point, and law serves only, so to speak, to secure, accompany, and correct them. But if the legislator gets his object wrong and adopts a principle other than the one circumstances naturally point to — if his principle tends toward servitude while circumstances tend toward liberty, or toward riches while they tend toward a growing population, or toward peace while they tend toward conquest — then the laws will gradually lose their influence, the constitution will deteriorate, and the State will know no rest from trouble until it is either destroyed or transformed, and nature has resumed her invincible sway.
