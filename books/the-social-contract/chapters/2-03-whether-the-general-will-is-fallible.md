# Book II, Chapter III — Whether the General Will Is Fallible

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter III
>
> [← II.II That Sovereignty Is Indivisible](2-02-that-sovereignty-is-indivisible.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.IV The Limits of the Sovereign Power →](2-04-the-limits-of-the-sovereign-power.md)

## Overview

Having established that sovereignty is inalienable and indivisible, Rousseau now confronts an obvious objection: if the general will is always right, why do peoples so often decide badly? His answer turns on a distinction between the rightness of the will and the correctness of its judgments. The general will always aims at the common good, but the people can be mistaken about where that good lies — the people is never corrupted, but it is often deceived.

This chapter contains the book's crucial distinction between the *general will* and the *will of all*. The will of all is merely the arithmetic sum of private wills, each looking to its own interest; the general will looks only to the common interest, and emerges when the self-canceling pluses and minuses of private interest are stripped away. From this Rousseau draws a striking conclusion about political sociology: what corrupts public deliberation is not disagreement among individual citizens but the formation of factions. When partial associations form inside the State, each votes as a bloc; there are then fewer effective "wills" in play, the result is less general, and if one faction grows large enough to dominate, the general will disappears entirely and a merely particular opinion wins. The remedies are either to prevent partial societies altogether, as Lycurgus did at Sparta, or — failing that — to multiply them and keep them equal, as Solon, Numa, and Servius did.

## Key Concepts

- **General will vs. will of all** — the chapter's central distinction: the general will considers only the common interest, while the will of all is just the sum of particular wills, each considering private interest; remove the mutually canceling private differences and the general will remains.
- **Deceived, not corrupted** — the people always wills its own good but does not always see it; bad public decisions come from error and manipulation, never from a corruption of the general will itself.
- **Factions (partial associations)** — groups formed within the State whose will is general with respect to their own members but particular with respect to the State; they reduce the number of effective votes and make the outcome less general.
- **The dominant faction** — when one association prevails over all the rest, the outcome is no longer a sum of many small differences but a single difference: no general will at all, only a particular opinion that has won.
- **Two remedies** — either allow no partial societies within the State and let each citizen judge for himself (Lycurgus's system), or, if such societies must exist, multiply them and keep them equal (Solon, Numa, Servius).

## Modern Translation

From what has gone before, it follows that the general will is always right and always tends toward the public good. But it does not follow that the people's decisions are always equally sound. We always will our own good, but we do not always see what it is. The people is never corrupted, but it is often deceived — and only then does it seem to will what is bad.

There is often a great difference between the will of all and the general will. The general will considers only the common interest; the will of all takes private interest into account, and is nothing more than a sum of particular wills. But take away from these same wills the pluses and minuses that cancel one another out, and what remains as the sum of the differences is the general will.

> **Rousseau's note:** "Every interest," says the Marquis d'Argenson, "has different principles. Agreement between two particular interests is formed in opposition to a third." He might have added that agreement among all interests is formed in opposition to the interest of each. If there were no differing interests, the common interest would scarcely be felt, since it would meet no obstacle: everything would run of its own accord, and politics would cease to be an art.

If, when an adequately informed people deliberates, the citizens had no communication with one another, the grand total of the many small differences would always yield the general will, and the decision would always be good. But when factions arise — partial associations formed at the expense of the great association — the will of each of these groups becomes general in relation to its own members while remaining particular in relation to the State. It can then be said that there are no longer as many votes as there are men, but only as many as there are associations. The differences become fewer and yield a less general result. Finally, when one of these associations grows so large that it prevails over all the rest, the result is no longer a sum of small differences but a single difference; then there is no longer a general will at all, and the opinion that prevails is purely particular.

If the general will is to be able to express itself, then, it is essential that there be no partial society within the State, and that each citizen think his own thoughts — which was in fact the sublime and unique system established by the great Lycurgus. But if partial societies do exist, it is best to have as many of them as possible and to prevent them from being unequal, as Solon, Numa, and Servius did. These precautions are the only ones that can guarantee that the general will is always enlightened and that the people never deceives itself.

> **Rousseau's note:** "There are in fact," says Machiavelli, "some divisions that harm a Republic and some that benefit it. Harmful are those that come with sects and parties; beneficial are those attended by neither. Since, then, the founder of a Republic cannot prevent enmities from arising, he ought at least to prevent them from growing into sects" (*History of Florence*, Book VII). Rousseau quotes the passage in the original Italian.
