# Book II, Chapter XII — The Division of the Laws

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter XII
>
> [← II.XI The Various Systems of Legislation](2-11-the-various-systems-of-legislation.md) · [Book index](../README.md)

## Overview

Closing Book II, Rousseau classifies the laws according to the relations they regulate within the State. Political (or fundamental) laws govern the relation of the whole body to itself — of the Sovereign to the State; civil laws govern the relations of the members to one another and to the body as a whole; criminal laws govern the relation between disobedience and its penalty, and are really less a separate class of law than the enforcement behind all the others. Along the way he inserts a characteristic caveat: no law, however good, is beyond the people's power to change — even "fundamental" laws bind only so long as the sovereign people chooses to keep them.

Above these three he places a fourth kind of law, the most important of all: morals, customs, and above all public opinion — written not on marble or bronze but in the hearts of the citizens. This, not the statute book, is the State's real constitution; it is what the great legislator works on in secret while seeming to busy himself with particular regulations. The chapter ends by narrowing the book's scope: of all these classes, only the political laws — those that determine the form of the government — concern the inquiry that follows.

## Key Concepts

- **Political (fundamental) laws** — the laws regulating the relation of the whole to the whole, of the Sovereign to the State; they determine the form of the government and are the only class relevant to Rousseau's subject.
- **The people's right to change any law** — even the best laws are never beyond revision: a people always retains the power to alter its laws, and if it chooses to harm itself, no one has the right to stop it.
- **Civil laws** — the laws arising from the relation of citizens to one another (which should be as slight as possible) and to the city (which should be as strong as possible); only the strength of the State can secure the liberty of its members.
- **Criminal laws** — the laws attaching penalties to disobedience; at bottom not a distinct class of law but the sanction that backs all the rest.
- **Morals, custom, and public opinion as the real constitution** — a fourth kind of law, engraved in citizens' hearts, that renews or replaces the others as they decay and gradually substitutes the force of habit for the force of authority.
- **The legislator's secret work** — while appearing to confine himself to particular regulations, the great legislator is really shaping manners and morals, which form the immovable keystone of the whole structure.

## Archaic Terms

Words and usages that have fallen out of use or shifted meaning (in Cole's translation or Rousseau's terminology).

| Term | Meaning here |
|---|---|
| commonwealth | the State or republic as a whole political community — not a modern federation |
| the Sovereign | the whole people acting collectively as supreme authority — not a king |
| the city | the political community itself (Rousseau's *cité*), not an urban area |
| fundamental laws | what we would call constitutional laws — those fixing the basic order of the State |
| sanction | the penalty or enforcement backing a law — not "approval" |
| graven | engraved, inscribed |
| insensibly | imperceptibly, so gradually as not to be noticed |
| manners (and morals) | Rousseau's *moeurs*: a people's moral habits, customs, and way of life — not etiquette |
| intermediate terms | the middle elements linking the extremes of a relation (a term from mathematical proportion) |

## Modern Translation

If the whole is to be set in order — if the commonwealth is to be given the best possible form — there are several relations to consider. First comes the action of the entire body on itself: the relation of the whole to the whole, of the Sovereign to the State. This relation, as we shall see, is made up of the relations of the intermediate terms.

The laws that regulate this relation are called political laws, and also fundamental laws — not without reason, if they are wise ones. For if there is only one good system for each State, the people that has found it should hold on to it; but if the established order is bad, why should laws that prevent men from being good count as fundamental? Besides, in any case, a people is always free to change its laws, even the best of them; for if it chooses to do itself harm, who has the right to stop it?

The second relation is that of the members to one another, or to the body as a whole. This relation should be as slight as possible in the first respect and as strong as possible in the second: each citizen perfectly independent of all his fellow citizens, and at the same time deeply dependent on the city. Both results are always achieved by the same means, since only the strength of the State can secure the liberty of its members. From this second relation arise the civil laws.

We may also consider a third kind of relation between the individual and the law: the relation of disobedience to its penalty. This gives rise to the criminal laws — which, at bottom, are less a distinct class of law than the sanction behind all the others.

Along with these three kinds of law goes a fourth, the most important of all, which is engraved not on tablets of marble or bronze but in the hearts of the citizens. It forms the real constitution of the State. It gathers new strength every day; when other laws age or die out, it revives them or takes their place; it keeps a people in the ways it was meant to follow; and it imperceptibly substitutes the force of habit for the force of authority. I am speaking of morality, of custom, and above all of public opinion — a power unknown to our political thinkers, yet one on which the success of everything else depends. This is what the great legislator attends to in secret, even while he seems to confine himself to particular regulations; for regulations are only the arc of the arch, while manners and morals, slower to take shape, form in the end its immovable keystone.

Among these different classes of laws, only the political laws — those that determine the form of the government — are relevant to my subject.
