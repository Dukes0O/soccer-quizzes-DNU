# Book I, Chapter IV — Slavery

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book I, Chapter IV
>
> [← I.III The Right of the Strongest](1-03-the-right-of-the-strongest.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [I.V That We Must Always Go Back to a First Convention →](1-05-that-we-must-always-go-back-to-a-first-convention.md)

## Overview

Having ruled out natural authority and force as foundations for legitimate rule, Rousseau turns to the last remaining candidate his opponents offer: voluntary agreement to servitude. Grotius and others had argued that just as an individual can sell himself into slavery, a whole people can hand itself over to a king — and, separately, that conquest in war gives the victor a right to enslave the conquered. This chapter, the longest of Book I, demolishes both arguments in turn.

Against voluntary submission, Rousseau argues that a people gets nothing in exchange for its liberty — not even subsistence, since kings live off their subjects rather than the reverse — and that a gift of oneself for nothing is the act of a madman, which can create no right. Even if one generation could give itself away, it could not bind its children, who are born free. More fundamentally, renouncing liberty means renouncing one's very humanity: an agreement pairing absolute authority on one side with unlimited obedience on the other is self-contradictory and void. Against the argument from conquest, Rousseau redefines war itself: war is a relation between states, not between individual men, so once a soldier surrenders he becomes simply a man again, whose life no one has the right to take. The supposed bargain — life in exchange for liberty — rests on a right to kill that does not exist, and merely continues the state of war rather than ending it.

The chapter completes the destructive phase of Book I. With nature, force, and slavery-conventions all eliminated, only one possible foundation for legitimate authority remains — a genuine first convention among equals — which the next chapter takes up.

## Key Concepts

- **Alienation** — in Rousseau's technical sense, to give or to sell away a right or one's liberty. His attack turns on the terms of the transaction: a slave at least sells himself for subsistence, but a people that "alienates" itself to a king receives nothing at all, so the transaction is either a gift by madmen or no valid transaction whatsoever.
- **The nullity of self-enslavement** — renouncing liberty means renouncing one's status as a human being, along with humanity's rights and even its duties. Stripping all freedom from the will strips all morality from action, so no such agreement can bind.
- **The contradiction of absolute authority** — a convention establishing absolute authority on one side and unlimited obedience on the other cancels itself: you owe nothing to someone entitled to take everything from you, since "his right being mine" makes any claim against the master meaningless.
- **War as a relation between states** — war holds between states, not persons; individuals are enemies only incidentally, as soldiers. This redefinition destroys the "right to kill the vanquished" and with it the alleged right to enslave prisoners of war.
- **The vicious circle of conquest** — Grotius grounds the right of slavery on the right to kill, and the right to kill on the right of slavery. Since the victor has no right over a surrendered enemy's life, exchanging that life for liberty is a fraudulent bargain that leaves the state of war in force.
- **Generational consent** — even if a father could give himself away, he cannot irrevocably give away his children, who are born free. A legitimate arbitrary government would need each generation's fresh consent — at which point it would no longer be arbitrary.

## Modern Translation

Since no man has natural authority over his fellow man, and force creates no right, we must conclude that conventions form the basis of all legitimate authority among men.

If an individual, says Grotius, can alienate his liberty and make himself the slave of a master, why couldn't a whole people do the same and make itself subject to a king? There are plenty of ambiguous words in that passage that would need explaining, but let us stick to the word "alienate." To alienate is to give or to sell. Now, a man who becomes another's slave does not give himself; he sells himself, at the very least in exchange for his subsistence. But in exchange for what does a people sell itself? Far from providing his subjects with their subsistence, a king draws his own entirely from them — and, as Rabelais says, kings do not live on nothing. Do subjects then give up their persons on condition that the king takes their goods as well? I fail to see what they have left to hold on to.

It will be said that a despot guarantees his subjects civil peace. Fine — but what do they gain, if the wars his ambition drags them into, his insatiable greed, and the harassment of his ministers weigh on them more heavily than their own quarrels would have? What do they gain, if this very peace is one of their miseries? There is peace in dungeons too, but is that enough to make them desirable places to live? The Greeks imprisoned in the Cyclops' cave lived there very peacefully, while they waited their turn to be eaten.

To say that a man gives himself away for nothing is to say something absurd and inconceivable; such an act is null and illegitimate for the simple reason that anyone who does it is out of his mind. To say the same of a whole people is to imagine a nation of madmen — and madness creates no right.

Even if each man could alienate himself, he could not alienate his children. They are born human and free; their liberty belongs to them, and no one but they has the right to dispose of it. Before they reach the age of discretion, their father can, in their name, set conditions for their preservation and well-being, but he cannot give them away irrevocably and unconditionally: such a gift runs contrary to the purposes of nature and exceeds the rights of fatherhood. To make an arbitrary government legitimate, then, it would be necessary for the people of each generation to be free to accept or reject it — but if that were so, the government would no longer be arbitrary.

To renounce liberty is to renounce being a man — to surrender the rights of humanity, and even its duties. No compensation is possible for someone who gives up everything. Such a renunciation is incompatible with human nature: to strip all liberty from a man's will is to strip all morality from his acts. Finally, it is an empty and self-contradictory convention that sets up absolute authority on one side and unlimited obedience on the other. Isn't it obvious that we can owe nothing to a person from whom we have the right to demand everything? Doesn't this condition alone — with no equivalence, no exchange — by itself make the act null and void? For what right can my slave have against me, when everything he has belongs to me? His right is my right, and a right of mine against myself is a phrase without meaning.

Grotius and the rest find another origin for the so-called right of slavery in war. On their view, the victor has the right to kill the vanquished, so the vanquished can buy back his life at the price of his liberty — a convention all the more legitimate, they say, because it benefits both parties.

But it is clear that this supposed right to kill the conquered cannot be derived from the state of war at all. Men in their primitive independence have no relations with one another stable enough to constitute either a state of peace or a state of war, so they cannot be natural enemies. War is constituted by a relation between things, not between persons; and since a state of war cannot arise out of simple personal relations, but only out of property relations, private war — war of man against man — can exist neither in the state of nature, where there is no settled property, nor in the social state, where everything is under the authority of law.

Individual fights, duels, and skirmishes are acts that cannot constitute a state of war. As for the private wars authorized by the Establishments of King Louis IX of France and suspended by the Peace of God, these were abuses of feudalism — itself an absurd system if there ever was one, contrary to the principles of natural right and to all good government.

War, then, is a relation not between man and man, but between state and state. In war, individuals are enemies only incidentally — not as men, nor even as citizens, but as soldiers; not as members of their country, but as its defenders. Finally, each state can have only other states as enemies, never men, for no real relation can hold between things of entirely different natures.

> **Rousseau's note:** The Romans, who understood and respected the right of war more than any other nation on earth, carried their scruples on this point so far that a citizen was not allowed to serve as a volunteer without expressly committing himself against the enemy — and against a specific, named enemy. When a legion in which the younger Cato was seeing his first service under Popilius was reorganized, the elder Cato wrote to Popilius that if he wanted his son to continue serving under him, he had to administer a new military oath, because the first oath had been annulled and his son could no longer bear arms against the enemy. The same Cato wrote to his son telling him to take great care not to go into battle before swearing this new oath. I know the siege of Clusium and other isolated incidents can be cited against me, but I am citing laws and customs. The Romans were the people who least often broke their own laws, and no other people has had such good ones.

What's more, this principle agrees with the established rules of every age and the constant practice of all civilized peoples. Declarations of war are notices addressed less to governments than to their subjects. A foreigner — whether king, private individual, or people — who robs, kills, or imprisons another prince's subjects without declaring war on that prince is not an enemy but a brigand. Even in the middle of a real war, a just prince may seize everything in enemy territory that belongs to the public, but he respects the lives and property of individuals: he respects the very rights on which his own are founded. Since the object of war is the destruction of the enemy state, one has the right to kill its defenders as long as they are bearing arms; but the moment they lay down their weapons and surrender, they cease to be enemies or instruments of the enemy and become simply men again — and no one has any right to take their lives. Sometimes it is possible to kill a state without killing a single one of its members; and war confers no right that is not necessary to achieving its object. These are not Grotius's principles, and they do not rest on the authority of poets; they are derived from the nature of things and grounded in reason.

The right of conquest has no foundation other than the right of the strongest. If war does not give the conqueror the right to massacre the conquered peoples, then a right that does not exist cannot serve as the basis for a right to enslave them. One only has the right to kill an enemy when one cannot make him a slave; so the right to enslave him cannot be derived from the right to kill him. It is therefore a fraudulent exchange to make him purchase, at the price of his liberty, a life over which the victor holds no right at all. In founding the right of life and death on the right of slavery, and the right of slavery on the right of life and death, isn't the vicious circle obvious?

Even if we grant this terrible right to kill everyone, I maintain that a slave taken in war, or a conquered people, owes a master nothing beyond obedience for exactly as long as he is compelled to obey. By taking an equivalent for the captive's life, the victor has done him no favor: instead of killing him unprofitably, he has killed him profitably. Far from acquiring any authority over him beyond that of force, the victor remains in a state of war with him. Their relationship is itself an effect of that war, and exercising the rights of war implies no peace treaty. A convention has been made, yes — but far from ending the state of war, this convention presupposes that it continues.

So from whatever angle we look at the question, the right of slavery is null and void — not only because it is illegitimate, but because it is absurd and meaningless. The words "slavery" and "right" contradict each other; they are mutually exclusive. Whether addressed to one man or to a whole people, this speech will always be equally senseless: "I make with you a convention wholly at your expense and wholly to my advantage; I will keep it as long as I please, and you will keep it as long as I please."
