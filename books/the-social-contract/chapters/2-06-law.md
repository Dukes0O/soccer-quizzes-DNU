# Book II, Chapter VI — Law

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter VI
>
> [← II.V The Right of Life and Death](2-05-the-right-of-life-and-death.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.VII The Legislator →](2-07-the-legislator.md)

## Overview

The social contract brought the body politic into existence; this chapter asks what gives it direction. Rousseau's answer is law — and his central move is to define law formally rather than metaphysically. A law is an act in which the whole people decrees for the whole people: the will that decides is general, and so is the object it decides about. Anything that targets a particular person, family, or case — even if the Sovereign itself issues it — is not a law but a decree, an act of magistracy rather than of sovereignty.

This definition dissolves several classic puzzles at a stroke: no one need ask who has the right to make laws (the general will does), whether the ruler is above the law (he is a member of the State), whether a law can be unjust (no one is unjust to himself), or how we can be both free and bound by law (laws are simply records of our own wills). It also yields Rousseau's definition of a "Republic": any State governed by laws in this sense, whatever the form of its administration.

The chapter ends with a problem that sets up the next one. The people ought to author the laws it lives under, but a "blind multitude" rarely knows what is good for it. The general will is always upright, but the judgment guiding it is not always enlightened. Someone must show the public the good it wills but cannot see — and this, Rousseau concludes, makes a legislator necessary.

## Key Concepts

- **Law** — an act in which the whole people decrees for the whole people: general in the will that makes it and general in its object. Law deals with subjects in the mass and actions in the abstract, never with a named person or a particular case.
- **Decree vs. law** — any command aimed at a particular object, even one issued by the Sovereign, is not a law but a decree: an act of magistracy (administration), not of sovereignty.
- **Republic** — Rousseau's term for any State governed by laws in his sense, regardless of its form of administration; on this definition, every legitimate government is republican.
- **The upright but unenlightened general will** — the people always wills the good but does not always see it; its judgment needs guidance, which is why a legislator is required.
- **Justice and convention** — universal justice flows from God and from reason, but without mutual enforcement it is ineffective among human beings; conventions and laws are needed to tie rights to duties and make justice operative.

## Modern Translation

The social compact gave the body politic its existence and its life; now, through legislation, we must give it movement and will. The original act that forms and unites the body still does nothing to determine what it must do to preserve itself.

Whatever is good and in accordance with order is so by the nature of things, independently of human agreements. All justice comes from God; he alone is its source. But if we knew how to receive inspiration from so high a source, we would need neither government nor laws. There is, no doubt, a universal justice that flows from reason alone; but for that justice to be accepted among us, it must be mutual. Speaking in human terms, without natural sanctions the laws of justice have no force among men. They work only to the advantage of the wicked and the ruin of the just, when the just man keeps them toward everyone and no one keeps them toward him. So conventions and laws are needed to join rights to duties and to bring justice to bear on its object. In the state of nature, where everything is held in common, I owe nothing to anyone to whom I have promised nothing; I recognize as belonging to others only what is of no use to me. In the state of society, all rights are fixed by law, and the situation is entirely different.

But what, in the end, is a law? As long as we are content to attach purely metaphysical ideas to the word, we will keep arguing without ever understanding one another; and even once we have defined a law of nature, we will be no closer to defining a law of the State.

I have already said that there can be no general will directed at a particular object. Such an object must be either inside or outside the State. If it is outside, then a will foreign to it cannot be general in relation to it. If it is inside, then it is a part of the State — and in that case a relation arises between the whole and the part that makes them two separate beings: the part is one, and the whole minus the part is the other. But the whole minus a part is not the whole; and as long as this relation holds, there is no whole at all, only two unequal parts. It follows that the will of one is in no way general in relation to the other.

But when the whole people makes a decree concerning the whole people, it considers only itself; and if a relation is formed, it is between the entire object seen from one point of view and the entire object seen from another, with no division of the whole. In that case, the matter being decreed is general, just like the will that decrees it. It is this act that I call a law.

When I say that the object of laws is always general, I mean that law considers subjects as a body and actions in the abstract — never a particular person or a particular action. So the law can certainly decree that there will be privileges, but it cannot grant them to anyone by name. It can create several classes of citizens, and even specify the qualifications for belonging to each class, but it cannot name this or that person as a member. It can establish a monarchical government and hereditary succession, but it cannot choose a king or name a royal family. In short, no function that has a particular object belongs to the legislative power.

Once we see this, several questions vanish at once. We can no longer ask whose business it is to make laws, since laws are acts of the general will. We cannot ask whether the prince is above the law, since he is a member of the State. We cannot ask whether the law can be unjust, since no one is unjust to himself. And we cannot ask how we can be free and yet subject to the laws, since the laws are nothing but records of our own wills.

We also see that, since law unites universality of will with universality of object, whatever any man commands on his own initiative — no matter who he is — cannot be a law. Even what the Sovereign commands concerning a particular matter is no closer to being a law: it is a decree, an act not of sovereignty but of magistracy.

I therefore give the name "Republic" to every State that is governed by laws, whatever the form of its administration may be; for only then does the public interest govern, and only then is the *res publica* — the public thing — a reality. Every legitimate government is republican; what government itself is, I will explain later on.

> **Rousseau's note:** By this word I mean not merely an aristocracy or a democracy, but in general any government directed by the general will, which is the law. To be legitimate, the government must not be one and the same as the Sovereign, but its minister. In that case even a monarchy is a Republic. This will become clearer in the following book.

Laws are, strictly speaking, nothing but the conditions of civil association. The people, being subject to the laws, ought to be their author: only those who join together to form a society have the right to set its terms. But how are they to set them? By common agreement, by sudden inspiration? Does the body politic have an organ for declaring its will? Who will give it the foresight to formulate its acts and announce them in advance — or how will it announce them at the moment of need? How can a blind multitude, which often does not know what it wills because it rarely knows what is good for it, carry out on its own an enterprise as vast and difficult as a system of legislation? By itself, the people always wills the good, but by itself it does not always see it. The general will is always in the right, but the judgment that guides it is not always enlightened. It must be made to see things as they are — and sometimes as they ought to appear to it. It must be shown the good road it is looking for, protected from the seductions of individual wills, taught to view times and places in sequence, and made to weigh the appeal of present, tangible advantages against the danger of distant, hidden evils. Individuals see the good they reject; the public wills the good it does not see. Both stand equally in need of guidance: individuals must be compelled to bring their wills into line with their reason, and the public must be taught to know what it wills. When that is done, public enlightenment produces the union of understanding and will in the social body; the parts work exactly together, and the whole is raised to its greatest power. This is what makes a legislator necessary.
