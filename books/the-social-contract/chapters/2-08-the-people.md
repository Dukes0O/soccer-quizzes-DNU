# Book II, Chapter VIII — The People

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter VIII
>
> [← II.VII The Legislator](2-07-the-legislator.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.IX The People, Continued →](2-09-the-people-continued.md)

## Overview

Having described the legislator in the previous chapter, Rousseau now turns to the material the legislator must work with: the people themselves. His central claim is that laws cannot simply be good in the abstract — a wise lawgiver first examines whether a particular people is *fit* to receive them, just as an architect tests the ground before building. Most nations, he argues, are teachable only in their "youth"; once customs harden and prejudices take root, reform becomes dangerous and futile.

Rousseau allows one dramatic exception: rare moments of crisis — civil wars and revolutions — can shock a state into a kind of rebirth, as with Sparta under Lycurgus or Rome after the Tarquins. But such renewals happen at most once; a people that has lost its civic vigor afterward needs a master, not a liberator. He closes with his famous polemic against Peter the Great: Russia was "civilized too soon," made to imitate Germans and Englishmen instead of becoming Russian — a warning that legislating before a people is ripe spoils the work permanently. The chapter thus opens a three-chapter inquiry (II.VIII–X) into which peoples are suited for legislation.

## Key Concepts

- **Fitness of a people for laws** — Legislation must match the character and condition of the nation it is meant for; laws "good in themselves" fail if the people cannot bear them, as the architect analogy makes vivid.
- **National youth and maturity** — Nations, like individuals, have a window of docility in which they can be shaped by law; act too early and the work is spoiled, too late and the people is incorrigible.
- **Revolutionary rebirth** — Violent crises can occasionally restore a state's youthful vigor, but only once per people and only in exceptional constitutions; it is not a repeatable cure.
- **Liberty cannot be recovered** — Rousseau's maxim for free peoples: a barbarous people can win its freedom, but a people whose civic spirit has decayed can never win it back.
- **Premature civilization (the Peter the Great case)** — Imposing foreign polish on an unripe people prevents it from ever becoming what it might have been; imitation is not the creative genius true lawgiving requires.

## Modern Translation

Before putting up a large building, an architect surveys and tests the ground to see whether it will bear the weight. In the same way, the wise legislator does not begin by drafting laws that are good in themselves, but by first asking whether the people they are meant for is fit to receive them. Plato refused to write laws for the Arcadians and the Cyrenaeans, because he knew both peoples were rich and would never accept equality; and Crete had good laws and bad men together, because Minos had imposed discipline on a people already weighed down with vice.

A thousand nations have achieved worldly greatness that could never have endured good laws — and even those that could have endured them could have done so only for a very short stretch of their long history. Most peoples, like most men, are teachable only in youth; as they grow old they become incorrigible. Once customs are established and prejudices deeply rooted, trying to reform them is dangerous and useless. Like foolish, cowardly patients who panic at the sight of the doctor, the people can no longer stand to have anyone touch its faults, even to cure them.

There are, it is true, moments in the history of states when — just as certain illnesses unhinge men's minds and wipe out their memory of the past — periods of violence and revolution do to peoples what these crises do to individuals. Horror of the past does the work of forgetting, and the state, set ablaze by civil war, is reborn, so to speak, from its ashes and springs from the jaws of death with the vigor of youth restored. Sparta in the time of Lycurgus was like this; so was Rome after the Tarquins; and, in modern times, Holland and Switzerland after driving out their tyrants.

But such events are rare. They are exceptions, and the cause always lies in the particular constitution of the state in question. They cannot even happen twice to the same people, for a people can make itself free only while it is still barbarous — not once its civic energy has worn out. After that, disturbances may destroy it, but revolutions cannot repair it: it needs a master, not a liberator. Free peoples, remember this maxim: "Liberty may be gained, but can never be recovered."

Youth is not infancy. Nations, like men, have a period of youth — or, if you prefer, maturity — that must come before they are made subject to laws. But a people's maturity is not always easy to recognize, and if you act before it arrives, the work is ruined. One people can be brought under discipline from the very start; another cannot manage it after ten centuries. Russia will never be truly civilized, because it was civilized too soon. Peter had a genius for imitation, but he lacked true genius — the kind that creates and makes everything from nothing. Some of what he did was good, but most of it was out of place. He saw that his people was barbarous, but he did not see that it was not yet ripe for civilization: he wanted to civilize it when it needed only to be toughened. His first ambition was to make Germans or Englishmen, when he should have been making Russians; and by persuading his subjects that they were something they are not, he prevented them from ever becoming what they might have been. A French tutor does the same when he trains his pupil to shine as a child prodigy — and to be nothing at all for the rest of his life. The Russian empire will try to conquer Europe, and will itself be conquered. The Tartars, its subjects or its neighbors, will become its masters and ours — a revolution I regard as inevitable. Indeed, all the kings of Europe are working together to hasten it.
