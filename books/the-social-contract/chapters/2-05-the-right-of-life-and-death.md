# Book II, Chapter V — The Right of Life and Death

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter V
>
> [← II.IV The Limits of the Sovereign Power](2-04-the-limits-of-the-sovereign-power.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.VI Law →](2-06-law.md)

## Overview

This short chapter answers a standing objection to Rousseau's theory: if individuals have no right to take their own lives, how can they hand the Sovereign a right of life and death over them? Rousseau dissolves the puzzle by reframing it. The question is not about a right to suicide but about the right to risk one's life in order to preserve it — and the social contract is exactly such a bargain. Whoever wants the protection of the state must accept the risks that protection requires, including, in the extreme case, dying when the state demands it: under the contract, life is no longer a mere gift of nature but a conditional gift of the state.

On capital punishment, Rousseau argues that we consent in advance to die if we become murderers, precisely so that we will not be murdered ourselves — the consent secures our lives rather than disposing of them. A criminal who attacks the law of the society makes war on it and thereby ceases to be a member of it; he is executed not as a citizen but as an enemy. Yet Rousseau immediately hedges the doctrine with humane limits: sentencing particular criminals is not an act of the Sovereign (which by the previous chapter can act only generally); frequent punishment is a symptom of a weak government; and the state has no right to kill anyone it can safely leave alive. The closing remarks on pardons — rare, belonging only to the Sovereign, and dangerous when frequent — connect the health of a state to the rarity of crime rather than to the mercy of its rulers, before Rousseau breaks off, declaring that his heart restrains his pen.

## Key Concepts

- **Life as a conditional gift of the state** — By entering the contract, the citizen exchanges a natural, precarious life for a protected one; in return the state may require him to risk or surrender that life. He is no longer the judge of the dangers the law asks him to face.
- **Willing the end wills the means** — Since the social treaty exists to preserve its members, consenting to that end means consenting to the risks and losses that preserving everyone entails, including military service and the penal law.
- **The criminal as public enemy** — A serious offender breaks the social treaty, ceases to be a member of the state, and stands to it as an enemy in war; executing him is killing an enemy, not a citizen. Trial and judgment are what prove the breach.
- **Punishment as a sign of weak government** — Every wrongdoer could be made good for something; the state has no right to execute anyone it can safely leave alive, even to make an example. Many punishments mean a failing government, not a rigorous one.
- **The right of pardon** — Only the Sovereign, standing above both judge and law, can pardon; but the right is doubtful and should almost never be used. In a well-governed state punishments are few because crimes are few, not because pardons are many.

## Modern Translation

People often ask how individuals, who have no right to dispose of their own lives, can transfer to the Sovereign a right they do not themselves possess. The question seems hard to answer only because it is badly framed. Every man has the right to risk his life in order to preserve it. Has anyone ever called it suicide when a man jumps out of a window to escape a fire? Has that crime ever been charged to someone who dies in a storm at sea, even though he knew the danger when he boarded the ship?

The purpose of the social treaty is the preservation of the contracting parties. Whoever wills the end wills the means as well, and the means here inevitably involve some risks, even some losses. Whoever wants to preserve his life at the expense of others must also be ready, when necessary, to give it up for their sake. Moreover, the citizen is no longer the judge of the dangers the law requires him to face; and when the prince says to him, "It is expedient for the State that you should die," he ought to die — because it is only on that condition that he has lived in security up to now, and because his life is no longer merely a gift of nature but a conditional gift of the State.

The death penalty inflicted on criminals can be seen in much the same light: it is so that we will not become the victims of a murderer that we consent to die if we become murderers ourselves. Under this agreement, far from disposing of our own lives, we are thinking only of protecting them; and we should not assume that any of the parties, at the time, is planning to get himself hanged.

Furthermore, every criminal who attacks the rights of society becomes, by his crimes, a rebel and a traitor to his country. By violating its laws he ceases to be a member of it; indeed, he makes war on it. In that case the preservation of the State is incompatible with his own — one or the other must perish — and when we put the guilty man to death, we kill not so much a citizen as an enemy. The trial and the verdict are the proof and the declaration that he has broken the social treaty and is therefore no longer a member of the State. And since he had acknowledged himself to be a member, at least by living there, he must be cut off from it — by exile as a violator of the compact, or by death as a public enemy. For such an enemy is not a moral person but simply a man, and in that case the right of war is to kill the defeated.

But, it will be objected, the condemnation of a criminal is a particular act. Granted — and that is why such condemnation is not a function of the Sovereign. It is a right the Sovereign can confer without being able to exercise it itself. All my ideas hang together, but I cannot lay them all out at once.

We may add that frequent punishments are always a sign of weakness or laziness in the government. There is no wrongdoer who could not be made good for something. The State has no right to put anyone to death, even as an example, if he can be left alive without danger.

The right to pardon a guilty man, or to exempt him from a penalty imposed by law and pronounced by the judge, belongs only to the authority that stands above both judge and law — that is, the Sovereign. And even the Sovereign's right here is far from clear, and the occasions for using it are extremely rare. In a well-governed State there are few punishments — not because pardons are many, but because criminals are few; it is when a State is in decay that the sheer number of crimes guarantees they will go unpunished. Under the Roman Republic, neither the Senate nor the Consuls ever attempted to grant a pardon; even the people never did so, though it sometimes revoked its own judgments. Frequent pardons announce that crime will soon have no need of them, and everyone can see where that leads. But I feel my heart protesting and holding back my pen. Let us leave these questions to the just man who has never done wrong and who has never himself needed pardon.
