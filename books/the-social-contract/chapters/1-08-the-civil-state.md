# Book I, Chapter VIII — The Civil State

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book I, Chapter VIII
>
> [← I.VII The Sovereign](1-07-the-sovereign.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [I.IX Real Property →](1-09-real-property.md)

## Overview

This short chapter is Rousseau's balance sheet for the social contract: what a human being loses and gains by leaving the state of nature for the civil state. His answer is striking. Far from mourning lost natural freedom, he argues the transition transforms us — justice replaces instinct, duty replaces impulse, and a creature that was little more than a clever animal becomes a moral and intelligent being. The abuses of society can degrade people below their natural condition, but the change itself is an elevation.

Rousseau then draws the ledger precisely. We lose natural liberty (doing whatever we can get away with) and mere possession (holding things by force or first grab); we gain civil liberty (freedom bounded by the general will) and true property (ownership grounded in legal title). He closes with a third, deeper gain: moral liberty — obedience to a law we prescribe to ourselves — which alone makes us masters of ourselves. That final idea, dropped almost as an aside, would become one of the most influential sentences in modern political philosophy, echoing through Kant's ethics of autonomy.

## Key Concepts

- **The civil state** — the condition of living under the social contract, contrasted with the state of nature; entering it changes not just our situation but our nature, substituting justice for instinct.
- **Natural liberty vs. civil liberty** — natural liberty is limited only by an individual's own strength; civil liberty is limited by the general will. The contract trades the first for the second.
- **Possession vs. property** — possession rests on force or being the first occupier; property exists only in the civil state, resting on a positive (legal) title.
- **Moral liberty** — obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself; the impulse of mere appetite is slavery, so only this self-legislated freedom makes a person truly master of himself.

## Archaic Terms

Words and usages that have fallen out of use or shifted meaning (in Cole's translation or Rousseau's terminology).

| Term | Meaning here |
|---|---|
| civil state | Life under an established political community and its laws, as opposed to the pre-political "state of nature" |
| right of the first occupier | The claim to a thing based simply on getting to it first — "finders keepers" as a basis for holding goods |
| positive title | A claim established by enacted law or formal convention, not by nature or force ("positive" as in "posited," man-made law) |
| proprietorship | Ownership; secure legal property in what one holds |
| commensurable | Measurable on a common scale, so the two sides can be directly compared |
| on this head | On this topic or point |
| stupid | Dull, unthinking, lacking understanding — not the modern insult |

## Modern Translation

The passage from the state of nature to the civil state produces a remarkable change in a human being. It substitutes justice for instinct in his conduct and gives his actions a moral quality they never had before. Only then — when the voice of duty replaces physical impulse, and right replaces appetite — does man, who until now had thought only of himself, find himself forced to act on different principles and to consult his reason before listening to his inclinations. In this new state he does give up certain advantages that nature gave him, but he gains such great ones in return — his faculties are exercised and developed, his ideas broadened, his feelings ennobled, his whole soul lifted so high — that if the abuses of this new condition did not often drag him below the condition he left, he would have to bless without ceasing the happy moment that took him out of it forever and turned him from a stupid, unimaginative animal into an intelligent being and a man.

Let us reduce the whole exchange to terms that can be easily compared. What man loses by the social contract is his natural liberty and an unlimited right to anything that tempts him and that he can get; what he gains is civil liberty and secure ownership of everything he possesses. To avoid miscalculating in this trade-off, we must clearly distinguish natural liberty, which is limited only by the individual's own strength, from civil liberty, which is limited by the general will; and we must distinguish possession, which is merely the effect of force or the right of the first occupier, from property, which can be founded only on a positive legal title.

Beyond all this, we could add to what man acquires in the civil state moral liberty, which alone makes him truly his own master; for to be driven by appetite alone is slavery, while obedience to a law we prescribe to ourselves is liberty. But I have already said too much on this point, and the philosophical meaning of the word *liberty* is not my subject here.
