# Book I, Chapter III — The Right of the Strongest

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book I, Chapter III
>
> [← I.II The First Societies](1-02-the-first-societies.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [I.IV Slavery →](1-04-slavery.md)

## Overview

Having argued in the previous chapter that no natural authority of one person over another exists, Rousseau now demolishes the other candidate foundation for political authority: force. The so-called "right of the strongest" — the idea that might makes right — is, he argues, not a right at all. Force is a physical fact, and physical facts cannot create moral obligations. If you yield to a gunman, you act out of necessity or prudence, not duty.

Rousseau's central move is a reductio: if force really did create right, then right would change hands every time a greater force appeared, and "legitimacy" would mean nothing more than "currently strongest." Under that logic, the only rational project would be to become the strongest oneself, and disobedience would become legitimate the moment one could get away with it. A "right" that evaporates whenever force fails is no right; the word adds nothing to the concept of force. Even the biblical injunction to obey the powers that be, and the doctrine that all power comes from God, cannot rescue the argument — all sickness comes from God too, yet no one thinks calling a doctor is forbidden.

The chapter ends where the book's real problem begins: if neither nature nor force can ground legitimate authority, and we are obliged to obey only legitimate powers, then what does make power legitimate? That question drives the chapters that follow, leading toward Rousseau's answer — convention, the social contract.

## Key Concepts

- **The "right of the strongest"** — the claim that superior force itself confers a right to rule and imposes a duty to obey. Rousseau treats it as an incoherent phrase: force is physical, right is moral, and the one cannot generate the other.
- **Necessity vs. duty** — yielding to force is something you *must* do, not something you *ought* to do. An act compelled by necessity (or chosen out of prudence) carries no moral weight, so obedience to mere force can never be a duty.
- **Right that perishes with force** — Rousseau's reductio: if force creates right, then any greater force inherits that right, and disobedience becomes legitimate as soon as it can be carried out safely. A "right" with those properties is empty — the word adds nothing.
- **Legitimate powers** — the conclusion of the chapter: since force does not create right, we are obliged to obey only powers that are legitimate. What makes a power legitimate is the question the rest of the book must answer.

## Modern Translation

The strongest person is never strong enough to stay in charge forever unless he converts his strength into right, and obedience into duty. Hence the "right of the strongest" — a phrase that sounds like irony but is in practice treated as a fundamental principle. Will no one ever explain it to us? Force is a physical power, and I cannot see what moral effect it could possibly have. Yielding to force is an act of necessity, not of will — at most, an act of prudence. In what sense could it ever be a duty?

Suppose for a moment that this so-called "right" exists. I maintain that the only result is a heap of inexplicable nonsense. For if force creates right, then the effect changes along with the cause: any force greater than the first inherits its right. The moment it becomes possible to disobey safely, disobedience becomes legitimate; and since the strongest is always in the right, the only thing that matters is to make yourself the strongest. But what kind of right is it that dies the moment force fails? If we have to obey because we are forced to, there is no need to obey out of duty; and if we are not forced to obey, we are under no obligation at all. Clearly, then, the word "right" adds nothing to force: here it means absolutely nothing.

Obey the powers that be. If this means "yield to force," it is sound advice, but redundant — I can guarantee it will never be violated. All power comes from God, I admit; but so does all sickness. Does that mean we are forbidden to call a doctor? A robber ambushes me at the edge of a wood: I must hand over my purse under compulsion, certainly — but if I could manage to keep it, would I be morally bound to give it up anyway? After all, the pistol he is holding is also a power.

Let us agree, then, that force does not create right, and that we are obliged to obey only legitimate powers. In that case, my original question returns.
