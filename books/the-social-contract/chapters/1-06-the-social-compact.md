# Book I, Chapter VI — The Social Compact

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book I, Chapter VI
>
> [← I.V That We Must Always Go Back to a First Convention](1-05-that-we-must-always-go-back-to-a-first-convention.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [I.VII The Sovereign →](1-07-the-sovereign.md)

## Overview

This is the pivotal chapter of the whole book: here Rousseau finally states the problem the *Social Contract* exists to solve, and gives the terms of the contract itself. He begins from a crisis in the state of nature — a point at which the obstacles to survival outstrip what any individual can manage alone, so that humanity must either combine its forces or perish. But combining forces raises a paradox: each person's force and freedom are the very tools of his self-preservation, so how can he hand them over without destroying himself? The fundamental problem, then, is to find a form of association in which the whole common force protects each member, yet each member "still obeys himself alone and remains as free as before."

Rousseau's solution is a contract whose clauses reduce to a single one: the total alienation of each associate, with all his rights, to the whole community. Precisely because the surrender is total and identical for everyone, no one is subordinated to anyone else — each, in giving himself to all, gives himself to no one, and gains over every other associate the same right he grants over himself. The formula of the compact follows: each puts his person and power under the supreme direction of the general will, and the body receives each member as an indivisible part of the whole. The act creates a new moral and collective person, and the chapter closes with the precise vocabulary that governs the rest of the book — Republic, body politic, State, Sovereign, Power, people, citizens, subjects.

## Key Concepts

- **The fundamental problem** — how to design an association in which the whole common force defends each member's person and goods, while each member obeys only himself and remains as free as before. Everything else in the book is the working-out of this problem's solution.
- **Total alienation** — the single clause to which all the terms of the contract reduce: each associate gives himself and all his rights entirely to the community. Because the surrender is complete and equal, the conditions are the same for all, no one has an interest in making them oppressive, and no residual private rights survive to breed disputes.
- **The general will** — introduced here in the formula of the compact itself: the supreme direction under which each member places his person and power. The following chapters develop what it is; here it appears as the unifying principle of the new body.
- **The moral and collective body** — the act of association creates a new public person distinct from the individuals who compose it, with its own unity, common identity, life, and will.
- **The vocabulary of the body politic** — one entity under different aspects: *Republic* or *body politic* generally; *State* when passive, *Sovereign* when active, *Power* in relation to other states; its members are the *people* collectively, *citizens* as sharers in sovereign power, *subjects* as bound by the State's laws.

## Archaic Terms

Words and usages that have fallen out of use or shifted meaning (in Cole's translation or Rousseau's terminology).

| Term | Meaning here |
|---|---|
| alienation | the legal giving-up or transfer of something one owns — here, of one's rights and person to the community; not the modern psychological sense of estrangement |
| compact | a formal agreement or covenant; "social compact" = the social contract |
| subsist | to continue to exist ("that primitive condition can then subsist no longer") |
| engender | to create, generate ("men cannot engender new forces") |
| motive power | the single driving force that sets the combined forces in motion, as in machinery |
| conventional liberty | freedom created by convention (agreement), as opposed to natural liberty |
| moral body / public person | "moral" in the old sense of an artificial, non-physical entity — a corporate person created by agreement, not a comment on ethics |
| city | in Rousseau's strict sense, a body of citizens (a political community), not merely a town of houses |
| severally | individually, taken one by one (as opposed to collectively) |

## Modern Translation

I assume that men have reached the point where the obstacles to their survival in the state of nature have grown stronger than the resources each individual can bring to bear to maintain himself in that state. At that point, the primitive condition can no longer continue, and the human race would perish unless it changed its way of living.

Now, since men cannot create new forces, but only unite and direct the ones that exist, they have no other way of preserving themselves than to form, by joining together, a sum of forces great enough to overcome the resistance — forces they must set in motion by a single driving power and make act in concert.

Such a sum of forces can arise only when several people come together. But since each man's force and liberty are the primary instruments of his self-preservation, how can he commit them without harming his own interests and neglecting the care he owes himself? This difficulty, as it bears on my subject, can be stated in the following terms:

"The problem is to find a form of association that will defend and protect, with the whole common force, the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, still obeys himself alone and remains as free as before." This is the fundamental problem to which the social contract provides the solution.

The clauses of this contract are so completely determined by the nature of the act that the slightest modification would make them null and void. So although they have perhaps never been formally stated, they are everywhere the same, and everywhere tacitly accepted and recognized — until, the moment the social compact is violated, each person regains his original rights and takes back his natural liberty, while losing the conventional liberty for which he had given it up.

Properly understood, these clauses all come down to a single one: the total alienation of each associate, together with all his rights, to the whole community. For, in the first place, since each gives himself entirely, the conditions are the same for everyone; and since the conditions are the same for everyone, no one has any interest in making them burdensome to the others.

Moreover, since the alienation is made without reservation, the union is as perfect as it can be, and no associate has anything further to claim. For if individuals kept certain rights back, then — since there would be no common superior to judge between them and the public — each person, being his own judge on one point, would soon demand to be his own judge on everything. The state of nature would thus continue, and the association would inevitably become either useless or tyrannical.

Finally, each man, in giving himself to all, gives himself to no one; and since there is no associate over whom he does not acquire the same right that he grants others over himself, he gains the equivalent of everything he loses, plus greater force to preserve what he has.

If, then, we strip the social compact of everything that is not essential to it, we will find that it comes down to these terms:

"*Each of us puts his person and all his power in common under the supreme direction of the general will; and, in our corporate capacity, we receive each member as an indivisible part of the whole.*"

In that instant, in place of the individual person of each contracting party, this act of association creates a moral and collective body, composed of as many members as the assembly has votes, and receiving from this same act its unity, its common identity, its life, and its will. This public person, formed by the union of all the other persons, once took the name of "city," and now takes that of "Republic" or "body politic." Its members call it the "State" when it is passive, the "Sovereign" when it is active, and a "Power" when comparing it with others of its kind. Those who are associated in it take, collectively, the name of "people"; individually, they are called "citizens," as sharers in the sovereign power, and "subjects," as being bound by the laws of the State. These terms are often confused and mistaken for one another; it is enough to know how to distinguish them when they are being used with precision.

> **Rousseau's note:** The real meaning of this word has been almost entirely lost in modern times: most people mistake a town for a city and a townsman for a citizen. They do not know that houses make a town, but citizens make a city. This same mistake once cost the Carthaginians dearly. I have never read of the title "citizen" being given to the subjects of any prince — not even to the ancient Macedonians, or to the English of today, though they are closer to liberty than anyone else. Only the French adopt the name "citizen" casually everywhere, because, as their dictionaries show, they have no idea what it means; otherwise, in usurping it, they would be guilty of lèse-majesté. Among them the name expresses a virtue, not a right. When Bodin spoke of our citizens and townsmen, he made a serious blunder in taking the one class for the other. M. d'Alembert avoided the error, and in his article on Geneva clearly distinguished the four orders of men (or even five, counting simple foreigners) who live in our town, of which only two make up the Republic. No other French writer, to my knowledge, has understood the real meaning of the word "citizen."
