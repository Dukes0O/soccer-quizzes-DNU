# Book II, Chapter IV — The Limits of the Sovereign Power

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter IV
>
> [← II.III Whether the General Will Is Fallible](2-03-whether-the-general-will-is-fallible.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.V The Right of Life and Death →](2-05-the-right-of-life-and-death.md)

## Overview

Having declared sovereignty absolute, Rousseau now faces the obvious worry: does the social contract swallow the individual whole? His answer is a careful drawing of boundaries. The sovereign does have total power over its members — just as nature gives each person total power over their own limbs — but that power is only sovereignty at all when it acts through the general will, and the general will has a built-in limit: it must be general in its object as well as in its source. It must come from everyone and apply to everyone. The moment the community pronounces on a particular person or a particular case, it stops being sovereign and becomes something else — a magistrate, a party to a dispute — with no special claim to be right.

This gives Rousseau his key structural result: every genuine act of sovereignty binds or benefits all citizens equally, because each voter, in voting for all, is necessarily voting for himself. Equality of rights is not a moral add-on; it is baked into the mechanics of general willing. The sovereign therefore has no competence to burden one subject more than another — such a demand would be a particular act, outside its jurisdiction by definition.

The chapter closes by cashing out the bargain. What the individual gives up under the contract is not a real renunciation but a profitable trade: precarious natural independence for civil liberty, private strength for a right the whole community defends, an exposed life for a protected one. Even the duty to fight for the state, Rousseau argues, is just giving back a fraction of the security the state provides — risks we would run far more often, and alone, in the state of nature. This defense of the contract as a gain rather than a loss sets up the hardest case of all, taken up next: the state's power over life and death.

## Key Concepts

- **Absolute but bounded sovereignty** — The social compact gives the body politic total power over its members, but that power counts as sovereignty only when directed by the general will; it cannot exceed the scope of general conventions.
- **Generality of object** — The general will must be general in what it wills, not just in who wills it. Directed at a particular person or fact, it loses its natural rectitude, because the voters are then judging something external to themselves with no principle of fairness to guide them.
- **Sovereign vs. magistrate** — When a people decides particular cases (appointing rulers, honoring one citizen, punishing another), it acts as government/magistrate, not as sovereign. Rousseau's example is Athens, whose assembly did everything by particular decree.
- **Equality as a structural product** — Because each person, in voting for all, thinks of "each" as including himself, acts of sovereignty automatically bind or favor all citizens alike; the sovereign sees only the body of the nation, never individuals.
- **The advantageous exchange** — The contract involves no genuine renunciation: individuals trade an uncertain, precarious existence for a secure one, natural independence for liberty, and vulnerable strength for an invincible right backed by the whole social union.

| undertakings | Commitments, obligations entered into |
| equity | Fairness, impartial justice |
| body politic | The people considered as a single collective body created by the social contract |

## Modern Translation

If the state is an artificial person whose life consists in the union of its members, and if its most important concern is its own preservation, then it needs a universal, compelling force to move and arrange each part in whatever way best serves the whole. Just as nature gives every man absolute power over all his limbs, the social compact gives the body politic absolute power over all its members; and it is this power which, when directed by the general will, bears — as I have said — the name of Sovereignty.

But besides the public person, we have to consider the private persons who compose it, whose life and liberty are naturally independent of it. So we must distinguish clearly between the respective rights of the citizens and the Sovereign, and between the duties the former must fulfill as subjects and the natural rights they should enjoy as men.

> **Rousseau's note:** Attentive readers, please don't rush to accuse me of contradicting myself. Given the poverty of the language, the terminology made it unavoidable — but wait and see.

I grant that under the social compact each man alienates only the portion of his powers, goods, and liberty that it matters for the community to control; but it must also be granted that the Sovereign alone judges what matters.

Every service a citizen can render the state, he ought to render the moment the Sovereign demands it. But the Sovereign, for its part, cannot impose on its subjects any restraint that is useless to the community — it cannot even wish to; for under the law of reason, no less than under the law of nature, nothing happens without a cause.

The commitments that bind us to the social body are obligatory only because they are mutual, and their nature is such that in fulfilling them we cannot work for others without also working for ourselves. Why is the general will always right? Why does everyone continually will the happiness of each individual? Precisely because there is no one who does not take the word "each" to mean himself, and who is not thinking of himself when he votes for all. This proves that equality of rights, and the idea of justice that such equality produces, originate in the preference each man gives to himself — and therefore in human nature itself. It proves that the general will, to be truly general, must be general in its object as well as in its essence; that it must both come from all and apply to all; and that it loses its natural rightness when it is aimed at some particular, definite object, because then we are judging something foreign to us and have no true principle of fairness to guide us.

Indeed, as soon as a question of particular fact or right arises on a point not already settled by a prior general agreement, the matter becomes a dispute. It is a case in which the individuals concerned are one party and the public is the other, but in which I can see neither the law that ought to be followed nor the judge who ought to decide. It would be absurd, in such a case, to propose referring the question to an express decision of the general will — that decision could only be the conclusion reached by one of the parties, and so, for the other party, it would be merely an external, particular will, inclined on this occasion toward injustice and liable to error. So just as a particular will cannot stand in for the general will, the general will in turn changes its nature when its object is particular: being general, it cannot pass judgment on a specific man or a specific fact. When the people of Athens, for instance, appointed or removed its leaders, decreed honors for one man and penalties for another, and through a mass of particular decrees indiscriminately performed all the functions of government, it no longer had a general will in the strict sense. It was acting no longer as Sovereign but as magistrate. This will seem contrary to received opinion; but I must be given time to lay out my own.

From the foregoing it should be clear that what makes the will general is less the number of voters than the common interest uniting them. Under this system, each person necessarily submits to the conditions he imposes on others — an admirable alignment of interest and justice that gives the common deliberations a quality of fairness which vanishes the moment any particular question is discussed, since there is then no common interest to unite and identify the judge's standard with the party's.

From whatever direction we approach our principle, we reach the same conclusion: the social compact establishes among the citizens an equality of such a kind that they all bind themselves under the same conditions and should therefore all enjoy the same rights. Thus, from the very nature of the compact, every act of Sovereignty — that is, every authentic act of the general will — binds or benefits all the citizens equally, so that the Sovereign recognizes only the body of the nation and draws no distinctions among those who make it up. What, then, strictly speaking, is an act of Sovereignty? It is not an agreement between a superior and an inferior, but an agreement between the body and each of its members. It is legitimate, because it is based on the social contract; equitable, because it is common to all; useful, because it can have no object but the general good; and stable, because it is guaranteed by the public force and the supreme power. As long as subjects have to submit only to agreements of this kind, they obey no one but their own will; and to ask how far the respective rights of the Sovereign and the citizens extend is to ask how far the citizens can commit themselves to one another — each to all, and all to each.

We can see from this that the sovereign power, absolute, sacred, and inviolable as it is, does not and cannot exceed the limits of general agreements, and that every man may dispose freely of whatever goods and liberty those agreements leave him. It follows that the Sovereign never has the right to burden one subject more than another, because then the question becomes particular and falls outside its jurisdiction.

Once these distinctions are admitted, it becomes clear how false it is to say that the social contract involves any real renunciation on the part of individuals. The position they find themselves in as a result of the contract is actually preferable to the one they were in before. Instead of a renunciation, they have made an advantageous exchange: an uncertain, precarious way of living for one that is better and more secure; natural independence for liberty; the power to harm others for their own safety; and their own strength, which others might overpower, for a right that the social union makes invincible. Their very life, which they have devoted to the state, is constantly protected by it; and when they risk that life in the state's defense, what are they doing but giving back what they have received from it? What are they doing that they would not do more often, and at greater danger, in the state of nature — where they would inevitably have to fight battles at the risk of their lives to defend the very means of their preservation? Everyone must indeed fight when the country needs them; but then, no one ever has to fight for himself alone. Is it not a gain to run, on behalf of what secures us, only a portion of the risks we would have to run for ourselves the moment we lost that security?
