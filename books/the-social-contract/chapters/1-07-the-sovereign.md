# Book I, Chapter VII — The Sovereign

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book I, Chapter VII
>
> [← I.VI The Social Compact](1-06-the-social-compact.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [I.VIII The Civil State →](1-08-the-civil-state.md)

## Overview

Having defined the social compact in the previous chapter, Rousseau now works out its internal logic. Because each person who enters the compact is at once a member of the Sovereign (the collective law-making body) and a subject of the State, everyone is bound in two directions at once. From this double relation Rousseau draws two striking conclusions: the Sovereign cannot bind itself by any fundamental law — not even the social contract itself — because a body cannot contract with itself in a single capacity; and the Sovereign, being made up of nothing but its members, cannot have an interest opposed to theirs and therefore needs to give its subjects no guarantees.

The reverse, however, does not hold. Individuals retain private wills that can pull against the general will they share as citizens — a person may want the benefits of citizenship without the burdens of a subject. So the compact tacitly contains the one clause that gives force to all the others: whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body. This is the chapter's famous and deliberately paradoxical claim — that such a person "will be forced to be free" — which Rousseau presents not as tyranny but as the very condition that protects each citizen from personal dependence on any other individual and makes civil obligations legitimate rather than absurd.

## Key Concepts

- **The double capacity** — Each member of the compact stands in two roles at once: as a member of the Sovereign he is bound to the individuals, and as a member of the State he is bound to the Sovereign. This dual relation structures the whole chapter's argument.
- **The Sovereign cannot bind itself** — Since the Sovereign can regard itself in only one capacity, a law it imposed on itself would be a contract with itself; hence no fundamental law, not even the social contract, can bind the body of the people — though it can make agreements with outsiders.
- **The Sovereign needs no guarantee** — Formed entirely of the individuals who compose it, the Sovereign can have no interest contrary to theirs; the body cannot wish to harm all its members. "The Sovereign, merely by virtue of what it is, is always what it should be."
- **Particular will vs. general will** — As a man, each individual can have a private will that differs from or opposes the general will he has as a citizen, tempting him to enjoy the rights of citizenship while shirking the duties of a subject.
- **"Forced to be free"** — The tacit clause that gives force to the whole compact: whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body — a compulsion Rousseau presents as securing each citizen against all personal dependence.

## Modern Translation

This formula shows that the act of association involves a mutual commitment between the public and the individuals, and that each individual — contracting, so to speak, with himself — is bound in a double capacity: as a member of the Sovereign he is bound to the individuals, and as a member of the State he is bound to the Sovereign. But the maxim of civil law that no one is bound by commitments made to himself does not apply here, for there is a great difference between taking on an obligation to yourself and taking one on to a whole of which you form a part.

It should further be noted that public deliberation, which can bind all the subjects to the Sovereign — because each of them can be regarded in those two different capacities — cannot, for the opposite reason, bind the Sovereign to itself. It is therefore contrary to the nature of the body politic for the Sovereign to impose on itself a law it cannot break. Since it can regard itself in only one capacity, it is in the position of an individual making a contract with himself; and this makes it clear that there is not, and cannot be, any kind of fundamental law binding on the body of the people — not even the social contract itself. This does not mean that the body politic cannot enter into commitments with others, so long as they do not violate the contract; for in relation to what is outside it, it becomes a simple being, an individual.

But the body politic, or the Sovereign, since it draws its very existence from the sanctity of the contract, can never bind itself — even toward an outsider — to anything that conflicts with that original act: for instance, to alienate any part of itself, or to submit to another Sovereign. To violate the act by which it exists would be to annihilate itself; and what is itself nothing can create nothing.

As soon as this multitude is united in one body in this way, no one can injure one of the members without attacking the body, still less injure the body without the members feeling it. Duty and interest alike therefore oblige the two contracting parties to help each other; and the same men should seek to combine, in their double capacity, all the advantages that capacity brings.

Again, the Sovereign, being formed entirely of the individuals who compose it, neither has nor can have any interest contrary to theirs; consequently the sovereign power needs to give no guarantee to its subjects, because it is impossible for the body to want to hurt all its members. We shall also see later that it cannot hurt any one of them in particular. The Sovereign, merely by virtue of what it is, is always what it should be.

This is not, however, true of the subjects' relation to the Sovereign. Despite their common interest, the Sovereign would have no security that the subjects would keep their commitments unless it found some way of assuring itself of their fidelity.

In fact, each individual, as a man, may have a particular will contrary to, or different from, the general will he has as a citizen. His private interest may speak to him quite differently from the common interest. His absolute and naturally independent existence may lead him to view what he owes to the common cause as an optional donation, whose loss will hurt others less than its payment burdens him; and, regarding the moral person that constitutes the State as a *persona ficta* — because it is not a man — he may wish to enjoy the rights of citizenship without being willing to fulfill the duties of a subject. If such injustice were allowed to continue, it could only bring about the ruin of the body politic.

So that the social compact may not be an empty formula, it tacitly includes the one commitment that alone can give force to all the others: that whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body. This means nothing less than that he will be forced to be free; for this is the condition which, by giving each citizen to his country, secures him against all personal dependence. Here lies the key to the working of the political machine; this alone makes civil commitments legitimate — without it they would be absurd, tyrannical, and open to the most terrible abuses.
