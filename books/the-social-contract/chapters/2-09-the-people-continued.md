# Book II, Chapter IX — The People (Continued)

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter IX
>
> [← II.VIII The People](2-08-the-people.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.X The People, Continued Again →](2-10-the-people-continued-2.md)

## Overview

Having asked in the previous chapter *which* peoples are fit for good laws, Rousseau now asks how *big* a state should be. His answer is that there are natural limits in both directions: a state can be too small to defend itself, but — his real concern here — it can also be too large to be governed well. Just as nature sets bounds on the height of a well-formed human body, every body politic has a maximum strength that it loses by growing beyond it.

Most of the chapter is a catalogue of the pathologies of size: layer upon layer of administration, each paid for by the people; laws that cannot fit provinces with different customs and climates; citizens who feel no attachment to rulers they never see or to fellow citizens they do not know; leaders so buried in business that the state ends up "governed by clerks." Against these internal reasons for staying small stands one external reason for growing: weak states risk being swallowed by aggressive neighbors, since all peoples press outward against one another.

Rousseau's conclusion is that the statesman's art lies in finding the mean between expansion and contraction — but the internal reasons for staying compact are absolute, while the external reasons for growing are merely relative, so a strong, healthy constitution should be preferred to a large territory. He closes with a warning aimed at conquering states (Rome is the unspoken example): a state built so that it must keep expanding to survive carries the date of its fall written into its constitution.

## Key Concepts

- **Natural limits of state size** — like a well-made human body, a state has proportions fixed by nature: too large and it cannot be well governed, too small and it cannot maintain itself.
- **The social bond weakens as it stretches** — every extension of the social tie relaxes it; a small state is, proportionally, stronger than a great one.
- **Administrative burden of distance** — governing at long range is like lifting a weight on a long lever; each added tier of government (city, district, province, viceroyalty) costs the people more while governing them worse.
- **Civic affection requires proximity** — people cannot love rulers they never see, a country that seems as vast as the world, or fellow citizens who are strangers; uniform laws cannot suit diverse provinces.
- **Expansion vs. contraction** — reasons to expand (security against neighbors) are external and relative; reasons to stay small (a healthy constitution) are internal and absolute, and should take priority.
- **States built on conquest** — a constitution that requires endless expansion to sustain itself contains, in the very limits of its growth, the inevitable moment of its collapse.

## Modern Translation

Nature sets limits on the height of a well-made man, and outside those limits she produces only giants or dwarfs. In the same way, if a state's constitution is to be at its best, there are limits to be set on its size: it must be neither too large to be well governed nor too small to sustain itself. Every body politic has a maximum strength that it cannot exceed, and that it actually loses by growing bigger. Every extension of the social bond means a loosening of it; and, as a general rule, a small state is proportionally stronger than a large one.

A thousand arguments could be made for this principle. First, long distances make administration harder, just as a weight grows heavier at the end of a longer lever. Administration becomes more and more burdensome as distance increases. To begin with, each city has its own administration, paid for by the people; each district has its own, still paid for by the people; then comes each province, and then the great governments, the satrapies, the viceroyalties — each costing more the higher you go, and always at the expense of the unfortunate people. Above them all sits the supreme administration, which dwarfs everything beneath it. All these accumulated charges are a constant drain on the subjects; and far from being better governed by all these layers of authority, they are worse governed than if a single authority stood over them. Meanwhile, hardly any resources are left for emergencies; and whenever the state has to draw on them, it stands on the brink of ruin.

That is not all. Not only does the government have less vigor and speed in enforcing the laws, preventing public harms, correcting abuses, and heading off seditious plots hatched in distant places; the people also feel less affection for rulers they never see, for a country that seems to them as vast as the world, and for fellow citizens who are mostly strangers. The same laws cannot suit so many diverse provinces, with different customs, set in wildly different climates, and unable to bear a uniform government. Yet different laws produce only trouble and confusion among peoples who live under the same rulers, are in constant communication, mix and intermarry, and — falling under the sway of new customs — can never be sure that their own inheritance is really theirs. In such a multitude of people, unknown to one another and crowded together at the seat of the central administration, talent goes buried, virtue unrecognized, and vice unpunished. The leaders, buried in business, see nothing with their own eyes; the state is governed by clerks. Finally, the measures required to uphold the general authority — an authority all these distant officials want either to evade or to exploit — soak up all the public's energy, leaving none for the people's happiness. There is barely enough left even to defend them when the need arises; and so a body grown too big for its constitution sinks and collapses, crushed under its own weight.

On the other hand, the state must secure a firm foundation if it is to be stable — if it is to withstand the shocks it is bound to suffer and the exertions it will be forced to make to maintain itself. For all peoples have a kind of centrifugal force that drives them to act continually against one another, each tending to expand at its neighbors' expense, like the vortices of Descartes. The weak therefore risk being swallowed up in short order; and hardly any state can preserve itself except by putting itself in equilibrium with all the others, so that the pressure is roughly equal on every side.

So there are reasons for expansion and reasons for contraction, and it is no small part of the statesman's skill to find, between the two, the mean most favorable to the state's preservation. It can be said, in general, that the reasons for expansion, being merely external and relative, ought to give way to the reasons for contraction, which are internal and absolute. A strong and healthy constitution is the first thing to seek; and it is better to rely on the vigor that comes from good government than on the resources a great territory supplies.

It may be added that there have been states so constituted that the necessity of conquest was built into their very constitution — states that, in order to maintain themselves, were forced to expand without end. Perhaps they congratulated themselves heartily on this fortunate necessity; yet it showed them, along with the limits of their greatness, the inevitable moment of their fall.
