# Book II, Chapter X — The People (Continued)

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter X
>
> [← II.IX The People, Continued](2-09-the-people-continued.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.XI The Various Systems of Legislation →](2-11-the-various-systems-of-legislation.md)

## Overview

This chapter concludes the three-chapter discussion of the people — the question of what raw material a legislator needs to work with. Where the previous chapter measured a State by territory alone, this one adds the second dimension: population. A State's real strength lies in the right ratio between land and people — enough land to feed the inhabitants, and enough inhabitants to work the land. Too much territory invites defensive wars; too little forces dependence on neighbors and breeds wars of conquest. But Rousseau refuses to give a fixed formula: soil quality, climate, the habits of the inhabitants, women's fertility, and local circumstances (mountains allow expansion, coastlines allow density) all shift the ratio, so the legislator must plan for the population a country will naturally reach, not the one it currently has.

To these material conditions Rousseau adds a condition of timing that trumps them all: a people can only receive laws in a moment of peace and plenty, because a State being founded is at its most vulnerable, like a battalion in the act of forming up. Governments erected during storms and crises are the work of usurpers, who exploit public terror to pass destructive laws a people would never accept calmly — indeed, the moment chosen is one of the surest tests for telling a legislator from a tyrant.

The chapter closes by gathering all the conditions from these three chapters into a single portrait of the people fit for legislation: already loosely united, not yet stamped by law or ingrained custom, secure from invasion, self-sufficient, neither rich nor poor, combining an old people's cohesion with a new people's teachability. Since these conditions almost never coincide, few States have good constitutions — but one European country still qualifies: Corsica, whose brave defense of its liberty prompts Rousseau's famous prediction that "some day that little island will astonish Europe."

## Key Concepts

- **The right ratio of land to people** — a State is truly great not through size but through proportion: the land should suffice to maintain the inhabitants, and the inhabitants should be as many as the land can maintain; this proportion yields the maximum strength for a given population.
- **Excess and deficit of territory** — too much land is hard to guard, poorly cultivated, and provokes defensive wars; too little makes the State dependent on its neighbors and drives it to wars of offense; a people forced to choose between commerce and war is weak, and "only insignificance or greatness can keep it free."
- **No fixed formula** — soil fertility, climate, the inhabitants' habits of consumption, women's fecundity, and the legislator's own influence all vary; the legislator must judge by what he foresees the population becoming, not by what he sees now.
- **The moment of founding** — lawgiving requires peace and plenty, because a State organizing itself is at its weakest, like a battalion forming up; war, famine, or sedition at that moment destroys it.
- **Legislator vs. tyrant** — usurpers deliberately choose (or create) times of trouble to pass, under cover of public terror, destructive laws the people would never adopt in cold blood; the moment chosen for lawgiving distinguishes the legislator's work from the tyrant's.
- **The people fit for legislation** — a checklist concluding the three chapters: loose prior unity, no deep-rooted laws or superstitions, security from invasion, mutual acquaintance among members, self-sufficiency, moderate wealth, and an old people's steadiness joined to a new people's docility — a combination so rare that few States have good constitutions.

## Modern Translation

A body politic can be measured in two ways: by the extent of its territory, or by the number of its people. Between these two measurements there is a right ratio that makes a State truly great. People make the State, and territory sustains the people; the right ratio, then, is that the land should be enough to maintain the inhabitants, and that the inhabitants should be as many as the land can maintain. In this proportion lies the maximum strength of a given number of people. If there is too much land, guarding it is a burden, cultivation falls short, the land produces more than is needed, and defensive wars soon follow. If there is not enough, the State must depend on its neighbors for what it lacks, and offensive wars soon follow. Any people whose situation leaves it no choice except commerce or war is weak in itself: it depends on its neighbors and on circumstances, and its existence can never be anything but brief and uncertain. Either it conquers others and changes its situation, or it is conquered and becomes nothing. Only insignificance or greatness can keep it free.

No fixed ratio can be laid down between the extent of territory and the population adequate to it. This is partly because of differences in the quality of the land, its fertility, the nature of its products, and the influence of climate, and partly because of the different temperaments of the people who live on it — some consume little in a fertile country, while others on barren soil consume much. We must also take into account the greater or lesser fertility of the women, the conditions in each country that are more or less favorable to population growth, and the influence the legislator can hope to exert through his institutions. The legislator, then, should base his judgment not on what he sees but on what he foresees; he should dwell less on the population as it actually stands than on the size it ought naturally to reach. Finally, there are countless cases in which particular local circumstances demand or permit taking in more territory than seems necessary. So a State will spread widely in mountain country, where the natural products — woods and pasture — require less labor, where experience shows that women are more fertile than on the plains, and where a vast expanse of hillside yields only a small flat area that can be counted on for crops. By contrast, a State can draw in tightly on the coast, even amid rocks and nearly barren sand, because fishing largely makes up for what the land fails to produce, because the inhabitants need to cluster together to fend off pirates, and also because it is easier there to relieve the country of its surplus population by founding colonies.

To these conditions for lawgiving one more must be added — one that cannot replace any of the others, but without which they are all useless: the enjoyment of peace and plenty. The moment when a State organizes itself is, like the moment when a battalion forms up, the moment when the body is least able to resist and easiest to destroy. Resistance would be stronger in a time of total disorganization than in a moment of ferment, when everyone is preoccupied with his own standing rather than with the common danger. If war, famine, or sedition strikes at this critical time, the State is inevitably overthrown.

Not that many governments haven't been set up during such storms — but in those cases it is the governments themselves that destroy the State. Usurpers always bring about, or pick, troubled times so that they can use the public panic as cover to pass destructive laws that the people would never adopt in cold blood. The choice of moment is one of the surest ways to tell the work of the legislator from the work of the tyrant.

What people, then, is fit to receive laws? One that already finds itself bound together by some tie of origin, interest, or agreement, but has never yet borne the real yoke of law. One with no deeply ingrained customs or superstitions. One that does not fear being crushed by sudden invasion, and that can stay out of its neighbors' quarrels while still resisting any one of them alone, or enlisting one to repel another. One in which every member can be known to every other, and no one has to carry burdens too heavy for a man to bear. One that can do without other peoples, and that all other peoples can do without. One that is neither rich nor poor, but self-sufficient. And finally, one that combines the steadiness of an ancient people with the docility of a new one. What makes lawgiving difficult is less what has to be built than what has to be torn down; and what makes success so rare is that natural simplicity and social needs are almost impossible to find together. All these conditions, it is true, rarely coincide — and that is why few States have good constitutions.

> **Rousseau's note:** If there were two neighboring peoples, one of which could not do without the other, the situation would be very hard on the first and very dangerous for the second. In such a case, any wise nation would move quickly to free the other from its dependence. The Republic of Tlaxcala, enclosed within the Mexican Empire, chose to do without salt rather than buy it from the Mexicans — or even accept it from them as a gift. The Tlaxcalans were wise enough to see the trap hidden beneath such generosity. They kept their freedom, and that little State, shut up inside that great Empire, was in the end the instrument of its ruin.

There is still one country in Europe capable of receiving laws: Corsica. The valor and persistence with which that brave people has regained and defended its liberty well deserve that some wise man teach it how to preserve what it has won. I have a feeling that some day that little island will astonish Europe.
