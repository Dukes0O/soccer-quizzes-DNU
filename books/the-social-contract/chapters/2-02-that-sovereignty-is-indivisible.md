# Book II, Chapter II — That Sovereignty Is Indivisible

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter II
>
> [← II.I That Sovereignty Is Inalienable](2-01-that-sovereignty-is-inalienable.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.III Whether the General Will Is Fallible →](2-03-whether-the-general-will-is-fallible.md)

## Overview

Having argued in the previous chapter that sovereignty cannot be transferred, Rousseau now argues that it cannot be split up either — and for the same reason. Sovereignty is the exercise of the general will, and a will is either general or it is not: it belongs either to the whole people or only to a part. What the whole people declares is law; what a part declares is merely a particular will or an administrative act. So any scheme that carves sovereignty into pieces — legislative versus executive power, the powers of taxation, justice, war, domestic administration, foreign treaty — mistakes the nature of the thing being divided.

Rousseau's target is the political theory of his day, which he mocks for dismembering the body politic like a stage magician and reassembling it by sleight of hand. His diagnosis: theorists confuse sovereignty itself with its *emanations* — subordinate powers that merely execute what the sovereign will has decided. Declaring war, for instance, applies a law to a particular case; it does not make law, and so it is not an act of sovereignty. The chapter closes with a pointed case study of where muddled principles lead: Grotius and his translator Barbeyrac, each writing to flatter a king (Louis XIII and George I respectively), tie themselves in knots because the true principle — that rights belong to the people — was a truth their patrons did not want told.

## Key Concepts

- **Indivisibility of sovereignty** — Because sovereignty just is the general will in action, it cannot be parceled out. A will either belongs to the whole body of the people (and its declaration is law) or to only a part (and its declaration is merely a particular act).
- **Generality of the will** — The test that decides whether an act is sovereign. In his note, Rousseau adds that generality does not require unanimity, but it does require that every vote be counted: excluding anyone breaks the generality.
- **Emanations vs. parts of sovereignty** — The powers theorists treat as divisions of sovereignty (war, taxation, justice, foreign treaty) are not parts of it but subordinate offshoots — executions of a supreme will, not fractions of it.
- **Law vs. application of law** — An act of sovereignty makes law; acts like declaring war or making peace only apply a law to a particular case, and so belong to government (magistracy), not to the sovereign.
- **Theory in the service of power** — Rousseau's Grotius/Barbeyrac example shows how writers who abandon true principles to please kings end up inconsistent and evasive, because the honest conclusion would favor the people, who hand out no rewards.

## Modern Translation

Sovereignty is indivisible for the same reason that it is inalienable. A will either is general or it is not; it is the will of the people as a whole, or of only a part of them. In the first case, the declared will is an act of sovereignty and constitutes law. In the second, it is merely a particular will, or an act of administration — at most, a decree.

> **Rousseau's note:** For a will to be general, it does not always have to be unanimous; but every vote must be counted. Any exclusion destroys the generality.

Our political theorists, though, unable to divide sovereignty in principle, divide it by its objects: into force and will; into legislative power and executive power; into rights of taxation, justice, and war; into internal administration and the power to make foreign treaties. Sometimes they run all these pieces together, sometimes they pull them apart; they turn the sovereign into a fantastic creature made of stitched-together parts — as if they were assembling a man out of several bodies, one with eyes, one with arms, another with feet, and each with nothing else. We are told that the conjurers of Japan dismember a child before the eyes of the audience, then throw the pieces into the air one after another, and the child comes down alive and whole. The tricks of our political theorists are much the same: they first dismember the body politic with an illusion worthy of a fairground, and then reassemble it — how, no one knows.

This error comes from lacking precise ideas about sovereign authority, and from mistaking mere emanations of it for parts of it. For example, the acts of declaring war and making peace have been treated as acts of sovereignty; but they are not, because such acts do not constitute law — they only apply a law. Each is a particular act that decides how the law applies in a given case, as will become clear once we have defined what the word "law" means.

If we examined the other supposed divisions in the same way, we would find that whenever sovereignty appears to be divided, we are looking at an illusion: the rights taken to be parts of sovereignty are in fact all subordinate to it, and they always presuppose supreme wills whose execution they merely carry out.

It is impossible to overstate how much obscurity this lack of precision has cast over the conclusions of writers on political right whenever they have used their own principles to judge the respective rights of kings and peoples. Anyone can see, in chapters three and four of the first book of Grotius, how that learned man and his translator, Barbeyrac, entangle themselves and tie themselves up in their own sophistries, afraid of saying either too little or too much of what they really think, and of offending the interests they need to keep on their side. Grotius — a refugee in France, disgusted with his own country, and eager to flatter Louis XIII, to whom his book is dedicated — spares no effort to strip the people of all their rights and to hand those rights to kings by every artifice he can devise. That approach would have suited Barbeyrac too, who dedicated his translation to George I of England. But unfortunately the expulsion of James II — which Barbeyrac called his "abdication" — forced him into caution, into hedging and equivocating, to avoid making William out to be a usurper. If these two writers had adopted the true principles, all their difficulties would have vanished and they would have been consistent throughout; but then they would have had to tell a sorry truth, one that flattered no one but the people. And truth is no road to fortune: the people hand out no ambassadorships, no professorships, and no pensions.
