# Book I, Chapter II — The First Societies

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book I, Chapter II
>
> [← I.I Subject of the First Book](1-01-subject-of-the-first-book.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [I.III The Right of the Strongest →](1-03-the-right-of-the-strongest.md)

## Overview

Rousseau opens his search for the basis of legitimate authority by examining the family — the only society that is genuinely natural. Even there, he argues, the natural bond lasts only as long as children need their father for survival; if the family holds together after that, it does so by agreement, not by nature. The family thus becomes the first model of political society, but with a telling difference: a father is repaid by love, while a ruler is repaid only by the pleasure of commanding.

The rest of the chapter is a polemic against writers who justify domination. Grotius and Hobbes reason from existing facts to rights, a method that conveniently favors tyrants and reduces humanity to herds of cattle kept by rulers who guard them in order to devour them — the same logic Caligula used to conclude that kings are gods or that men are beasts. Aristotle's claim that some people are born for slavery mistakes effect for cause: slavery against nature came first, and only then produced slaves who seem suited to it by nature. Rousseau closes with a sarcastic dismissal of arguments from the sovereignty of Adam and Noah, comparing Adam's world-empire to Robinson Crusoe's rule over his empty island. The chapter clears away rival theories — paternal authority, natural inequality, and divine-hereditary right — so that only convention remains as a possible foundation for legitimate rule.

## Key Concepts

- **The family as the only natural society** — The one social bond nature itself creates, and even it dissolves once children can survive on their own; anything beyond that point rests on voluntary agreement (convention).
- **Self-preservation as man's first law** — Each person's primary duty is to himself; on reaching the age of judgment, each becomes the sole judge of how to preserve himself, and so his own master.
- **The family as the first model of the State** — Ruler corresponds to father, people to children; but the analogy breaks down because the ruler lacks a father's love and is compensated instead by the pleasure of commanding.
- **Establishing right by fact** — Grotius's method of inferring what is legitimate from what actually exists; Rousseau condemns it as the reasoning most favorable to tyrants.
- **Slavery against nature** — Against Aristotle: no one is born for slavery by nature; force created the first slaves, and cowardice perpetuated their condition until slaves came to love their servitude.

## Modern Translation

The most ancient of all societies, and the only natural one, is the family. Even there, children stay bound to their father only as long as they need him to survive. As soon as that need ends, the natural bond dissolves. The children, freed from the obedience they owed their father, and the father, freed from the care he owed his children, both return to independence. If they remain together, it is no longer by nature but by choice, and the family itself is then held together only by convention.

This shared liberty follows from human nature. Man's first law is to look after his own preservation; his first duties are to himself. As soon as he reaches the age of reason, he alone judges the best means of preserving himself, and so he becomes his own master.

The family, then, can be called the first model of political societies: the ruler corresponds to the father, the people to the children; and all of them, being born free and equal, give up their liberty only for their own advantage. The whole difference is this: in the family, the father's love for his children repays him for the care he takes of them, while in the State, the pleasure of commanding replaces the love that a ruler cannot feel for the peoples beneath him.

Grotius denies that all human power is established for the benefit of the governed, and he cites slavery as his example. His usual method of reasoning is always to establish right by appealing to fact. One could use a more logical method, but not one more favorable to tyrants.

> **Rousseau's note:** "Learned inquiries into public right are often only the history of past abuses; and troubling to study them too deeply is a profitless infatuation" ("Essay on the Interests of France in Relation to Its Neighbours," by the Marquis d'Argenson). This is exactly what Grotius has done.

According to Grotius, then, it is an open question whether the human race belongs to a hundred men, or those hundred men belong to the human race — and throughout his book he seems to lean toward the first alternative, which is also Hobbes's view. On this account, the human species is divided into so many herds of cattle, each with its own ruler, who guards them in order to devour them.

Just as a shepherd is of a nature superior to his flock, so the shepherds of men — their rulers — are of a nature superior to the peoples under them. This, Philo tells us, is how the Emperor Caligula reasoned, concluding with equal logic either that kings were gods or that men were beasts.

Caligula's reasoning comes to the same thing as that of Hobbes and Grotius. Aristotle, before any of them, had said that men are by no means naturally equal: some are born for slavery, others for dominion.

Aristotle was right — but he mistook the effect for the cause. Nothing is more certain than that every man born into slavery is born for slavery. Slaves lose everything in their chains, even the desire to escape them: they come to love their servitude, as the companions of Ulysses came to love their brutish condition. If there are slaves by nature, then, it is because there were first slaves against nature. Force made the first slaves, and their cowardice perpetuated the condition.

> **Rousseau's note:** See a short treatise of Plutarch's entitled "That Animals Reason."

I have said nothing of King Adam, or of Emperor Noah, father of the three great monarchs who divided up the universe among themselves, like the children of Saturn — with whom some scholars have identified them. I trust I will be duly thanked for my restraint; for, being a direct descendant of one of these princes, perhaps of the eldest branch, how do I know that an examination of titles might not prove me the legitimate king of the human race? In any case, there is no doubt that Adam was sovereign of the world, just as Robinson Crusoe was sovereign of his island, so long as he was its only inhabitant. And that empire had this advantage: the monarch, secure on his throne, had no rebellions, wars, or conspirators to fear.
