# Book II, Chapter I — That Sovereignty Is Inalienable

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter I
>
> [← I.IX Real Property](1-09-real-property.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.II That Sovereignty Is Indivisible →](2-02-that-sovereignty-is-indivisible.md)

## Overview

Opening Book II, Rousseau draws the first major consequence from the principles established in Book I: only the general will can direct the State toward the purpose for which it was founded — the common good. Societies were made necessary by the clash of private interests, but they were made possible by what those interests have in common, and it is that shared interest alone that legitimately governs a society.

From this Rousseau derives the chapter's title claim: sovereignty, being nothing but the exercise of the general will, can never be given away or transferred. Power can be delegated, but will cannot. A private will may happen to coincide with the general will at a given moment, but the coincidence can never be guaranteed or made permanent, because a will cannot bind itself for the future. It follows that a people that promises simply to obey a master thereby dissolves itself: the moment there is a master, there is no longer a sovereign, and the body politic ceases to exist. Rousseau closes with a qualification — rulers' commands can stand in for the general will so long as the sovereign people, free to oppose them, stays silent — which he promises to explain later.

## Key Concepts

- **Inalienability of sovereignty** — sovereignty is the exercise of the general will, and a will cannot be handed over to someone else; power may be transmitted, but will may not.
- **The common good as the State's object** — the point of agreement among clashing private interests is what forms the social bond, and society must be governed solely on the basis of that common interest.
- **Particular will vs. general will** — the private will tends by nature toward partiality (favoring oneself), the general will toward equality; any agreement between them is accidental and cannot be guaranteed to last.
- **A will cannot bind itself for the future** — the sovereign can say what it wills now, but cannot promise what it will will tomorrow; a people that promises simple obedience destroys itself as a people.
- **Consent by silence** — rulers' commands may pass for expressions of the general will as long as the sovereign, being free to oppose them, does not.

## Modern Translation

The first and most important conclusion from the principles we have laid down so far is this: only the general will can direct the State toward the purpose for which it was founded — the common good. If the clash of private interests made it necessary to establish societies, it was the agreement among those same interests that made it possible. What these different interests have in common is what forms the social bond; if there were no point on which they all agreed, no society could exist. And it is solely on the basis of this common interest that every society ought to be governed.

I maintain, then, that sovereignty — which is nothing other than the exercise of the general will — can never be alienated, and that the sovereign, which is nothing other than a collective being, can be represented only by itself. Power can indeed be transmitted, but will cannot.

In fact, while it is not impossible for a private will to agree with the general will on some point, it is at least impossible for that agreement to be lasting and constant; for the private will tends by its very nature toward partiality, while the general will tends toward equality. Still more impossible is any guarantee of such agreement — even if it always held, it would be the product not of design but of chance. The sovereign can certainly say, "Right now I will what this man wills, or at least what he says he wills"; but it cannot say, "Whatever he wills tomorrow, I too shall will" — because it is absurd for a will to bind itself for the future, and because no will can be obliged to consent to anything contrary to the good of the being who wills. So if the people promises simply to obey, by that very act it dissolves itself and loses what makes it a people. The moment there is a master, there is no longer a sovereign, and from that moment the body politic has ceased to exist.

This does not mean that the commands of rulers cannot pass for expressions of the general will, so long as the sovereign, being free to oppose them, offers no opposition. In such a case the universal silence is taken to imply the people's consent. This will be explained later on.
