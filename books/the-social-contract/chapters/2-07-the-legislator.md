# Book II, Chapter VII — The Legislator

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book II, Chapter VII
>
> [← II.VI Law](2-06-law.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.VIII The People →](2-08-the-people.md)

## Overview

Having argued in the previous chapter that a blind multitude cannot by itself frame the system of laws it needs, Rousseau now confronts the paradox of founding. The person who could design the right laws for a nation would need superhuman qualities: an intelligence that understands human passions without sharing them, and that works in one century for rewards that arrive in the next. "It would take gods to give men laws." The legislator is thus an extraordinary figure — the engineer who invents the political machine, as distinct from the prince, the mere mechanic who operates it — and his task amounts to nothing less than changing human nature: turning self-sufficient individuals into parts of a larger whole from which they draw their life and being.

Yet this near-divine founder must hold no power. His office is neither magistracy nor sovereignty; he drafts laws but has no right to enact them, since only the people's free vote can turn a proposal into law — whoever commands the laws must not command men, or his laws will merely serve his passions. Legislation therefore joins two seemingly incompatible things: a task beyond human strength, and, to accomplish it, an authority that is no authority at all. Worse, the people who must ratify the laws cannot yet understand them; the social spirit the laws are meant to create would have to exist before the laws themselves. Unable to use either force or argument, the legislator must "persuade without convincing" — which is why the founders of nations have always put their wisdom in the mouths of the gods. Rousseau insists this recourse to religion is no mere fraud: cheap tricks can fool a crowd for a moment, but only genuine wisdom founds institutions that endure, as the lasting laws of Moses and Muhammad prove. Religion, at the birth of nations, is the instrument of politics — not, as Warburton claimed, its partner in a common object.

## Key Concepts

- **The legislator** — the extraordinary founder who designs a people's institutions; he requires almost divine insight into human nature, yet his function lies entirely outside the constitution he creates.
- **Legislator vs. prince** — the legislator is the engineer who invents the machine of state; the prince is only the mechanic who sets it up and keeps it running.
- **Changing human nature** — founding a people means transforming each individual from a complete, solitary whole into a dependent part of a greater whole, exchanging his natural powers for powers usable only with others' help; legislation is most perfect when each citizen can do nothing without all the rest.
- **An authority that is no authority** — the drafter of laws has no right to enact them; only the general will, expressed in the people's free vote, can bind individuals, so the legislator's proposals carry no legal force of their own.
- **Persuading without convincing** — since the people cannot yet grasp the reasons for good laws (the effect would have to become the cause), the legislator appeals to divine authority, crediting the gods with his own wisdom so the people obey freely.
- **Religion as founding instrument** — at the origin of nations, religion serves as the tool of politics; but only real wisdom, not tricks and impostures, can make the bond last.

## Modern Translation

To discover the rules of society best suited to nations would require a superior intelligence: one that could see all of men's passions without feeling any of them; that had no connection to our nature yet knew it through and through; whose happiness did not depend on us, and yet was willing to occupy itself with ours; and finally, one that could look forward across the march of time to a distant glory — working in one century to enjoy the reward in the next. It would take gods to give men laws.

> **Rousseau's note:** A people becomes famous only when its legislation begins to decline. We do not know for how many centuries the system of Lycurgus made the Spartans happy before the rest of Greece took any notice of it.

What Caligula argued from the facts, Plato argued on the basis of right, in defining the civil or kingly man in the dialogue called the *Politicus*. But if great princes are rare, how much rarer are great legislators? The prince has only to follow the model that the legislator must invent. The legislator is the engineer who designs the machine; the prince is merely the mechanic who sets it up and makes it run. "At the birth of societies," says Montesquieu, "the rulers of republics establish the institutions; afterward, the institutions mold the rulers."

> **Rousseau's note:** Montesquieu, *The Greatness and Decadence of the Romans*, ch. I.

Anyone who dares to undertake the founding of a people's institutions must feel capable of changing human nature, so to speak: of transforming each individual — who by himself is a complete and solitary whole — into part of a greater whole from which he in some sense receives his life and his being; of altering man's constitution in order to strengthen it; and of substituting a partial and moral existence for the physical and independent existence nature has given us all. He must, in a word, take away man's own powers and give him in exchange powers that are foreign to him and that he cannot use without the help of others. The more completely those natural powers are destroyed, the greater and more lasting the acquired ones become, and the more solid and perfect the new institutions are. So if each citizen is nothing and can do nothing without all the rest, and if the power acquired by the whole equals or exceeds the sum of the natural powers of all the individuals, then legislation can be said to have reached the highest point of perfection it can attain.

The legislator holds an extraordinary position in the State in every respect. If his genius makes it extraordinary, his office makes it no less so — for it is neither magistracy nor sovereignty. This office, which sets up the republic, has no place anywhere in the republic's constitution. It is a special and superior function that has nothing in common with rule over men; for if the one who commands men ought not to command the laws, then the one who commands the laws ought not, any more, to command men. Otherwise his laws, made the servants of his passions, would often do nothing but perpetuate his injustices, and his private aims would inevitably corrupt the sanctity of his work.

When Lycurgus gave laws to his country, he began by giving up the throne. It was the custom of most Greek towns to entrust the framing of their laws to foreigners. The republics of modern Italy often followed this example; Geneva did the same, and profited by it. In its most prosperous period, Rome saw all the crimes of tyranny spring up again within it and came to the brink of destruction, because it had placed legislative authority and sovereign power in the same hands.

> **Rousseau's note:** Those who know Calvin only as a theologian badly underestimate the range of his genius. The codification of our wise edicts, in which he played a large part, does him no less honor than his *Institute*. Whatever revolution time may bring to our religion, so long as the spirit of patriotism and liberty still lives among us, the memory of this great man will be forever blessed.

Even so, the decemvirs themselves never claimed the right to pass any law on their own authority alone. "Nothing we propose to you," they told the people, "can become law without your consent. Romans, be yourselves the authors of the laws that are to make you happy."

The man who drafts the laws, then, has — or should have — no legislative right of his own; and the people cannot strip itself of this incommunicable right even if it wants to, because under the fundamental compact only the general will can bind individuals, and there is no way to be sure that a particular will conforms to the general will until it has been submitted to the free vote of the people. I have said this already, but it is worth repeating.

So in the work of legislation we find two things joined that seem incompatible: an undertaking too difficult for human powers, and, to carry it out, an authority that is no authority at all.

There is a further difficulty that deserves attention. Wise men who insist on speaking their own language to the common people, instead of the people's language, cannot make themselves understood. There are a thousand kinds of ideas that simply cannot be translated into popular speech. Conceptions that are too general and objects that are too remote are equally beyond its reach: each individual, with no taste for any plan of government except the one that suits his particular interest, finds it hard to see the advantages he stands to gain from the continual sacrifices that good laws impose. For a young people to be able to appreciate sound principles of political theory and follow the fundamental rules of statecraft, the effect would have to become the cause: the social spirit that the institutions are meant to create would have to preside over their very founding, and men would have to be, before the laws exist, what the laws are supposed to make them. The legislator, therefore, unable to appeal either to force or to reason, must have recourse to an authority of a different order — one that can compel without violence and persuade without convincing.

This is what has, in every age, driven the fathers of nations to resort to divine intervention and to credit the gods with their own wisdom — so that the peoples, submitting to the laws of the State as they submit to the laws of nature, and recognizing the same power at work in the creation of the city as in the creation of man, might obey freely and bear the yoke of the public happiness with docility.

It is this sublime reason, soaring far above the reach of ordinary men, whose decisions the legislator puts into the mouths of the immortals, in order to sway by divine authority those whom human prudence could never move. But not just anyone can make the gods speak, or get himself believed when he proclaims himself their interpreter. The legislator's great soul is the only miracle that can prove his mission. Any man can carve tablets of stone, buy an oracle, feign secret dealings with some divinity, train a bird to whisper in his ear, or find some other crude way of imposing on the people. A man whose skill goes no further than that may perhaps gather a band of fools around him, but he will never found an empire, and his extravagant claims will quickly perish with him. Empty tricks can form a passing bond; only wisdom can make it last. The Judaic law, which still endures, and the law of the child of Ishmael, which has ruled half the world for ten centuries, still proclaim today the greatness of the men who laid them down. And while the pride of philosophy or the blind spirit of faction sees in these men nothing but lucky impostors, the true political thinker admires, in the institutions they built, the great and powerful genius that presides over things made to endure.

> **Rousseau's note:** "In truth," says Machiavelli, "there has never been, in any country, an extraordinary legislator who has not had recourse to God; for otherwise his laws would not have been accepted. There are, in fact, many useful truths that a wise man may know without their carrying, in themselves, reasons clear enough to convince others" (*Discourses on Livy*, Bk. V, ch. XI). [Rousseau quotes the Italian.]

We should not conclude from all this, with Warburton, that among us politics and religion have a common object — only that, at the birth of nations, the one is used as the instrument of the other.
