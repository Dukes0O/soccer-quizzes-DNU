# Book I, Chapter IX — Real Property

> Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* (1762), trans. G. D. H. Cole · Book I, Chapter IX
>
> [← I.VIII The Civil State](1-08-the-civil-state.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [II.I That Sovereignty Is Inalienable →](2-01-that-sovereignty-is-inalienable.md)

## Overview

Closing Book I, Rousseau asks what happens to people's possessions when they enter the social contract. His answer: each member hands over everything, goods included, to the community — yet far from being robbed, possessors come out ahead. The state converts mere possession (a precarious grip based on being there first or being strongest) into genuine property, guaranteed by the whole community's force. Toward outsiders, the state itself holds its territory only by the right of the first occupier, which it inherits from its members.

Along the way Rousseau sets strict limits on that right of first occupancy: the land must be unoccupied, one may take only what one needs to live, and possession must be established by actual labor and cultivation — not by empty ceremony. His mockery of Balboa claiming the whole of South America for Castile by standing on a beach makes the point: planting a flag dispossesses no one.

The chapter — and the book — ends on the note that anchors everything to follow: the fundamental compact does not destroy natural inequality but replaces it. Whatever physical inequalities nature has created between people, the contract substitutes a moral and legitimate equality: people who are unequal in strength or intelligence become equal by convention and by right.

## Key Concepts

- **Right of the first occupier** — the claim to something because you got there first. More genuine than the "right of the strongest," but it only becomes a true right once property has been legally established; in the state of nature it is weak.
- **Possession vs. property** — before the contract, individuals merely *possess* things (by force or first occupancy); the social contract transforms this precarious possession into legitimate *property*, backed by the whole state.
- **Conditions of legitimate first occupancy** — the land must be uninhabited, one may occupy only what one needs to subsist, and title must be established by labor and cultivation, not by ceremony.
- **The community's overriding right** — every individual's right to his own land remains subordinate to the community's right over all land; without this, the social bond would have no stability and sovereignty no real force.
- **Equality by convention** — the compact's fundamental effect: physical inequality is replaced by moral and legal equality, so that people unequal in strength or talent become equal in rights.

## Modern Translation

Each member of the community gives himself to it at the moment it is founded, just as he is — with all his resources, including the goods he possesses. This act does not change the nature of possession as it changes hands; it does not become property in the hands of the Sovereign. But since the city's forces are incomparably greater than any individual's, public possession is in fact stronger and harder to revoke, without being any more legitimate — at least in the eyes of foreigners. For the State, in relation to its members, is master of all their goods through the social contract, which within the State is the basis of all rights; but in relation to other powers, it is master only by the right of the first occupier, which it derives from its members.

The right of the first occupier, though more genuine than the right of the strongest, becomes a true right only once the right of property has already been established. Every man naturally has a right to everything he needs; but the positive act that makes him owner of one thing excludes him from everything else. Once he has his share, he must keep to it, and he has no further claim against the community. This is why the right of the first occupier, so weak in the state of nature, commands the respect of everyone in civil society. In honoring this right, we are respecting not so much what belongs to someone else as what does not belong to us.

In general, to establish the right of the first occupier over a piece of land, the following conditions must be met: first, the land must not yet be inhabited; second, a man must occupy only as much as he needs to subsist; and third, possession must be taken not by an empty ceremony but by labor and cultivation — the only sign of ownership that others should be bound to respect in the absence of a legal title.

In granting the right of first occupancy to need and labor, haven't we stretched it as far as it can go? Can such a right be left without limits? Is setting foot on a piece of common ground enough to instantly make you its master? Is having the strength to drive others off for a moment enough to establish a right to keep them from ever coming back? How can a man or a people seize a vast territory and shut out the rest of the human race except by a criminal usurpation, since the act robs everyone else of a place to live and the means of subsistence that nature gave to all in common? When Núñez de Balboa stood on the shore and took possession of the South Seas and the whole of South America in the name of the crown of Castile, was that enough to dispossess all the people actually living there and to exclude every prince in the world? By that logic, such ceremonies multiply to no purpose, and the Catholic King could simply take possession of the entire universe all at once from his private apartments — merely carving out afterward whatever was already held by other princes.

We can see how the lands of individuals, once they bordered one another and were joined together, became public territory, and how the right of Sovereignty, extending from the subjects to the lands they held, became at once real and personal — a right over things as well as over people. This made the possessors more dependent and turned their very resources into a guarantee of their loyalty. Ancient monarchs do not seem to have appreciated this advantage: calling themselves King of the Persians, the Scythians, or the Macedonians, they apparently saw themselves as rulers of men rather than masters of a country. Today's monarchs are shrewder: they call themselves Kings of France, of Spain, of England, and so on. By holding the land, they are quite sure of holding its inhabitants.

The remarkable thing about this alienation is that when the community takes over the goods of individuals, far from stripping them of anything, it only secures their legitimate possession — turning usurpation into a genuine right, and mere use into ownership. The possessors, now regarded as trustees of the public good, with their rights respected by every member of the State and defended against foreign attack by all its forces, have — through a surrender that benefits the public and benefits themselves still more — acquired, so to speak, everything they gave up. This paradox is easily explained by the distinction between the rights the Sovereign and the owner have over the same estate, as we shall see later.

It can also happen that men begin to unite before they possess anything, and that afterward, taking over a tract of land sufficient for all, they either enjoy it in common or divide it among themselves — equally, or according to proportions set by the Sovereign. However the acquisition is made, each individual's right to his own estate is always subordinate to the community's right over everything. Without that, there would be no stability in the social bond and no real force in the exercise of Sovereignty.

I will end this chapter and this book with a remark that should serve as the foundation of the whole social system: instead of destroying natural inequality, the fundamental compact substitutes a moral and legitimate equality for whatever physical inequality nature may have set up between men. Men who may be unequal in strength or intelligence become equal, every one of them, by convention and by legal right.

> **Rousseau's note:** Under bad governments this equality is only apparent and illusory: it serves only to keep the poor man in his poverty and the rich man in the position he has usurped. In reality, laws are always useful to those who own things and harmful to those who have nothing. It follows that the social state is advantageous to men only when all have something and none has too much.
