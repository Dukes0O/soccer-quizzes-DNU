# Chapter XVI — Of Conquest

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 175–196
>
> [← Of Paternal, Political, and Despotical Power](15-of-paternal-political-and-despotical-power.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Usurpation →](17-of-usurpation.md)

## Overview

Having argued throughout the treatise that legitimate government can only be founded on the consent of the people, Locke now confronts the most common rival theory in practice: that conquest is itself an origin of government. His answer is blunt. Conquest is no more the founding of a government than demolishing a house is the building of a new one. War may clear the ground for a new commonwealth by destroying an old one, but it can never, by itself, erect one.

The chapter moves in two stages. First, the easy case: an unjust conqueror — an aggressor who wins — acquires no rights at all, any more than a robber acquires title to the deed he extorts at dagger-point. The only difference between a criminal and a conquering king is scale, and scale aggravates rather than excuses the crime. Since the conquered have no earthly court to appeal to, they may, like Jephthah, "appeal to heaven," and renew that appeal generation after generation until they recover their right.

Second, the hard case: a *lawful* conqueror, one who wins a just war. Locke systematically shrinks even this conqueror's rights. He gets no power over those who fought alongside him; no power over the people of the defeated country who did not actually join in the unjust force; and over those who did join, his power is "purely despotical" — absolute over their *lives*, because they forfeited them by making war, but it does not extend to their *possessions*. Estates belong also to innocent wives and children, and damages from war can almost never equal the value of a settled country's land, so conquest gives no title to territory. Nor does the conqueror's power pass to the next generation: children are born free, promises extorted by force bind no one, and descendants of a subjugated people always retain the right to throw off the yoke — as Hezekiah rightly rebelled against Assyria.

The chapter matters for the treatise's larger case in two ways. It demolishes the argument (popular among defenders of absolute monarchy) that the English crown holds absolute dominion by right of the Norman Conquest. And it prepares the ground for the chapters on usurpation, tyranny, and the dissolution of government: if even a just conqueror gains no lasting title without consent, then no ruler anywhere can rest his authority on mere force.

## Key Concepts

- **Conquest is not an origin of government** — Force can destroy a commonwealth but cannot create one. Only the consent of the people erects legitimate government; conquest at most "makes way" for a new one by demolishing the old.
- **The unjust conqueror as robber writ large** — An aggressor who wins an unjust war has exactly the title a burglar gets by putting a dagger to your throat: none. Crowned heads and petty villains commit the same crime; the great robbers merely go unpunished because they hold the power that should punish them.
- **Appeal to heaven** — Where the conquered have no court or arbitrator on earth, they (and their children after them) may appeal to God, as Jephthah did, and keep renewing that appeal until they recover their ancestors' native right to a legislative of their own choosing.
- **Despotical power and its strict limits** — Even a *just* conqueror's power is despotical only over the persons of those who actually used or abetted the unjust force against him. Their lives are forfeit; but non-combatants, children, and posterity are innocent and remain free.
- **Lives, not estates** — Forfeiture by war reaches a man's life, not his property. Force gives a right to destroy the aggressor as one would a dangerous beast, but only *damage sustained* gives title to another's goods — and even that claim yields to the prior title of innocent wives and children who would otherwise perish.
- **Why war damages can never buy a country** — In a fully settled land, the destruction a war causes (a few years' produce at most) cannot approach the value of perpetual inheritance in the land itself; money and treasure have only a "fantastical imaginary value." So reparation never justifies dispossessing the vanquished people's posterity forever.
- **Extorted consent is no consent** — Promises forced from someone bind not at all. Whatever is taken by force I retain the right to, just as handing my purse to a highwayman with a pistol at my chest transfers no right to it.
- **The double birthright** — Every person is born with (1) a right of freedom over his own person, which no one else can control, and (2) a right to inherit his father's goods. From these it follows that a conqueror's power dies with the men he subdued and that their descendants may always shake off a government imposed by the sword.

## Modern Translation

### § 175

Governments can originally arise in no other way than the one I have already described, and political societies can be founded on nothing but the consent of the people. Yet ambition has filled the world with such disorders that, amid the noise of war — which makes up so great a part of human history — this consent is scarcely noticed. As a result, many people have mistaken the force of arms for the consent of the people and count conquest as one of the origins of government. But conquest is as far from setting up any government as demolishing a house is from building a new one in its place. True, conquest often makes way for a new frame of commonwealth by destroying the old one; but without the consent of the people it can never erect a new one.

### § 176

An aggressor who puts himself into a state of war with another and unjustly invades another man's right can never, by such an unjust war, come to have any right over the conquered. Everyone will readily agree to this — everyone, that is, who does not think that robbers and pirates have a right of empire over whomever they have force enough to master, or that men are bound by promises which unlawful force extorts from them. If a robber breaks into my house and, with a dagger at my throat, makes me sign deeds conveying my estate to him, would that give him any title? That is exactly the title an unjust conqueror gets by his sword when he forces me into submission. The injury and the crime are equal whether committed by the wearer of a crown or by some petty villain. The rank of the offender and the number of his followers make no difference to the offense — unless it is to make it worse. The only difference is this: great robbers punish little ones to keep them obedient, while the great ones are rewarded with laurels and triumphs, because they are too big for the weak hands of justice in this world and hold in their own possession the very power that ought to punish offenders.

What is my remedy against a robber who breaks into my house like that? Appeal to the law for justice. But perhaps justice is denied me, or I am crippled and cannot stir, or robbed and lack the means to pursue it. If God has taken away every means of seeking a remedy, nothing is left but patience. But my son, when he is able, may seek the relief of the law that I was denied; he or his son may renew the appeal until he recovers his right. The conquered, however, and their children, have no court, no arbitrator on earth to appeal to. Then they may appeal, as Jephthah did, to heaven, and repeat their appeal until they have recovered the native right of their ancestors — which was to have over them such a legislative as the majority should approve and freely accept.

If someone objects that this would cause endless trouble, I answer: no more trouble than justice itself causes, wherever she lies open to all who appeal to her. A man who troubles his neighbor without cause is punished for it by the justice of the court he appeals to; and a man who appeals to heaven must be sure he has right on his side — and a right, moreover, worth the trouble and cost of the appeal, since he will answer for it at a tribunal that cannot be deceived, and that will be sure to repay everyone according to the harm he has done to his fellow subjects — that is, to any part of mankind. From all this it is plain that a man who conquers in an unjust war can thereby gain no title to the subjection and obedience of the conquered.

### § 177

But suppose victory favors the right side. Let us consider a conqueror in a lawful war, and see what power he gets, and over whom.

First: it is plain he gets no power by his conquest over those who conquered *with* him. Those who fought on his side cannot be made worse off by the conquest; they must be at least as free as they were before. And most commonly they serve on terms, on condition of sharing with their leader and enjoying a part of the spoils and the other advantages that attend the conquering sword — or at least of having a part of the subdued country bestowed on them. The conquering people are not, I hope, to be made slaves by conquest, wearing their laurels only to show that they are sacrifices to their leader's triumph. Those who found absolute monarchy on the title of the sword turn their heroes — the founders of such monarchies — into outright Drawcansirs, and forget that these men had officers and soldiers who fought on their side in the battles they won, who helped them subdue the countries they mastered, and who shared in possessing them.

We are told by some that the English monarchy is founded on the Norman Conquest, and that our princes thereby have a title to absolute dominion. Even if that were true (and the historical record shows otherwise), and even if William had a right to make war on this island, still his dominion by conquest could reach no further than the Saxons and Britons who then inhabited this country. The Normans who came with him and helped him conquer, and everyone descended from them, are freemen, not subjects by conquest — whatever dominion conquest may be supposed to give. And if I, or anyone else, claim freedom as descended from them, it will be very hard to prove the contrary. And it is plain that the law, which has made no distinction between the one group and the other, does not intend there to be any difference in their freedom or privileges.

### § 178

But let us suppose — what seldom happens — that the conquerors and the conquered never incorporate into one people under the same laws and freedom. Let us see, next, what power a lawful conqueror has over the subdued. And that power, I say, is purely despotical. He has an absolute power over the lives of those who, by waging an unjust war, have forfeited them; but not over the lives or fortunes of those who took no part in the war, nor over the possessions even of those who actually did.

### § 179

Secondly, then, I say the conqueror gets power only over those who actually assisted in, concurred in, or consented to the unjust force used against him. For the people never gave their governors any power to do an unjust thing — such as making an unjust war — since they never had such a power in themselves. So they ought not to be charged as guilty of the violence and injustice committed in an unjust war any further than they actually abet it; no more than they should be thought guilty of any violence or oppression their governors inflict on the people themselves, or on any part of their fellow subjects — for they empowered them no more to the one than to the other. Conquerors, it is true, seldom trouble themselves to make this distinction; they willingly let the confusion of war sweep everything together. But this does not alter the right. The conqueror's power over the lives of the conquered exists only because they used force to commit or to maintain an injustice; therefore he can have that power only over those who joined in that force. All the rest are innocent. Over the people of that country who have done him no injury — and so have made no forfeit of their lives — he has no more title than he has over anyone else who has lived with him on fair terms, without injury or provocation.

### § 180

Thirdly: the power a conqueror gets over those he overcomes in a just war is perfectly despotical. He has an absolute power over the lives of those who, by putting themselves into a state of war, have forfeited them; but he does not thereby get a right and title to their possessions. I have no doubt this will at first sight seem a strange doctrine, being so contrary to the practice of the world — nothing is more familiar, when speaking of who rules a country, than to say that so-and-so *conquered* it, as if conquest, without anything more, conveyed a right of possession. But the practice of the strong and powerful, however universal it may be, is seldom the rule of right — even though one part of the subjection of the conquered is that they are in no position to argue against the terms carved out for them by the conquering sword.

### § 181

In every war there is usually a tangle of force and damage together, and the aggressor rarely fails to harm the property of those he makes war on when he uses force against their persons. Yet it is the use of *force* alone that puts a man into the state of war. Whether he begins the injury by force, or, having done the injury quietly and by fraud, refuses to make reparation and maintains it by force (which is the same as having done it by force in the first place), it is the unjust use of force that makes the war. A man who breaks open my house and violently throws me out of doors, and a man who gets in peaceably and then keeps me out by force, do in effect the same thing — supposing we are in a condition where we have no common judge on earth to whom I can appeal and to whom we are both bound to submit; for that is the situation I am speaking of. It is the unjust use of force, then, that puts a man into the state of war with another; and by it, the one guilty of it forfeits his life. For by quitting reason — which is the rule given between man and man — and using force, the way of beasts, he becomes liable to be destroyed by the man he uses force against, like any savage ravenous beast that is dangerous to his existence.

### § 182

But the wrongdoings of the father are no faults of the children, who may be rational and peaceable despite the brutishness and injustice of the father. So the father, by his misdeeds and violence, can forfeit only his own life; he does not involve his children in his guilt or destruction. His goods — which nature, willing the preservation of all mankind as far as possible, has made to belong to the children, to keep them from perishing — continue to belong to his children. Supposing they did not join in the war, whether through infancy, absence, or choice, they have done nothing to forfeit those goods; nor has the conqueror any right to take them away by the bare title of having subdued the man who tried by force to destroy him. He may perhaps have *some* right to them, to repair the damages he sustained in the war and in the defense of his own right — how far that reaches into the possessions of the conquered we shall see shortly. So a man who by conquest has a right over another's *person*, to destroy him if he pleases, does not thereby get a right over his *estate*, to possess and enjoy it. It is the brutal force the aggressor used that gives his adversary a right to take away his life and destroy him, if he pleases, as a noxious creature; but it is *damage sustained* that alone gives title to another man's goods. Though I may kill a thief who attacks me on the highway, I may not (which seems the lesser thing) take away his money and let him go — that would be robbery on my part. His force, and the state of war he put himself in, made him forfeit his life, but gave me no title to his goods. The right of conquest, then, extends only to the lives of those who joined in the war; not to their estates, except so far as is needed to make reparation for the damages received and the costs of the war — and even that with a reservation for the right of the innocent wife and children.

### § 183

Let the conqueror have as much justice on his side as can possibly be supposed: he still has no right to seize more than the vanquished man could forfeit. The man's life is at the victor's mercy, and the victor may appropriate his service and his goods to make himself reparation; but he cannot take the goods of the man's wife and children. They too had a title to the goods he enjoyed, and their shares in the estate he possessed. For example: suppose I, in the state of nature (and all commonwealths are in the state of nature with one another), have injured another man, and, refusing to give satisfaction, it comes to a state of war, in which my defending by force what I got unjustly makes me the aggressor. I am conquered. My life, it is true, being forfeit, is at his mercy — but not my wife's and children's. They did not make the war, nor assist in it. I could not forfeit their lives; they were not mine to forfeit. My wife had a share in my estate; that too I could not forfeit. And my children, being born of me, had a right to be maintained out of my labor or my substance.

Here, then, is the case: the conqueror has a title to reparation for damages received, and the children have a title to their father's estate for their subsistence. (As for the wife's share, whether it was her own labor or a marriage contract that gave her title to it, it is plain her husband could not forfeit what was hers.) What must be done in this case? I answer: since the fundamental law of nature is that all, as far as possible, should be preserved, it follows that if there is not enough to satisfy both claims in full — the conqueror's losses and the children's maintenance — then the one who has enough and to spare must give up something of his full satisfaction, and yield to the pressing and preferable title of those who are in danger of perishing without it.

### § 184

But suppose the costs and damages of the war are to be made good to the conqueror to the last farthing, and that the children of the vanquished, stripped of all their father's goods, are to be left to starve and perish. Even so, paying off everything owed to the conqueror on this account will scarcely give him a title to any country he conquers. For the damages of war can hardly amount to the value of any considerable tract of land in any part of the world where all the land is owned and none lies vacant. And if I have not taken away the conqueror's land — which, being the one vanquished, I could not possibly have done — then scarcely any other damage I have done him can amount to the value of *my* land, supposing his is equally cultivated and anywhere near the extent of what I overran of his. The destruction of a year's produce or two — it seldom reaches four or five — is the utmost damage that can usually be done. As for money and other such riches and treasure carried off, these are none of nature's goods; they have only a fantastical, imaginary value. Nature has put no such value on them; by her standard they count for no more than the wampum of the Americans counts to a European prince, or the silver money of Europe would once have counted to an American.

Five years' produce is not worth the perpetual inheritance of land in a country where all is owned and none remains vacant to be taken up by the man who is dispossessed. This will be readily granted once you strip away the imaginary value of money — the disproportion being more than five to five hundred. (At the same time, half a year's produce is worth *more* than the inheritance of land where there is more land than the inhabitants own and use, and anyone is free to take up the vacant ground; but in such places conquerors take little care to seize the lands of the vanquished.) No damage, then, that men in the state of nature suffer from one another (and all princes and governments are in the state of nature with respect to one another) can give a conqueror the power to dispossess the posterity of the vanquished and turn them out of the inheritance which ought to be the possession of them and their descendants for all generations. The conqueror, indeed, will be apt to think himself master; and it is the very condition of the subdued that they are unable to contest their right. But if that is all, it gives no other title than what bare force gives the stronger over the weaker — and by that reasoning, whoever is strongest will have a right to whatever he pleases to seize.

### § 185

Over those, then, who joined with him in the war, over those inhabitants of the subdued country who did not oppose him, and over the posterity even of those who did, the conqueror — even in a just war — has by his conquest no right of dominion. They are free from any subjection to him; and if their former government is dissolved, they are at liberty to begin and erect another for themselves.

### § 186

The conqueror, it is true, usually compels them by the force he holds over them — with a sword at their breasts — to stoop to his conditions and submit to whatever government he pleases to grant them. But the question is: what *right* does he have to do so? If it is said that they submit by their own consent, then this concedes that their consent is necessary to give the conqueror a title to rule over them. It only remains to consider whether promises extorted by force, without right, can count as consent, and how far they bind. To that I say: they bind not at all. Whatever another gets from me by force, I still retain the right to, and he is bound to restore it immediately. A man who takes my horse from me by force ought immediately to return him, and I still have the right to take him back. By the same reasoning, a man who forced a promise from me ought immediately to give it back — that is, release me from its obligation — or I may take it back myself, that is, choose whether I will perform it. For the law of nature, which lays obligations on me only by the rules she prescribes, cannot bind me through a violation of her rules; and extorting anything from me by force is exactly that. Nor does it change the case at all to say "but I gave my promise" — any more than it excuses the force, and passes title, when I put my hand in my own pocket and hand over my purse myself to a thief who demands it with a pistol at my breast.

### § 187

From all of this it follows that the government of a conqueror, imposed by force on people he had no right of war against — or on people who did not join in the war against him, where he did have such a right — lays no obligation on them at all.

### § 188

But let us suppose that all the men of that community, being all members of the same body politic, can be taken to have joined in the unjust war in which they were subdued, so that their lives are at the mercy of the conqueror.

### § 189

I say this does not touch their children who are minors. Since a father does not have in himself any power over the life or liberty of his child, no act of his can possibly forfeit it. So the children, whatever may have happened to their fathers, are freemen; and the absolute power of the conqueror reaches no further than the persons of the men he subdued, and dies with them. Even if he governs those men as slaves, subject to his absolute arbitrary power, he has no such right of dominion over their children. He can have power over the children only by their own consent, whatever he may drive them to say or do; and he has no lawful authority so long as force, and not choice, compels them to submit.

### § 190

Every man is born with a double right. First, a right of freedom over his own person, which no other man has power over — the free disposal of it lies in himself alone. Second, a right, before any other man, to inherit, along with his brothers, his father's goods.

### § 191

By the first of these, a man is naturally free from subjection to any government, even though he was born in a place under its jurisdiction. But if he disclaims the lawful government of the country he was born in, he must also give up the rights that belonged to him under its laws, and the possessions descending to him there from his ancestors — provided it was a government made by their consent.

### § 192

By the second, the inhabitants of any country who are descended from, and derive title to their estates from, people who were subdued and had a government forced on them against their free consent, retain the right to the possessions of their ancestors even though they do not freely consent to the government whose harsh terms were imposed by force on the possessors of that country. For since the first conqueror never had any title to the land of that country, the people who are descended from — or who claim under — those who were forced to submit to the yoke of a government by constraint always have a right to shake it off, and to free themselves from the usurpation or tyranny the sword has brought in upon them, until their rulers put them under a frame of government to which they willingly and by choice consent. Who doubts that the Greek Christians, descendants of the ancient possessors of that country, may justly cast off the Turkish yoke they have groaned under so long, whenever they have the opportunity to do it? For no government can have a right to obedience from a people who have not freely consented to it — and they can never be supposed to have done so until either they are put in a full state of liberty to choose their government and governors, or at least until they have standing laws to which they, by themselves or their representatives, have given their free consent; and also until they are allowed their due property — that is, until they are such proprietors of what they have that no one can take away any part of it without their own consent. Without this, men under any government are not in the condition of freemen but are outright slaves under the force of war.

### § 193

But even granting that a conqueror in a just war has a right to the estates, as well as power over the persons, of the conquered — which, plainly, he does not — no absolute power follows from this in the continued course of the government. For the descendants of the conquered are all freemen; and if he grants them estates and possessions so that they may inhabit his country (without which it would be worth nothing to him), then whatever he grants them, they have, so far as it is granted, *property* in. And the very nature of property is that it cannot be taken from a man without his own consent.

### § 194

Their persons are free by native right, and their properties, be they larger or smaller, are their own, at their own disposal and not at his — otherwise it is not property at all. Suppose the conqueror gives one man a thousand acres, to him and his heirs forever; and to another he leases a thousand acres for life, at a rent of £50 or £500 a year. Does not the first have a right to his thousand acres forever, and the second, for life, so long as he pays the rent? And does not the tenant for life have property in everything he earns over and above his rent by his labor and industry during that term — suppose it comes to double the rent? Can anyone say that the king, or conqueror, after making his grant, may by his power as conqueror take away all or part of the land from the heirs of the one, or from the other during his life while he pays the rent? Or that he can take from either, at his pleasure, the goods or money they have earned on that land? If he can, then all free and voluntary contracts cease and are void in the world; nothing is needed to dissolve them at any moment but sufficient power; and all the grants and promises of men in power are mere mockery and trickery. For can anything be more ridiculous than to say: "I give this to you and yours forever, and by the surest and most solemn form of conveyance that can be devised" — and yet to have it understood that I have the right, if I please, to take it away from you again tomorrow?

### § 195

I will not dispute now whether princes are exempt from the laws of their own country. But of this I am sure: they owe subjection to the laws of God and nature. No body, no power, can exempt them from the obligations of that eternal law. Those obligations are so great and so strong in the matter of promises that omnipotence itself can be bound by them. Grants, promises, and oaths are bonds that hold the Almighty — whatever some flatterers may tell the princes of the world, who, all of them together, with all their people joined to them, are, in comparison with the great God, but as a drop of the bucket, or a speck of dust on the scales: insignificant, nothing!

### § 196

The short of the matter regarding conquest is this. The conqueror, if he had a just cause, has a despotical right over the persons of all who actually aided and concurred in the war against him, and a right to recover his damages and costs out of their labor and estates — so long as he does not injure the right of anyone else. Over the rest of the people, if there were any who did not consent to the war, and over the children of the captives themselves, and over the possessions of either, he has no power. And so he can have, by virtue of conquest, no lawful title of dominion over them himself, nor pass one down to his posterity. On the contrary, he is an aggressor if he makes an attempt on their properties, and thereby puts himself into a state of war against them; and he has no better right of rule — neither he nor any of his successors — than Hingar or Hubba, the Danes, had here in England, or than Spartacus would have had if he had conquered Italy: that is, a right fit to have its yoke thrown off as soon as God gives those under their subjection the courage and the opportunity to do it. Thus, whatever title the kings of Assyria had over Judah by the sword, God assisted Hezekiah in throwing off the dominion of that conquering empire: "And the Lord was with Hezekiah, and he prospered; therefore he went forth, and he rebelled against the king of Assyria, and served him not" (2 Kings 18:7). From this it is plain that shaking off a power which force, and not right, has set over anyone — though it bears the name of rebellion — is no offense before God, but is something he allows and approves, even where promises and covenants obtained by force have intervened. For it is very probable, to anyone who reads the story of Ahaz and Hezekiah attentively, that the Assyrians subdued Ahaz and deposed him, and made Hezekiah king during his father's lifetime; and that Hezekiah, by agreement, had done him homage and paid him tribute all that time.
