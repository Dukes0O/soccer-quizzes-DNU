# Chapter I — Introduction

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 1–3
>
> [← Preface](00-preface.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the State of Nature →](02-of-the-state-of-nature.md)

## Overview

This opening chapter carries the treatise's full title as its heading: "An Essay Concerning the True Original, Extent and End of Civil Government." Standard editions call it the Introduction, and that is what it is — a bridge from the *First Treatise* to the constructive argument that follows.

Locke begins by recapping, in four numbered points, what the *First Treatise* demolished: Sir Robert Filmer's theory that all political authority descends from the dominion God supposedly gave Adam. Even granting Filmer one step at a time — that Adam had such authority, that his heirs inherited it, that a rightful heir could be identified — the chain breaks at every link, and in any case the eldest line of Adam's descendants is irrecoverably lost. No ruler on earth, therefore, can claim the least shred of authority from that source. Locke then turns the tables on Filmer's followers: unless we want to conclude that all government is nothing but force and violence — the very charge they hurl at their opponents — we must find a different origin for political power and a different way of identifying who rightfully holds it.

That sets the agenda for the whole *Second Treatise*. Locke proposes to start by defining political power precisely, so that the authority of a magistrate over a subject is not confused with the other kinds of authority one person can have over another — father over child, master over servant, husband over wife, lord over slave. Section 3 delivers the famous working definition: political power is the right to make laws backed by penalties up to and including death, for the regulation and preservation of property, together with the right to use the community's force to execute those laws and defend the commonwealth — all of it only for the public good. Every subsequent chapter unpacks, justifies, and limits the elements of this definition.

## Key Concepts

- **Refutation of Filmer's patriarchalism** — Locke's four-step summary of the *First Treatise*: Adam had no God-given dominion; even if he had, his heirs had no right to it; even if they had, no law identifies the right heir; and even if it did, knowledge of Adam's eldest line is utterly lost. Present rulers can derive no authority from "Adam's private dominion and paternal jurisdiction."
- **The alternative to force** — If Filmer's account fails and no other origin of government is found, the only remaining conclusion is that government rests on force and violence, with men living by the rule of beasts, "where the strongest carries it" — a recipe for perpetual disorder, sedition, and rebellion. Avoiding that conclusion is the motive for the whole inquiry.
- **The "original" of political power** — Locke's stated task: to find another rise of government, another source of political power, and another way of designating and knowing who rightfully holds it.
- **Distinguishing kinds of power** — Political power (magistrate over subject) must be separated from paternal power (father over child), despotical power (lord over slave), and the power of master over servant and husband over wife. One man may hold several of these at once, but they are distinct in kind — a ruler of a commonwealth is not the same as a father of a family or a captain of a galley.
- **Political power defined** — A *right* (not mere strength) of making laws with penalties of death and all lesser penalties, for regulating and preserving property; of employing the community's force to execute those laws and to defend the commonwealth against foreign injury; and all of this only for the public good.

## Modern Translation

### § 1

In the preceding treatise I showed the following:

(1) Adam did not have — either by any natural right of fatherhood or by any express grant from God — the kind of authority over his children or dominion over the world that is claimed for him.

(2) Even if he had possessed it, his heirs still had no right to it.

(3) Even if his heirs had possessed a right to it, there is no law of nature and no positive law of God that determines who the rightful heir is in every case that might arise, so the right of succession — and with it the right to rule — could never have been settled with certainty.

(4) Even if that had been settled, the knowledge of which line is the eldest branch of Adam's descendants was lost so long ago that, among all the races of mankind and families of the world, no one has the slightest claim over anyone else to be the eldest house and to hold the right of inheritance.

Since all these points have, I think, been clearly established, it is impossible for the rulers now on earth to gain any benefit, or to derive even the faintest shadow of authority, from what is claimed to be the fountain of all power: Adam's private dominion and paternal jurisdiction. So anyone who does not want to give real grounds for thinking that all government in the world is merely the product of force and violence, and that men live together by no other rules than those of beasts — where the strongest wins — thereby laying a foundation for perpetual disorder and harm, tumult, sedition, and rebellion (the very things the followers of that hypothesis protest against so loudly), must necessarily find another origin of government, another source of political power, and another way of designating and identifying the persons who hold it than what Sir Robert Filmer has taught us.

### § 2

To that end, I think it will be useful to set down what I understand political power to be, so that the power of a MAGISTRATE over a subject can be distinguished from that of a FATHER over his children, a MASTER over his servant, a HUSBAND over his wife, and a LORD over his slave. Since all these distinct powers are sometimes found together in the same man, considering him under these different relations may help us tell these powers apart from one another and show the difference between a ruler of a commonwealth, a father of a family, and a captain of a galley.

### § 3

POLITICAL POWER, then, I take to be a RIGHT of making laws — with penalties of death, and consequently all lesser penalties — for regulating and preserving property; and of employing the force of the community to execute such laws and to defend the commonwealth from foreign injury; and all of this only for the public good.
