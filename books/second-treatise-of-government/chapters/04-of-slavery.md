# Chapter IV — Of Slavery

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 22–24
>
> [← Of the State of War](03-of-the-state-of-war.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Property →](05-of-property.md)

## Overview

In this short chapter Locke defines liberty — both natural liberty and liberty under government — and then uses that definition to show why slavery, in the strict sense, can never rest on consent. He opens by rejecting Sir Robert Filmer's caricature of freedom as everyone doing whatever they please. True freedom, for Locke, is life under a known, standing rule: the law of nature in the natural state, or laws made by a consented-to legislature in civil society. Freedom is precisely the opposite of being subject to another person's arbitrary will.

The argument then turns on a striking premise: because no one has absolute power over their own life, no one can transfer such power to anyone else. You cannot sell yourself into slavery, because you cannot give what you do not have. Legitimate slavery, if it exists at all, arises only one way — as the continuation of a just state of war, where a lawful conqueror, who could rightly kill an aggressor who has forfeited his life, instead delays the killing and puts the captive to work. The moment master and captive strike any agreement — limited power on one side, obedience on the other — the state of war ends and so does slavery.

The chapter closes by defusing an obvious biblical objection: the Israelites "sold themselves." Locke insists this was contractual servitude (drudgery), not slavery, since the master had no power to kill or even maim the servant, and had to release him at a set time. The chapter matters structurally: it completes Locke's account of the state of war and clears the ground for Chapter V on property, while also planting the anti-absolutist seed of the whole treatise — if a person cannot consent to absolute, arbitrary power over himself, then no people can consent to an absolute, arbitrary government.

## Key Concepts

- **Natural liberty** — Freedom from any superior power on earth: not being under any human will or legislative authority, with only the law of nature as one's rule.
- **Liberty in society (civil liberty)** — Being under no legislative power except one established by consent in the commonwealth, and under no will or law except what that legislature enacts according to its trust. It includes a standing, common rule to live by and the liberty to follow one's own will wherever the rule is silent.
- **Freedom vs. license** — Against Filmer, freedom is not doing whatever one pleases unbound by any law; it is exemption from the inconstant, uncertain, unknown, arbitrary will of another person.
- **Inalienability of life** — A person does not hold absolute power over his own life, so he cannot by compact or consent enslave himself or place himself under another's absolute, arbitrary power. No one can give more power than he has.
- **Forfeiture** — The one legitimate ground of slavery: by an act deserving death, a person forfeits his life; the injured party may delay taking it and use the offender's service instead, doing him no wrong.
- **Slavery as continued state of war** — Perfect slavery is nothing but the state of war continued between a lawful conqueror and a captive; any compact between them ends both the war and the slavery for as long as it holds.
- **Drudgery vs. slavery** — Selling oneself into service (as among the Jews, Exodus 21) creates limited servitude, not slavery: the master cannot kill or even maim the servant, and must free him at the appointed time.

## Modern Translation

### § 22

Man's natural liberty means being free from any superior power on earth: not being under the will or legislative authority of any human being, and having only the law of nature as one's rule. Man's liberty in society means being under no legislative power except the one established by consent in the commonwealth, and under no one's dominion, and restrained by no law, except what that legislature enacts according to the trust placed in it.

Freedom, then, is not what Sir Robert Filmer tells us it is (*Observations*, A. 55): a liberty for everyone to do whatever he pleases, to live as he likes, and to be bound by no laws. Rather, the freedom of people under government means having a standing rule to live by, common to every member of that society and made by the legislative power set up in it — a liberty to follow my own will in everything the rule does not address, and not to be subject to the inconstant, uncertain, unknown, arbitrary will of another man. In the same way, natural freedom means being under no restraint except the law of nature.

### § 23

This freedom from absolute, arbitrary power is so necessary to a man's preservation, and so closely bound up with it, that he cannot give it up except by giving up his preservation and his life along with it. Since a man does not have power over his own life, he cannot — by compact or by his own consent — enslave himself to anyone, or put himself under another's absolute, arbitrary power to take his life whenever that other person pleases. Nobody can give more power than he himself has; and someone who cannot take away his own life cannot give another person power over it.

There is, however, one case. If a man has forfeited his own life by his own fault, through some act that deserves death, then the person to whom he has forfeited it may (once he has him in his power) delay taking it and make use of him for his own service — and in doing so does him no wrong. For whenever the slave finds the hardship of his slavery outweighing the value of his life, it is within his power, by resisting his master's will, to bring on himself the death he desires.

### § 24

This is the perfect condition of slavery, which is nothing other than the state of war continued between a lawful conqueror and a captive. For if a compact is ever made between them — an agreement for limited power on one side and obedience on the other — the state of war and the slavery cease for as long as the compact lasts. For, as I have said, no man can transfer to another by agreement something he does not possess himself: a power over his own life.

I admit that among the Jews, as well as other nations, we find that men sold themselves. But it is plain this was only into drudgery, not into slavery. It is evident the person sold was not under an absolute, arbitrary, despotical power: the master could not have had the power to kill, at any time, someone he was obliged to set free from his service at a fixed time. Far from having arbitrary power over such a servant's life, the master could not, at his pleasure, so much as maim him — the loss of an eye or a tooth set the servant free (Exodus 21).
