# Chapter XV — Of Paternal, Political, and Despotical Power, Considered Together

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 169–174
>
> [← Of Prerogative](14-of-prerogative.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Conquest →](16-of-conquest.md)

## Overview

This short chapter is a deliberate recapitulation. Locke has already treated paternal power (Chapter VI), political power (Chapters VII–IX), and despotical power (Chapter IV and elsewhere) separately, but he pauses here to set all three side by side. His stated reason is diagnostic: the "great mistakes of late about government" — above all the absolutist arguments of writers like Filmer — arise from confusing these three quite different kinds of power with one another. If a king's authority is modeled on a father's, or if political rule is treated as absolute dominion over subjects' lives, the confusion has already done its damage.

The chapter's method is to distinguish each power by its source, its purpose, and its limits. Paternal power comes from nature; it exists only for the child's good during minority and ends when the child reaches the use of reason, never extending to the child's life or property. Political power comes from voluntary agreement — the consent of those who form the community; it exists for the subjects' good, namely the preservation of their lives, liberties, and possessions, and therefore can never be absolute or arbitrary. Despotical power comes from forfeiture: only an aggressor who abandons reason and makes war on another forfeits his life and can justly be held under absolute power, and even then that condition is simply the state of war continued — it rests on no compact and dissolves the moment a compact is made.

The closing sections drive the political point home. Paternal power falls as far short of the magistrate's power as despotical power exceeds it, and absolute dominion — wherever it is lodged — is not a form of civil society at all but its contradiction, "as inconsistent with it as slavery is with property." The chapter thus supplies the conceptual hinge between Locke's account of legitimate government and the chapters that follow on conquest, usurpation, and tyranny: each of those is a case of one power masquerading as, or displacing, another.

## Key Concepts

- **Paternal (parental) power** — The natural authority parents have over children, given solely for the children's good during their minority, until they reach the use of reason. It never extends to the child's life or property, and once the child is grown it leaves only duties of honor, gratitude, and support — not subjection.
- **Political power** — The power each person had in the state of nature to preserve his property and punish breaches of the law of nature, given up to society by consent. In the magistrate's hands it can aim only at preserving members' lives, liberties, and possessions; it is a power to make laws with penalties, never an absolute or arbitrary power.
- **Despotical power** — Absolute, arbitrary power of one man over another's life. Nature does not grant it and no compact can convey it (no one has arbitrary power over his own life to give away); it arises only from forfeiture, when an aggressor puts himself into a state of war.
- **Forfeiture** — The act by which an unjust aggressor loses the protection of the law of nature. By quitting reason and using force, he "revolts to the state of beasts" and may be destroyed or enslaved like a dangerous animal; this is the sole origin of legitimate despotical power.
- **The state of war continued** — Locke's characterization of lawful slavery: the relation between a master and a captive taken in a just war. Because the captive is not master of his own life, no compact is possible between them; the moment any compact is made, slavery — and the master's absolute power — ceases.
- **Property (broad sense)** — Locke explicitly notes that "property" here, as elsewhere in the treatise, means the property people have in their persons as well as in their goods. Each of the three powers maps onto a condition of property: minority (unable to manage it), civil society (having it at one's own disposal), and slavery (stripped of it entirely).
- **Consent/compact as the origin of political power** — Political power arises only from the mutual agreement of those who make up the community; this is what distinguishes it in kind, not merely degree, from both paternal rule and despotism.

## Modern Translation

### § 169

Although I have already had occasion to discuss each of these powers separately, the great mistakes made recently about government have, I believe, arisen from confusing these distinct powers with one another. So it may be worthwhile to consider them here together.

### § 170

First, then: paternal or parental power is nothing more than the authority parents have over their children, to govern them for the children's own good until they come to the use of reason — that is, to a state of understanding in which they can be presumed capable of grasping the rule they must govern themselves by, whether that rule is the law of nature or the enacted law of their country. Capable, I mean, of knowing it as well as the various other people who live as free men under that same law. The affection and tenderness that God has planted in parents' hearts toward their children makes it obvious that this power was never intended to be a harsh, arbitrary government, but exists only for the help, instruction, and preservation of their offspring. But however that may be, there is — as I have proved — no reason to think this power ever extends to life and death over their children, any more than over anyone else. Nor is there any ground for claiming that parental power keeps a child, once grown to adulthood, in subjection to his parents' will — beyond this: that having received life and education from his parents obliges him to respect, honor, gratitude, assistance, and support toward both father and mother for the whole of his life. So it is true that paternal power is a natural government, but it in no way extends to the purposes and jurisdiction of political government. The father's power does not reach the child's property at all, which is at the child's own disposal alone.

### § 171

Secondly: political power is the power that every man possessed in the state of nature and has surrendered into the hands of society — and through society to the governors it has set over itself — under this express or tacit trust: that it will be used for the members' good and the preservation of their property. Now this power, which every man has in the state of nature and which he gives up to society in all those cases where society can protect him, is the power to use whatever means he judges appropriate, and nature permits, for preserving his own property, and to punish violations of the law of nature by others in whatever way (according to his best judgment) will most contribute to the preservation of himself and the rest of mankind. Since the end and measure of this power, while it rests in each individual's hands in the state of nature, is the preservation of everyone in his society — that is, of all mankind in general — it can have no other end or measure when placed in the magistrate's hands than to preserve the members of that society in their lives, liberties, and possessions. It therefore cannot be an absolute, arbitrary power over their lives and fortunes, which are to be preserved as far as possible. It is instead a power to make laws, and to attach to them such penalties as tend to preserve the whole — by cutting off those parts, and those only, which are so corrupt that they threaten the sound and healthy; without that necessity, no severity is lawful. And this power has its origin solely in compact and agreement, the mutual consent of those who make up the community.

### § 172

Thirdly: despotical power is an absolute, arbitrary power that one man holds over another, to take his life whenever he pleases. This is a power that nature does not give, for nature has made no such distinction between one man and another. Nor can any compact convey it: since a man does not have such arbitrary power over his own life, he cannot give another man such power over it. It is the effect of forfeiture alone — the forfeiture an aggressor makes of his own life when he puts himself into a state of war with another. For he has abandoned reason, which God gave to be the rule between man and man and the common bond by which humankind is united into one fellowship and society. He has renounced the way of peace that reason teaches, and has used the force of war to accomplish his unjust designs on another where he has no right. In so doing he has deserted his own kind for that of beasts, making force — which is their rule — his rule of right; and so he makes himself liable to be destroyed by the injured person, and by the rest of mankind who join in executing justice, as one would destroy any other wild beast or dangerous brute with which mankind can have neither society nor security.* And so captives taken in a just and lawful war — and only such captives — are subject to a despotical power. Since it does not arise from compact, neither is it capable of entering into one; it is simply the state of war continued. For what compact can be made with a man who is not master of his own life? What condition of agreement could he possibly perform? And if he is once allowed to be master of his own life, the despotical, arbitrary power of his master ends. Whoever is master of himself and his own life also has a right to the means of preserving it. So the moment compact enters, slavery ceases: whoever enters into terms with his captive thereby gives up his absolute power to that extent, and puts an end to the state of war.

(*Another copy corrected by Mr. Locke has this instead: "dangerous brute that is destructive to their being.")

### § 173

Nature gives the first of these — namely, paternal power — to parents for the benefit of their children during their minority, to make up for their lack of ability and of understanding in how to manage their property. (By "property" I must be understood here, as in other places, to mean the property men have in their persons as well as in their goods.) Voluntary agreement gives the second — namely, political power — to governors for the benefit of their subjects, to secure them in the possession and use of their properties. And forfeiture gives the third — despotical power — to lords, for their own benefit, over those who have been stripped of all property.

### § 174

Whoever considers the distinct origin and extent, and the different purposes, of these several powers will see plainly that paternal power falls as far short of the magistrate's power as despotical power exceeds it; and that absolute dominion, wherever it is placed, is so far from being one kind of civil society that it is as inconsistent with civil society as slavery is with property. Paternal power exists only where minority makes the child incapable of managing his property; political power, where men have property at their own disposal; and despotical power, over those who have no property at all.
