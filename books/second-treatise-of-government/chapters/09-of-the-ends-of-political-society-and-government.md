# Chapter IX — Of the Ends of Political Society and Government

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 123–131
>
> [← Of the Beginning of Political Societies](08-of-the-beginning-of-political-societies.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the Forms of a Commonwealth →](10-of-the-forms-of-a-commonwealth.md)

## Overview

This short chapter answers the obvious objection to everything Locke has argued so far: if people in the state of nature are perfectly free — masters of themselves and their possessions, subject to no one — why would anyone ever trade that freedom for subjection to a government? Locke's answer is that natural freedom, however complete in principle, is insecure in practice. Everyone else is just as free and just as much a "king," most people do not strictly observe justice, and so the enjoyment of one's life, liberty, and possessions — which Locke bundles together under the single word *property* — is constantly under threat.

From this Locke derives his famous thesis: the great and chief end of government is the preservation of property. He identifies three specific defects of the state of nature that make it unsafe: there is no settled, known, publicly accepted law; there is no impartial judge with authority to apply the law; and there is no reliable power to enforce a correct judgment. People escape these "inconveniencies" by giving up two natural powers — the power to do whatever they judge fit for their own and others' preservation, and the power to punish violations of the law of nature. The first is placed under regulation by society's laws; the second is surrendered entirely to society's executive power. This exchange, Locke says, is the true origin of legislative and executive power and of governments themselves.

The chapter closes with the crucial limiting move that drives the rest of the treatise. Because no rational person would change conditions in order to be worse off, the power people transfer to society can never be supposed to extend beyond the common good. The legislative — whoever holds supreme power — is therefore *bound* to govern by promulgated standing laws, through impartial judges, and to use the community's force only to execute those laws at home and to defend against injury from abroad, all directed to nothing but the peace, safety, and public good of the people. The ends of government thus become the measure of its legitimacy, setting up the later chapters on the limits of legislative power, tyranny, and the dissolution of government.

## Key Concepts

- **Property (in the broad sense)** — Locke's general name for people's "lives, liberties and estates" taken together. When he says the chief end of government is "the preservation of property," he means the protection of all three, not merely of material possessions.
- **The three defects of the state of nature** — What the state of nature lacks: (1) an established, settled, known law accepted by common consent as the standard of right and wrong; (2) a known and impartial judge with authority to decide disputes by that law; (3) power to back and enforce a just sentence. These three "wants" are what drive people into political society.
- **The two natural powers a person gives up** — First, the power to do whatever one judges fit for the preservation of oneself and others within the law of nature; second, the power to punish crimes against that law. On entering society, the first is submitted to regulation by society's laws; the second is surrendered wholly to assist the society's executive power.
- **Self-partiality and bias** — Locke's diagnosis of why natural law fails in practice even though it is plain to all rational creatures: people are biased by their own interest, ignorant of the law for lack of study, judges in their own cases carried too far by passion and revenge, and negligent in the cases of others.
- **Origin of legislative and executive power** — Both powers, and government itself, arise from individuals resigning their single natural power of punishing to be exercised only by appointed persons under rules the community agrees on.
- **The common good as the limit of political power** — Since everyone enters society only to preserve himself, his liberty, and his property better, the power of society or its legislative can never be supposed to extend farther than the common good; it exists to remedy the three defects, and nothing more.
- **Government by standing laws** — The concrete obligations that follow: rule by established, promulgated, known laws rather than improvised ("extemporary") decrees; adjudication by impartial and upright judges; and use of the community's force only to execute the laws at home or to prevent and redress foreign injury.

## Modern Translation

### § 123

If man in the state of nature is as free as I have said — if he is absolute lord of his own person and possessions, equal to the greatest, and subject to nobody — why would he ever part with his freedom? Why would he give up this dominion over himself and place himself under the rule and control of any other power? The answer is obvious: although in the state of nature he has such a right, his enjoyment of it is very uncertain and constantly exposed to invasion by others. Since everyone else is as much a king as he is — every man his equal — and since the greater part of mankind are no strict observers of fairness and justice, the enjoyment of the property he has in this state is very unsafe, very insecure. This makes him willing to leave a condition which, however free, is full of fears and continual dangers. And it is not without reason that he seeks out, and is willing to join in, society with others who are already united, or who intend to unite, for the mutual preservation of their lives, liberties, and estates — which I call by the general name *property*.

### § 124

The great and chief end, therefore, of people uniting into commonwealths and putting themselves under government is the preservation of their property. For this purpose, the state of nature lacks many things.

First, it lacks an established, settled, known law — one received and accepted by common consent as the standard of right and wrong, and the common measure for deciding all disputes between people. For although the law of nature is plain and intelligible to all rational creatures, people are biased by their own interest, and ignorant of that law for lack of studying it. As a result, they are not inclined to accept it as a law binding on them when it comes to applying it to their own particular cases.

### § 125

Second, in the state of nature there is no known and impartial judge with authority to settle all disputes according to the established law. Since everyone in that state is both judge and executioner of the law of nature, and since people are partial to themselves, passion and revenge are very likely to carry them too far, and with too much heat, in their own cases — just as negligence and indifference are likely to make them too lax in the cases of others.

### § 126

Third, in the state of nature there is often no power to back up and support a sentence even when it is right, and to give it proper enforcement. Those who have committed an injustice will seldom fail, wherever they are able, to make good their injustice by force; and such resistance often makes punishment dangerous, and frequently fatal, to those who attempt it.

### § 127

So despite all the privileges of the state of nature, mankind is in a bad condition while remaining in it, and is quickly driven into society. This is why we seldom find any number of people living together in this state for any length of time. The harms they are exposed to in it — from the irregular and uncertain exercise of the power every man has to punish the transgressions of others — make them take refuge under the established laws of government, and there seek the preservation of their property. This is what makes each of them so willing to give up his individual power of punishing, so that it can be exercised only by those who are appointed to it among them, and according to whatever rules the community — or those it authorizes for that purpose — shall agree on. And here we have the original right and origin of both the legislative and the executive power, as well as of governments and societies themselves.

### § 128

In the state of nature — leaving aside the liberty he has to enjoy innocent pleasures — a man has two powers.

The first is the power to do whatever he thinks fit for the preservation of himself and others, within the bounds permitted by the law of nature. By that law, which is common to them all, he and all the rest of mankind form one community, making up one society distinct from all other creatures. And were it not for the corruption and viciousness of degenerate men, there would be no need of any other society — no necessity for people to separate from this great natural community and, by explicit agreements, combine into smaller, divided associations.

The other power a man has in the state of nature is the power to punish crimes committed against that law. He gives up both of these powers when he joins what I might call a private, or particular, political society, and incorporates himself into any commonwealth separate from the rest of mankind.

### § 129

The first power — namely, of doing whatever he thought fit for the preservation of himself and the rest of mankind — he gives up to be regulated by laws made by the society, to the extent that the preservation of himself and the rest of that society requires it. And these laws of society in many respects confine the liberty he had under the law of nature.

### § 130

Second, the power of punishing he gives up entirely. He commits his natural force — which he could previously use, on his own individual authority and as he thought fit, to execute the law of nature — to assist the executive power of the society, as its law requires. For he is now in a new condition, in which he is to enjoy many advantages from the labor, assistance, and companionship of others in the same community, as well as protection from its whole strength. He must therefore also part with as much of his natural liberty in providing for himself as the good, prosperity, and safety of the society require. This is not only necessary but just, since the other members of the society do the same.

### § 131

But although, when they enter society, people give up the equality, liberty, and executive power they had in the state of nature into the hands of the society — to be disposed of by the legislative as far as the good of the society requires — they do so only with the intention, in each of them, of better preserving themselves, their liberty, and their property. (No rational creature can be supposed to change his condition intending to be worse off.) Therefore the power of the society, or of the legislative constituted by them, can never be supposed to extend farther than the common good. It is obliged to secure everyone's property by providing against the three defects mentioned above, which made the state of nature so unsafe and uneasy. And so whoever holds the legislative or supreme power of any commonwealth is bound to govern by established standing laws, promulgated and known to the people, and not by improvised decrees; to govern through impartial and upright judges, who are to decide disputes by those laws; and to employ the force of the community at home only in the execution of such laws, and abroad only to prevent or redress foreign injuries and to secure the community from raids and invasion. And all of this is to be directed to no other end than the peace, safety, and public good of the people.
