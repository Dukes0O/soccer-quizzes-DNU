# Chapter XI — Of the Extent of the Legislative Power

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 134–142
>
> [← Of the Forms of a Commonwealth](10-of-the-forms-of-a-commonwealth.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the Legislative, Executive, and Federative Power →](12-of-the-legislative-executive-and-federative-power.md)

## Overview

Having established in Chapter X that every commonwealth is defined by where it places its legislative power, Locke now asks the decisive question: what may that power actually do? His answer is the heart of constitutionalism. The legislative is indeed the supreme power in any commonwealth — no edict from anyone else counts as law without its sanction — but "supreme" does not mean "unlimited." Because the legislative gets its authority only from the consent of the people, it can never hold more power than the people themselves had to give.

The chapter's argument unfolds as four explicit limits. First (§ 135), the legislative cannot be arbitrary over people's lives and fortunes, because no individual in the state of nature had arbitrary power over himself or others to transfer in the first place; the law of nature binds legislators as much as anyone. Second (§§ 136–137), it must rule through promulgated, standing laws applied by known, authorized judges — not by improvised decrees — since escaping the uncertainty of the state of nature was the whole point of forming society. Third (§§ 138–140), it cannot take any part of a person's property without his consent; even absolute military command, which can send a soldier to his death, cannot touch a penny of his money, and taxes require the consent of the majority or their representatives. Fourth (§ 141), the legislative cannot transfer its lawmaking power to other hands, because a delegated power cannot be re-delegated: the people authorized it to make laws, not to make legislators.

Section 142 gathers these four bounds into a summary that reads like a draft bill of constitutional rights: one standing rule for rich and poor alike, laws aimed only at the people's good, no taxation without consent, and no transfer of the legislative power. These limits, set by the society's trust and by the law of God and nature, hold "in all forms of government" — and they lay the groundwork for Locke's later argument that a legislative which breaks them forfeits its authority.

## Key Concepts

- **Supremacy of the legislative** — The legislative is the supreme power of the commonwealth: nothing has the force of law without its sanction, and all obedience ultimately terminates in it. But its supremacy is held in trust from the people, "sacred and unalterable" only in the hands where the community has placed it.
- **Consent as the source of law** — What makes a rule a law at all is the consent of the society. No one can make laws over a people except by their own consent and by authority received from them — a point Locke anchors twice in quotations from Hooker.
- **Limits on delegated power** — No one can transfer to another more power than he has in himself. Since no one in the state of nature has arbitrary power over his own or another's life, liberty, or possessions, the legislative — which is only the joint natural power of the members given up to it — cannot be arbitrary either.
- **The law of nature in society** — The obligations of the law of nature do not end when society is formed; they are often drawn tighter, with known human penalties attached. The law of nature stands as an eternal rule for legislators as much as for everyone else, and no human enactment is valid against it.
- **Standing promulgated laws** — Government must proceed by declared, established, published laws and known authorized judges, not by extemporary arbitrary decrees. Otherwise people are worse off than in the state of nature, having armed a ruler with the joint power of the multitude while disarming themselves.
- **Property and consent to taxation** — Since the preservation of property is the end of government, the supreme power cannot take any part of a person's property without his consent. Government must be funded, but taxes require the consent of the majority, given directly or through chosen representatives.
- **Absolute versus arbitrary power** — Even where power must be absolute — as in military discipline, where disobedience is justly punished with death — it is not therefore arbitrary: it remains confined to the end that requires it. A general can hang a soldier for desertion but cannot seize a farthing of his estate.
- **Non-transferability of legislative power** — The legislative holds a delegated power to make laws, not to make legislators. Only the people can constitute the legislative and decide whose hands it is in, so the legislative can never pass its lawmaking authority to anyone else.

## Modern Translation

### § 134

The great purpose for which people enter into society is to enjoy their property in peace and safety, and the great instrument for achieving that is the laws established in that society. So the first and fundamental *positive* law of every commonwealth is the establishment of the legislative power — just as the first and fundamental *natural* law, which governs even the legislative itself, is the preservation of the society and (so far as is compatible with the public good) of every person in it. This legislative is not only the supreme power of the commonwealth; it is sacred and unalterable in the hands where the community has once placed it. No edict from anyone else — however it is framed, and whatever power stands behind it — can have the force and obligation of a law unless it is sanctioned by the legislative that the public has chosen and appointed. Without that, a law would lack the one thing absolutely necessary to its being a law:* the consent of the society, over whom no one can have the power to make laws except by their own consent and by authority received from them. Therefore all the obedience anyone can be bound to give, by even the most solemn ties, ultimately terminates in this supreme power and is directed by the laws it enacts. No oath to any foreign power whatsoever, and no domestic subordinate power, can release any member of the society from his obedience to the legislative acting within its trust, or oblige him to any obedience contrary to the laws so enacted, or beyond what those laws allow. It is ridiculous to imagine that a person could be ultimately bound to obey any power in the society that is not the supreme one.

(*"The lawful power of making laws to command whole political societies of men belongs so properly to those entire societies themselves, that for any prince or potentate of any kind on earth to exercise it on his own — without an express commission received immediately and personally from God, or without authority derived originally from the consent of the very people on whom he imposes laws — is nothing better than mere tyranny. Rules that public approval has not made into laws are therefore not laws at all." Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 10.

"On this point, then, we must note that since such men naturally have no full and perfect power to command whole political multitudes, then without our consent we could not, strictly speaking, be under any living man's command. And we do consent to be commanded when the society of which we are a part has at some earlier time consented, without afterwards revoking that consent by a similarly universal agreement. Human laws, then, of whatever kind, derive their force from consent." Hooker, ibid.)

### § 135

The legislative — whether placed in one person or in several, whether it sits permanently or only at intervals — is the supreme power in every commonwealth. And yet:

*First*, it is not, and cannot possibly be, absolutely arbitrary over the lives and fortunes of the people. It is nothing but the joint power of every member of the society, handed over to the person or assembly that acts as legislator; so it can be no more than the power those persons had in the state of nature before they entered society and gave it up to the community. No one can transfer to another more power than he has in himself, and no one has absolute arbitrary power over himself or anyone else — power to destroy his own life, or to take away another's life or property. A person, as I have already proved, cannot subject himself to the arbitrary power of another. And since in the state of nature he had no arbitrary power over the life, liberty, or possessions of another — only as much power as the law of nature gave him for preserving himself and the rest of mankind — this is all he does or can give up to the commonwealth, and through it to the legislative power. So the legislative can have no more than this. Its power, at its utmost limits, is bounded by the public good of the society. It is a power that has no other end but preservation, and therefore it can never have a right to destroy, enslave, or deliberately impoverish the subjects.* The obligations of the law of nature do not cease in society; in many cases they are only drawn tighter, with known penalties attached to them by human laws to enforce their observance. Thus the law of nature stands as an eternal rule for all people — legislators as much as anyone else. The rules they make for other people's actions must conform to the law of nature — that is, to the will of God, of which that law is a declaration — just as their own actions and everyone else's must. And since the fundamental law of nature is the preservation of mankind, no human enactment can be good or valid against it.

(*"There are two foundations that bear up public societies: one is a natural inclination by which all men desire sociable life and fellowship; the other is an order, agreed on expressly or tacitly, concerning how they will be united in living together. The latter is what we call the law of a commonwealth — the very soul of a political body, whose parts are animated by law, held together, and set to work in whatever actions the common good requires. Political laws, ordained for external order and government among men, are never framed as they should be unless they presume that the human will is inwardly obstinate, rebellious, and averse to all obedience to the sacred laws of its nature — in a word, unless they presume that man, in respect of his depraved mind, is little better than a wild beast — and accordingly provide, notwithstanding, to frame his outward actions so that they pose no hindrance to the common good for which societies are instituted. Unless they do this, they are not perfect." Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 10.)

### § 136

*Secondly*, the legislative or supreme authority cannot assume for itself a power to rule by improvised, arbitrary decrees. It is bound to dispense justice and decide the rights of the subject by promulgated standing laws and known, authorized judges.* The law of nature is unwritten, and so is to be found nowhere but in the minds of men; and those who, through passion or self-interest, misquote or misapply it cannot easily be convinced of their mistake where there is no established judge. So in that condition it fails to serve, as it should, to settle the rights and fence in the property of those who live under it — especially where everyone is its judge, interpreter, and executioner, and in his own case at that; and where the man who has right on his side ordinarily has only his own single strength, and so lacks the force to defend himself from injuries or to punish offenders. To avoid these evils, which unsettle men's property in the state of nature, people unite into societies, so that they may have the united strength of the whole society to secure and defend their property, and may have standing rules to define it, by which everyone may know what is his. To this end they give up all their natural power to the society they enter, and the community puts the legislative power into whatever hands it thinks fit, with this trust: that they shall be governed by declared laws. Otherwise their peace, quiet, and property will remain just as uncertain as they were in the state of nature.

(*"Human laws are measures for men, whose actions they must direct; yet they are measures that have higher rules by which they in turn are measured — and these rules are two: the law of God and the law of nature. So human laws must be made according to the general laws of nature, and without contradicting any positive law of Scripture; otherwise they are badly made." Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 3, section 9.

"To compel men to anything harmful seems unreasonable." Hooker, ibid., book 1, section 10.)

### § 137

Absolute arbitrary power, and governing without settled standing laws — neither of these is consistent with the ends of society and government. People would never give up the freedom of the state of nature and bind themselves under government except to preserve their lives, liberties, and fortunes, and to secure their peace and quiet by fixed rules of right and property. It cannot be supposed that they should intend — even if they had the power to do it — to give any one person, or any group, an absolute arbitrary power over their persons and estates, and put force into the magistrate's hand to execute his unlimited will on them arbitrarily. That would be to put themselves into a worse condition than the state of nature, where at least they had the liberty to defend their rights against the injuries of others, and stood on equal terms of force to maintain them, whether invaded by a single man or by many acting together. Whereas if we suppose they have surrendered themselves to the absolute arbitrary power and will of a legislator, they have disarmed themselves and armed him to make prey of them whenever he pleases. A man is in a much worse condition when he is exposed to the arbitrary power of one man who commands 100,000 than when he is exposed to the arbitrary power of 100,000 separate men — for no one can be sure that the will of a man with such a command is any better than other men's, though his force is 100,000 times stronger. Therefore, whatever form the commonwealth takes, the ruling power ought to govern by declared and accepted laws, not by extemporary dictates and undetermined resolutions. Otherwise mankind will be in a far worse condition than in the state of nature, if they have armed one man or a few with the joint power of the multitude, to force them to obey at pleasure the exorbitant and unlimited decrees of their sudden thoughts, or their unrestrained wills — unknown until that very moment — without any fixed standards laid down to guide and justify their actions. All the power the government has is only for the good of the society; and just as it must not be arbitrary and exercised at pleasure, so it must be exercised through established and promulgated laws — so that the people may know their duty and be safe and secure within the limits of the law, and the rulers too may be kept within their bounds, and not be tempted by the power in their hands to use it for purposes, and by methods, they would not want known and would not willingly acknowledge.

### § 138

*Thirdly*, the supreme power cannot take from any man any part of his property without his own consent. The preservation of property is the end of government and the reason people enter society; so it necessarily presupposes and requires that the people have property — otherwise we must suppose that by entering society they lose the very thing they entered it to secure, an absurdity too gross for anyone to accept. People in society, then, have property; and they have such a right to the goods that are theirs by the law of the community that no one has a right to take their substance, or any part of it, from them without their own consent. Without this, they have no property at all — for I truly have no property in anything that another can rightfully take from me whenever he pleases, against my consent. Hence it is a mistake to think that the supreme or legislative power of any commonwealth can do whatever it likes, dispose of the subjects' estates arbitrarily, or take any part of them at pleasure. There is not much to fear on this score in governments where the legislative consists wholly or partly of assemblies whose membership changes — whose members, when the assembly dissolves, become subjects under the common laws of their country like everyone else. But in governments where the legislative rests in one permanent assembly, always in being, or in one man, as in absolute monarchies, there is always a danger that they will come to think they have an interest distinct from the rest of the community, and so will be inclined to increase their own riches and power by taking what they see fit from the people. For a man's property is not secure at all — however good and equitable the laws that mark its boundaries between him and his fellow subjects — if the one who commands those subjects has the power to take from any private man whatever part of his property he pleases, and to use and dispose of it as he thinks good.

### § 139

But government, into whatever hands it is put, is (as I have shown before) entrusted on this condition and for this end: that people may have and secure their property. So the prince or senate, however much power it may have to make laws regulating property between subject and subject, can never have the power to take to itself the whole of the subjects' property, or any part of it, without their own consent — for that would in effect leave them no property at all. And to see that even absolute power, where it is necessary, is not arbitrary merely because it is absolute — that it is still limited by the reason that required it, and confined to the ends for which some cases demand it — we need look no further than the ordinary practice of military discipline. The preservation of the army, and with it of the whole commonwealth, requires absolute obedience to the command of every superior officer, and it is justly punishable by death to disobey or dispute even the most dangerous or unreasonable of those commands. Yet we see that the sergeant who can order a soldier to march up to the mouth of a cannon, or to stand in a breach where he is almost certain to perish, cannot order that soldier to give him a single penny of his money. And the general who can condemn him to death for deserting his post, or for disobeying the most desperate orders, cannot — with all his absolute power of life and death — dispose of one farthing of that soldier's estate or seize the least bit of his goods, though he can command him to do anything and hang him for the slightest disobedience. The reason is that such blind obedience is necessary to the end for which the commander holds his power, namely the preservation of the rest; but disposing of the soldier's goods has nothing to do with it.

### § 140

It is true that governments cannot be supported without great expense, and it is fitting that everyone who enjoys his share of the protection should pay his proportion out of his estate for its maintenance. But even so, it must be with his own consent — that is, the consent of the majority, given either by themselves or by representatives they have chosen. For if anyone claims a power to lay and levy taxes on the people by his own authority, without that consent of the people, he thereby invades the fundamental law of property and subverts the end of government. For what property do I have in that which another may rightfully take for himself whenever he pleases?

### § 141

*Fourthly*, the legislative cannot transfer the power of making laws into any other hands. It is only a power delegated from the people, and those who hold it cannot pass it on to others. The people alone can appoint the form of the commonwealth, which they do by constituting the legislative and deciding in whose hands it shall be. And once the people have said, "We will submit to rules and be governed by laws made by such men, and in such forms," no one else can say that other men shall make laws for them; nor can the people be bound by any laws except those enacted by the persons they have chosen and authorized to make laws for them. The power of the legislative is derived from the people by a positive, voluntary grant and institution, and it can be nothing other than what that positive grant conveyed. Since the grant was only to make *laws*, and not to make *legislators*, the legislative can have no power to transfer its authority of making laws and place it in other hands.

### § 142

These, then, are the bounds that the trust placed in the legislative by the society, and the law of God and of nature, have set to the legislative power of every commonwealth, in all forms of government.

First, they are to govern by promulgated, established laws, not to be varied in particular cases, but with one rule for rich and poor, for the favorite at court and the countryman at the plough.

Secondly, these laws must be designed for no ultimate end other than the good of the people.

Thirdly, they must not raise taxes on the property of the people without the consent of the people, given by themselves or their deputies. This point properly concerns only those governments where the legislative is always in being, or at least where the people have not reserved any part of the legislative to deputies to be chosen from time to time by themselves.

Fourthly, the legislative neither must nor can transfer the power of making laws to anyone else, or place it anywhere except where the people have placed it.
