# Preface

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Preface
>
> [Book index](../README.md) · [Introduction →](01-introduction.md)

## Overview

Locke's preface introduces the *Two Treatises of Government* as a fragment: the beginning and the end of a larger work whose middle portion — which he says was longer than everything that survives — has been lost. He declines to explain what happened to those papers and says he has neither the time nor the inclination to rewrite them. What remains, he claims, is enough to accomplish the book's political purpose: to secure the throne of King William after the Revolution of 1688, to show that William's title rests on the consent of the people — the only foundation of any lawful government — and to vindicate the English people for resisting a slide into slavery and ruin.

The lost middle would have continued Locke's detailed refutation of Sir Robert Filmer, whose *Patriarcha* argued that kings inherit absolute authority from Adam by divine right. Locke explains why he bothered to attack a long-dead author at all: the clergy had recently revived Filmer's doctrine from the pulpit and made it the fashionable theology of the day, so its authority had to be publicly exposed. He challenges any reader to strip Filmer's prose of its rhetorical polish and reduce it to plain propositions — the result, he predicts, will be self-contradictory nonsense, especially in Filmer's treatment of usurpation.

The preface closes with the terms on which Locke will engage critics: he promises to recant if fairly refuted or to answer serious objections, but he will not treat quibbling over stray expressions or mere abuse as arguments. A final note explains his citation conventions for Filmer's works.

## Key Concepts

- **Consent of the people** — The thesis of the whole treatise, announced here in advance: consent is the only basis of lawful government, and King William holds his title by it more fully and clearly than any prince in Christendom.
- **The Glorious Revolution as vindication** — Locke presents the events of 1688 not as rebellion but as the English people's lawful defense of their "just and natural rights" against impending slavery — the practical case his theory will justify.
- **Refutation of Filmer** — The *First Treatise* (and the lost middle section) answered Sir Robert Filmer's *Patriarcha*, the standard defense of absolute monarchy by divine patriarchal right. Locke's method is to translate Filmer's ornate rhetoric into plain propositions and show they contradict each other.
- **The lost middle** — Locke openly admits that more than half of the original work is missing and will not be replaced; he asks the reader to judge the surviving argument as sufficient on its own.
- **Pulpit patriarchalism** — Locke's stated reason for attacking a dead author: preachers had adopted Filmer's doctrine as the orthodoxy of the times, and teachers who mislead the public must be shown how weak their authority is.
- **Rules of fair controversy** — Locke will answer conscientious objections and recant proven errors, but he refuses to count nitpicking at isolated phrases, or insults, as answers to his book.

## Modern Translation

Reader, what you have here is the beginning and the end of a discourse on government. Fate has disposed otherwise of the papers that should have filled the middle — and they amounted to more than all the rest — but how that happened is not worth telling you. The parts that survive are, I hope, sufficient for three things: to establish the throne of our great restorer, our present King William; to make good his title on the basis of the consent of the people — the only title of any lawful government, and one he holds more fully and clearly than any prince in Christendom; and to justify to the world the people of England, whose love of their just and natural rights, together with their resolve to preserve them, saved the nation when it stood on the very brink of slavery and ruin. If these papers carry the evidential force I flatter myself they do, the lost ones will not be greatly missed, and my reader can be satisfied without them. Frankly, I expect to have neither the time nor the inclination to repeat my labors and fill in the missing part of my answer by chasing Sir Robert again through all the twists and obscurities that run through the several branches of his wonderful system. The king and the body of the nation have since so thoroughly refuted his hypothesis that I assume no one hereafter will have either the audacity to set himself against our common safety and become once more an advocate for slavery, or the weakness to be taken in by contradictions dressed up in a popular style and elegantly turned sentences. If anyone will take the trouble, in the parts of Filmer's work I have left untouched, to strip his discourses of their flourish of ambiguous expressions, to reduce his words to direct, definite, intelligible propositions, and then to compare those propositions with one another, he will quickly be convinced that never was so much glib nonsense strung together in fine-sounding English. If he does not think it worth examining Filmer's works all the way through, let him run the experiment on the part dealing with usurpation, and see whether, with all his skill, he can make Sir Robert intelligible and consistent — with himself or with common sense. I would not speak so bluntly of a gentleman long past the possibility of answering, had not the pulpit in recent years publicly endorsed his doctrine and made it the fashionable theology of the times. Men who set themselves up as teachers and have so dangerously misled others must be openly shown what sort of authority this Patriarch of theirs really is — the one they have followed so blindly — so that they may either retract what they have put about on such poor grounds and cannot defend, or else justify the principles they preached as gospel, even though their author was nothing better than an English courtier. For I would never have written against Sir Robert, or taken the trouble to expose his mistakes, his inconsistencies, and his lack of the scripture-proofs he boasts of so loudly and claims to build everything on, were there not men among us who, by praising his books and embracing his doctrine, spare me the reproach of attacking a dead adversary. They have been so zealous on this point that, if I have done him any wrong, I cannot expect them to spare me. I only wish that, where they have wronged the truth and the public, they would be as ready to make amends — and to give due weight to this reflection: that no greater harm can be done to prince and people alike than the spreading of wrong notions about government; so that future ages may not always have reason to complain of the Drum Ecclesiastic. If anyone genuinely concerned for truth undertakes to refute my hypothesis, I promise him one of two things: either to recant my mistake upon fair conviction, or to answer his difficulties. But he must remember two things.

First: quibbling here and there at some expression or minor incident of my discourse is not an answer to my book.

Second: I will not take abuse for argument, nor consider either of these worth my notice — though I will always regard myself as bound to give satisfaction to anyone who shows himself conscientiously scrupulous on the point and produces just grounds for his scruples.

I have nothing further, except to notify the reader that "Observations" stands for Filmer's *Observations on Hobbes, Milton, etc.*, and that a bare page citation always means a page of his *Patriarcha*, 1680 edition.
