# Chapter VIII — Of the Beginning of Political Societies

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 95–122
>
> [← Of Political or Civil Society](07-of-political-or-civil-society.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the Ends of Political Society and Government →](09-of-the-ends-of-political-society-and-government.md)

## Overview

This is the chapter where Locke finally answers the question his whole argument has been building toward: how does legitimate government actually get started? His answer is consent — and only consent. Since all people are by nature free, equal, and independent, no one can be brought under another's political power without agreeing to it. When a group of free individuals agrees to unite into one community, they form a single "body politic," and from that moment the majority has the right to decide for the whole. Locke spends several sections (§ 96–99) defending majority rule as a matter of practical necessity: a body must move in one direction, unanimous consent is unattainable in practice, and a society that required it would dissolve the day it was born.

Locke then takes on two standing objections. The first (§ 100–104) is historical: no one, the critics say, can point to an actual case of free and equal people meeting and setting up a government. Locke replies that government everywhere predates written records — of course we have little documentation of the state of nature — and then supplies examples anyway: Rome, Venice, the peoples of the Americas described by Acosta, and the Spartan colonists led by Palantus. He adds a pointed warning that defenders of "paternal empire" should not look too closely into how most governments actually began, since they would find origins very unfriendly to their theory.

Sections 105–112 concede and explain a real historical pattern: early governments usually were monarchies, often headed by a father. But Locke argues this reflects convenience and habit, not paternal right — early monarchs were essentially elected war captains (he documents this at length from the history of Israel's judges and kings), trusted because the simple, poor, virtuous conditions of early ages gave little scope for tyranny. Only when ambition and luxury corrupted rulers did people need to examine the origins and rights of government and devise restraints.

The second objection (§ 113–122) is that everyone is born under some government and so is never free to start a new one. Locke turns this against its makers — by their logic there could be only one lawful prince in the world — and then develops his famous account of how each new generation consents: no father can bind his children, a child is born a subject of no country, and each person, on coming of age, chooses membership. This leads to the distinction between express consent, which makes someone a full and permanent member of a commonwealth, and tacit consent, which anyone gives merely by holding property or even traveling within a government's territory, and which obligates only for as long as the enjoyment lasts. Nothing but express, positive agreement makes someone a member of a commonwealth.

## Key Concepts

- **Consent as the sole origin of political power** — Since people are naturally free, equal, and independent, the only way anyone can be legitimately subjected to political power is by their own agreement. The bare consent of free individuals to unite into one society is the whole of the original compact, and the only thing that ever did or could begin a lawful government.
- **The body politic and majority rule** — Once individuals incorporate into one community, it must act as a single body, and a body can only move the way the greater force carries it. So each member is bound, by the very act of joining, to be concluded (bound) by the majority — otherwise the compact would leave everyone exactly as free as before and would be no compact at all.
- **Government precedes records** — Locke's answer to the "no historical examples" objection: writing and record-keeping arrive only long after civil society is established, so the absence of documented foundings by consent proves nothing — like inferring that Xerxes' soldiers were never children because history first mentions them as an army.
- **Early monarchy as elective generalship** — The first kings were essentially chosen war captains. Their rule was accepted out of habit (the father's rule), simplicity, and trust appropriate to a poor and virtuous age — not out of any belief in paternal or divine right. Almost all monarchies near their origin were, at least on occasion, elective.
- **No inherited subjection** — A father's compact can bind himself but not his children. Every child is born a subject of no country; on reaching the age of discretion each person is free to choose what government to join. A father can, however, attach conditions to his estate, so a son who wants the inheritance must join the community it lies in.
- **Express vs. tacit consent** — Express consent makes a person a full, permanent member of a commonwealth, irrevocably (unless the government dissolves or expels him). Tacit consent is given by merely possessing or enjoying anything within a government's territory — owning land, lodging for a week, even using the highway — and obligates obedience to its laws, but only for as long as the enjoyment lasts, and it never by itself makes anyone a member.
- **Property annexed to the commonwealth** — When a person joins a commonwealth, his possessions join with him: it would be a contradiction to enter society to have property regulated while exempting one's land from the society's jurisdiction. Land once annexed carries the condition of submission to that government for whoever later holds it.

## Modern Translation

### § 95

As I have said, all men are by nature free, equal, and independent, so no one can be removed from that condition and subjected to the political power of another without his own consent. The only way anyone divests himself of his natural liberty and takes on the bonds of civil society is by agreeing with other men to join and unite into a community — for the sake of living together comfortably, safely, and peaceably, securely enjoying their property, and gaining greater protection against anyone outside the community. Any number of men may do this, because it does no injury to the freedom of everyone else; the rest remain, as they were, in the liberty of the state of nature. When any number of men have consented in this way to form one community or government, they are by that very act immediately incorporated and make up one body politic, in which the majority has the right to act for and bind the rest.

### § 96

Here is why. When any number of men have, by the consent of every individual, made a community, they have made that community one body, with the power to act as one body — and that can only happen through the will and decision of the majority. What moves any community is nothing but the consent of the individuals in it; and since anything that is one body must move in one direction, the body necessarily moves in the direction the greater force carries it — and that greater force is the consent of the majority. Otherwise it is impossible for it to act or continue as one body, one community, which is exactly what every individual who united into it agreed it should be. So everyone is bound, by that original consent, to be concluded by the majority. This is why we see that in assemblies empowered to act by positive laws, where the empowering law sets no specific number, the act of the majority passes for the act of the whole and naturally decides the matter, as having, by the law of nature and reason, the power of the whole.

### § 97

And so every man, by consenting with others to form one body politic under one government, puts himself under an obligation to everyone in that society to submit to the decision of the majority and to be bound by it. If he did not, this original compact — by which he and the others incorporate into one society — would mean nothing, and would be no compact at all, since he would be left just as free, and under no more ties, than he was before in the state of nature. For what would remain of any compact? What new commitment would there be, if he were bound by the society's decrees no further than he himself saw fit and actually consented to? That would leave him exactly as much liberty as he had before his compact — as much as anyone else has in the state of nature, who may submit himself and consent to any of society's acts if he thinks fit.

### § 98

For if the consent of the majority is not, in reason, accepted as the act of the whole, binding on every individual, then nothing short of the consent of every single individual can make anything the act of the whole. But such unanimous consent is next to impossible ever to obtain, once we consider the illnesses and the demands of business that will inevitably keep many people away from the public assembly, even in a group far smaller than a commonwealth. And if we add to that the variety of opinions and the clash of interests that unavoidably arise in every gathering of men, then entering society on such terms would be like Cato's going into the theatre — only in order to walk out again. A constitution like that would give the mighty Leviathan a shorter life than the feeblest of creatures, and would not let it outlast the day it was born. And that cannot be supposed unless we can believe that rational creatures would desire and establish societies only in order to see them dissolved. For wherever the majority cannot bind the rest, the community cannot act as one body, and consequently it will immediately dissolve again.

### § 99

Therefore whoever leaves the state of nature and unites into a community must be understood to give up to the majority of that community all the power necessary for the ends for which they unite into society — unless they expressly agreed on some number greater than a majority. And this is done simply by agreeing to unite into one political society; that agreement is the entire compact that exists, or needs to exist, between the individuals who enter into or make up a commonwealth. So what begins and actually constitutes any political society is nothing but the consent of any number of free men, capable of forming a majority, to unite and incorporate into such a society. And this — this and nothing else — is what did, or could, give a beginning to any lawful government in the world.

### § 100

Against this I find two objections raised. First, that there are no examples to be found in history of a group of men, independent and equal among themselves, who met together and in this way founded and set up a government.

Second, that it is impossible as a matter of right for men to do so, because all men are born under some government; they are bound to submit to that government and are not at liberty to begin a new one.

### § 101

To the first objection, the answer is this. It is not at all surprising that history gives us very little account of men living together in the state of nature. The inconveniences of that condition, and the love of society and the need for it, meant that no sooner did any number of them come together than they immediately united and incorporated, if they intended to stay together. And if we may not suppose that men were ever in the state of nature merely because we hear little of them in such a state, we may just as well suppose that the soldiers in the armies of Salmanasser or Xerxes were never children — because we hear little of them until they were grown men, formed into armies. Government everywhere comes before written records, and writing rarely appears among a people until a long stretch of civil society has, by means of other more necessary arts, provided for their safety, ease, and prosperity. Only then do they begin to look into the history of their founders and search for their own origins — after they have outlived all memory of them. For commonwealths are like individual persons in this respect: they are usually ignorant of their own births and infancies, and if they know anything of their origins, they owe it to the accidental records that others happened to keep. And the records we do have of the beginning of any polities in the world — excepting that of the Jews, where God himself directly intervened, and which lends no support at all to paternal dominion — are all either plain instances of exactly the kind of beginning I have described, or at least show its unmistakable footprints.

### § 102

A man must show a strange readiness to deny plain matters of fact whenever they disagree with his hypothesis, if he will not admit that Rome and Venice began through the uniting together of a number of men who were free and independent of one another, among whom there was no natural superiority or subjection. And if Josephus Acosta's word may be taken, he tells us that in many parts of America there was no government at all.

"There are strong and evident grounds for conjecture," he says (speaking of the people of Peru), "that these men for a long time had neither kings nor commonwealths, but lived in bands, as they do to this day in Florida — the Cheriquanas, the people of Brazil, and many other nations, which have no fixed kings, but as occasion arises, in peace or war, choose their captains as they please." (Book 1, chapter 25.)

If it is objected that every man there was born subject to his father, or to the head of his family, I have already proved that the subjection a child owes his father did not take away his freedom to unite into whatever political society he thought fit. But however that may be, these men were evidently free in actual fact; and whatever superiority some political writers would now assign to any of them, they themselves claimed none of it. By consent they were all equal, until by that same consent they set rulers over themselves. So their political societies all began from a voluntary union and the mutual agreement of men acting freely in the choice of their governors and their forms of government.

### § 103

And I hope that those who departed from Sparta with Palantus — mentioned by Justin, book 3, chapter 4 — will be granted to have been free men, independent of one another, who set up a government over themselves by their own consent. So I have given several examples out of history of people who were free and in the state of nature, and who, having come together, incorporated and began a commonwealth. And if a shortage of such examples were an argument to prove that government was not, and could not be, begun this way, then I suggest the champions of paternal empire would do better to drop that argument than to press it against natural liberty. For if they can produce so many instances from history of governments founded on paternal right — I think one might safely concede them the whole case (though at best an argument from what has been to what ought of right to be carries no great force). But if I might advise them in the matter, they would do well not to search too closely into the origins of governments as they actually began, in fact — lest they find, at the foundation of most of them, something very unfavorable to the design they promote and to the kind of power they contend for.

### § 104

But to conclude: reason is plainly on our side that men are naturally free, and the examples of history show that the governments of the world that began in peace were laid on that foundation and were made by the consent of the people. So there can be little room for doubt either about where the right lies, or about what the opinion and practice of mankind has been concerning the first establishment of governments.

### § 105

I will not deny that if we look back as far as history can guide us toward the origin of commonwealths, we shall generally find them under the government and administration of one man. And I am also inclined to believe that where a family was large enough to subsist by itself and stayed together as a unit, without mixing with others — as often happens where there is much land and few people — government commonly began in the father. For the father, having by the law of nature the same power as every other man to punish, as he saw fit, any offenses against that law, could on that basis punish his children when they transgressed, even after they were grown men and out of their childhood dependence. And they were very likely to submit to his punishment, and all to join with him against the offender, each in turn — thereby giving him the power to execute his sentence against any transgression, and so in effect making him the lawmaker and governor over everyone who remained attached to his family. He was the fittest person to be trusted: paternal affection secured their property and interests under his care, and the habit of obeying him in their childhood made it easier to submit to him than to anyone else. If, then, they had to have someone to rule them — and government can hardly be avoided among men who live together — who so likely to be the man as their common father, unless negligence, cruelty, or some other defect of mind or body made him unfit for it? But when the father died and left an heir who, for lack of age, wisdom, courage, or other qualities, was less fit to rule; or where several families met and agreed to continue together — there, it cannot be doubted, they used their natural freedom to set up the man they judged ablest and most likely to rule well over them. In keeping with this, we find that the peoples of America — who, living beyond the reach of the conquering swords and spreading domination of the two great empires of Peru and Mexico, enjoyed their own natural freedom — commonly prefer the heir of their deceased king, other things being equal; and yet if they find him in any way weak or incapable, they pass him over and set up the strongest and bravest man as their ruler.

### § 106

So although, looking back as far as records give us any account of the peopling of the world and the history of nations, we commonly find government in the hands of one man, this does not undermine what I affirm: that the beginning of political society depends on the consent of the individuals to join together and make one society; and that, once thus incorporated, they could set up whatever form of government they thought fit. But since this fact has given men occasion to mistake the matter and to think that government was by nature monarchical and belonged to the father, it may be worth considering here why people in the beginning generally settled on this form. The father's pre-eminence may perhaps, in the first founding of some commonwealths, have given rise to it and placed power at the outset in one man's hands; yet it is plain that the reason the form of government continued in a single person was not any regard or respect for paternal authority — since all petty monarchies (that is, almost all monarchies) near their origin have commonly been elective, at least on occasion.

### § 107

First, then: in the beginning of things, the father's government over the childhood of those descended from him had accustomed them to the rule of one man, and had taught them that where such rule was exercised with care and skill, with affection and love toward those under it, it was enough to secure and preserve for men all the political happiness they sought in society. It is no wonder that they should settle on, and naturally slide into, the form of government they had all been used to from infancy, and which experience had shown them to be both easy and safe. Add to this that monarchy is simple, and the most obvious form to men whom experience had not yet instructed in forms of government, and whom the ambition or insolence of empire had not yet taught to beware of the encroachments of prerogative or the inconveniences of absolute power — which hereditary monarchy was apt to claim and to bring upon them. Given all this, it is not at all strange that they did not trouble themselves much to think of methods for restraining any excesses of those to whom they had given authority over them, or of balancing the power of government by placing its several parts in different hands. They had never felt the oppression of tyrannical dominion, and neither the fashion of the age, nor their possessions, nor their way of living (which offered little material for greed or ambition) gave them any reason to fear it or provide against it. So it is no wonder they put themselves into a frame of government that was not only, as I said, the most obvious and simple, but also the best suited to their present state and condition — which needed defense against foreign invasions and injuries far more than it needed a multiplicity of laws. The equality of a simple, poor way of living, which confined their desires within the narrow bounds of each man's small property, produced few disputes, and so there was no need of many laws to settle them, nor of a variety of officers to supervise legal proceedings or oversee the execution of justice, where there were few trespasses and few offenders. And since people who like one another well enough to join into society must be presumed to have some acquaintance and friendship with each other, and some mutual trust, they could not help fearing outsiders more than one another. Their first care and thought, therefore, must be presumed to have been how to secure themselves against foreign force. It was natural for them to place themselves under a frame of government best suited to that end, and to choose the wisest and bravest man to conduct them in their wars and lead them out against their enemies — and chiefly in this capacity to be their ruler.

### § 108

Thus we see that the kings of the Indians in America — which is still a picture of the first ages in Asia and Europe, when the inhabitants were too few for the land, and the lack of people and money gave men no temptation to enlarge their landholdings or to fight over wider stretches of ground — are little more than generals of their armies. Though they command absolutely in war, at home and in time of peace they exercise very little dominion and have only a very moderate sovereignty; decisions of peace and war ordinarily rest either with the people or with a council. Yet war itself, which does not admit a plurality of commanders, naturally devolves the command into the king's sole authority.

### § 109

And thus in Israel itself, the chief business of their judges and first kings seems to have been to act as captains in war and leaders of their armies. This appears (besides what is implied by the phrase "going out and in before the people," which meant marching out to war and home again at the head of their forces) quite plainly in the story of Jephtha. When the Ammonites made war on Israel, the Gileadites in their fear sent to Jephtha — an illegitimate member of their family whom they had cast out — and struck a bargain with him: if he would help them against the Ammonites, they would make him their ruler. They did so in these words: "And the people made him head and captain over them" (Judges 11:11), which was, it seems, the same thing as being judge. "And he judged Israel" (Judges 12:7) — that is, he was their commander-in-chief — for six years. Likewise, when Jotham reproaches the Shechemites with the obligation they owed to Gideon, who had been their judge and ruler, he tells them: "He fought for you, and risked his life, and delivered you out of the hands of Midian" (Judges 9:17). Nothing is mentioned of him except what he did as a general — and indeed that is all that is found in his history, or in the history of any of the other judges. And Abimelech in particular is called king, though at most he was only their general. And when, weary of the misconduct of Samuel's sons, the children of Israel asked for a king, "like all the nations, to judge them, and to go out before them, and to fight their battles" (1 Samuel 8:20), God, granting their desire, says to Samuel: "I will send thee a man, and thou shalt anoint him to be captain over my people Israel, that he may save my people out of the hands of the Philistines" (9:16). As if the only business of a king were to lead out their armies and fight in their defense. Accordingly, at his inauguration, pouring a vial of oil upon him, Samuel declares to Saul that "the Lord had anointed him to be captain over his inheritance" (10:1). And so those who, after Saul had been solemnly chosen and hailed as king by the tribes at Mispah, were unwilling to have him as their king, raised no objection except this: "How shall this man save us?" (10:27) — as if to say, this man is unfit to be our king, since he lacks the skill and command in war to be able to defend us. And when God resolved to transfer the government to David, it is in these words: "But now thy kingdom shall not continue: the Lord hath sought him a man after his own heart, and the Lord hath commanded him to be captain over his people" (13:14). As if the whole of kingly authority were nothing but to be their general. And so the tribes who had stayed loyal to Saul's family and opposed David's reign, when they came to Hebron with terms of submission to him, told him — among the other arguments they had for submitting to him as their king — that he had in effect been their king in Saul's time, and so they had no reason not to receive him as their king now: "Also (they say) in time past, when Saul was king over us, thou wast he that leddest out and broughtest in Israel, and the Lord said unto thee, Thou shalt feed my people Israel, and thou shalt be a captain over Israel."

### § 110

So whichever way it happened — whether a family gradually grew up into a commonwealth, with the fatherly authority passing down to the eldest son, and everyone, growing up under it in turn, tacitly submitted to it, its easiness and fairness offending no one, and everyone acquiescing until time seemed to have confirmed it and settled a right of succession by long usage; or whether several families, or the descendants of several families, brought together by chance, neighborhood, or business, united into society — in either case, the need for a general whose leadership could defend them against their enemies in war, together with the great mutual confidence that the innocence and sincerity of that poor but virtuous age gave men in one another (and such are almost all the ages in which governments that ever last are begun), led the first founders of commonwealths generally to put the rule into one man's hands, without any express limitation or restraint beyond what the nature of the thing and the purpose of government required. Whichever of these it was that first put rule into the hands of a single person, one thing is certain: no one was entrusted with it except for the public good and safety, and in the infancy of commonwealths those who held it commonly used it for those ends. Had they not done so, young societies could not have survived. Without such nursing fathers, tender and careful of the public welfare, all governments would have sunk under the weakness and infirmities of their infancy, and prince and people would soon have perished together.

### § 111

But the golden age (before vain ambition and *amor sceleratus habendi* — wicked greed — had corrupted men's minds into a false idea of true power and honor) had more virtue, and consequently better governors as well as less vicious subjects. In that age there was no overreaching prerogative on the one side to oppress the people, and consequently no dispute on the other side about privilege, aimed at reducing or restraining the power of the magistrate — and so no contest between rulers and people about governors or government. Yet when, in later ages, ambition and luxury sought to keep and enlarge that power without doing the work for which it had been given, and, aided by flattery, taught princes to have interests distinct and separate from their people, men found it necessary to examine more carefully the origin and rights of government, and to find ways to restrain the excesses and prevent the abuses of a power which they had entrusted to another's hands only for their own good, and which they now found being used to hurt them.*

(*"At first, when some particular kind of regime had once been approved, perhaps nothing further was considered about the manner of governing; everything was left to the wisdom and discretion of those who were to rule — until by experience they found this highly inconvenient in every respect, so that the thing they had devised as a remedy in fact only inflamed the sore it was meant to cure. They saw that living at one man's will became the cause of all men's misery. This compelled them to come to laws, in which all men might see their duty beforehand and know the penalties for transgressing them." — Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 10.)

### § 112

Thus we can see how probable it is that people who were naturally free, and who by their own consent either submitted to the government of their father or united together out of different families to make a government, would generally put the rule into one man's hands and choose to be under the leadership of a single person, without even limiting or regulating his power by express conditions — thinking it safe enough in his honesty and prudence. Yet they never dreamed that monarchy was *jure divino* — by divine right — something never heard of among mankind until it was revealed to us by the theology of this last age; nor did they ever grant that paternal power had a right to dominion, or was the foundation of all government. And this much may suffice to show that, as far as history gives us any light, we have reason to conclude that all peaceful beginnings of government have been laid in the consent of the people. I say "peaceful," because I shall have occasion elsewhere to speak of conquest, which some regard as a way of beginning governments.

The other objection I find raised against the beginning of polities in the way I have described is this:

### § 113

That since all men are born under some government or other, it is impossible that any of them should ever be free and at liberty to unite together and begin a new one, or ever be able to erect a lawful government.

If this argument is sound, then I ask: how did so many lawful monarchies ever come into the world? For if anyone, on this supposition, can show me any one man, in any age of the world, who was free to begin a lawful monarchy, I will undertake to show him ten other free men, at liberty at the same time to unite and begin a new government under a kingly or any other form. For it is a demonstration that if anyone born under the dominion of another can be so free as to have a right to command others in a new and distinct empire, then everyone born under the dominion of another can be equally free, and can become a ruler or a subject of a distinct, separate government. And so, by their own principle, either all men, however born, are free, or else there is only one lawful prince, one lawful government, in the world. In that case they have nothing to do but simply show us which one that is — and once they have done so, I do not doubt that all mankind will readily agree to pay him obedience.

### § 114

Although it is a sufficient answer to their objection to show that it entangles them in the very same difficulties as those they use it against, I shall nevertheless try to expose the weakness of this argument a little further. All men, they say, are born under government, and therefore cannot be at liberty to begin a new one. Everyone is born a subject to his father, or to his prince, and is therefore under a perpetual tie of subjection and allegiance. But it is plain that mankind never acknowledged or recognized any such natural subjection into which they were born — to one or the other — that bound them, without their own consent, to subjection to them and their heirs.

### § 115

For no examples are so frequent in history, both sacred and secular, as those of men withdrawing themselves and their obedience from the jurisdiction they were born under, and from the family or community they were raised in, and setting up new governments in other places. From this sprang all that multitude of petty commonwealths in the first ages, which kept multiplying as long as there was room enough, until the stronger or luckier swallowed up the weaker; and those great ones, breaking to pieces in their turn, dissolved into lesser dominions. All of these are so many testimonies against paternal sovereignty, and plainly prove that it was not the natural right of the father, descending to his heirs, that made governments in the beginning — since on that basis it would have been impossible for there to be so many little kingdoms. There would have been only one universal monarchy, had men not been at liberty to separate themselves from their families and from whatever government had been set up in them, and to go and form distinct commonwealths and other governments as they thought fit.

### § 116

This has been the practice of the world from its first beginning down to this day. Nor is it now any more of a hindrance to the freedom of mankind that they are born under long-constituted and ancient polities, with established laws and settled forms of government, than if they had been born in the woods among the unconfined inhabitants who run loose there. For those who would persuade us that by being born under a government we are naturally its subjects, and have no longer any title or claim to the freedom of the state of nature, have no other reason to offer for it (setting aside the argument from paternal power, which we have already answered) than this: that our fathers or forefathers gave away their natural liberty, and thereby bound both themselves and their posterity to perpetual subjection to the government they submitted to. Now, it is true that whatever engagements or promises anyone has made for himself, he is obliged by them; but he cannot, by any compact whatsoever, bind his children or posterity. For his son, once a man, is every bit as free as the father ever was; no act of the father can give away the liberty of the son any more than it can the liberty of anyone else. The father may indeed attach conditions to the land that he enjoyed as a subject of some commonwealth — conditions that may oblige his son to join that community if he wants to enjoy the possessions that were his father's — because that estate is the father's property, and he may dispose of it or settle it as he pleases.

### § 117

And this is what has generally given rise to the confusion on this point. Because commonwealths do not permit any part of their territory to be broken off, or to be enjoyed by anyone but members of their community, the son cannot ordinarily enjoy his father's possessions except on the same terms his father held them — by becoming a member of the society. In doing so he at once puts himself under the government he finds established there, as fully as any other subject of that commonwealth. And so the consent of free men born under government — which alone makes them members of it — being given separately, each in his turn as he comes of age, and not by a whole multitude together, people take no notice of it; and thinking it never happens at all, or is not necessary, they conclude that men are naturally subjects simply by being men.

### § 118

But it is plain that governments themselves understand the matter otherwise. They claim no power over the son on account of the power they had over the father, nor do they regard children as their subjects because their fathers were. If a subject of England has a child by an English woman in France, whose subject is that child? Not the king of England's — for he must obtain leave to be admitted to the privileges of that kingdom. Not the king of France's — for how, in that case, would his father be free to take him away and raise him as he pleases? And who was ever judged a traitor or a deserter for leaving, or making war against, a country merely because he was born in it of parents who were foreigners there? It is plain, then, by the practice of governments themselves, as well as by the law of right reason, that a child is born a subject of no country and no government. He is under his father's care and authority until he comes to the age of discretion; and then he is a free man, at liberty to decide what government he will put himself under, what body politic he will unite himself to. For if an Englishman's son born in France is at liberty, and may do so, it is evident that no tie binds him through his father's being a subject of this kingdom, nor is he bound by any compact of his ancestors. Why, then, does not his son, by the same reasoning, have the same liberty, wherever he happens to be born? For the power a father naturally has over his children is the same wherever they are born, and the ties of natural obligation are not bounded by the positive limits of kingdoms and commonwealths.

### § 119

Every man being, as has been shown, naturally free, and nothing being able to put him into subjection to any earthly power except his own consent, we must consider what should be understood as a sufficient declaration of a man's consent to make him subject to the laws of any government. There is a common distinction between express and tacit consent, which bears on our present question. Nobody doubts that the express consent of a man entering into a society makes him a complete member of that society, a subject of that government. The difficulty is what should be counted as tacit consent, and how far it binds — that is, how far anyone should be regarded as having consented, and thereby submitted to a government, where he has made no expression of consent at all. To this I say that every man who has any possession or enjoyment of any part of the territory of any government thereby gives his tacit consent, and is obliged to obey the laws of that government, during such enjoyment, as fully as anyone under it — whether his possession is land held by him and his heirs forever, or a lodging for only a week, or merely traveling freely on the highway. In effect it reaches as far as a person's very presence within the territories of that government.

### § 120

To understand this better, we should consider that every man, when he first incorporates himself into any commonwealth, by uniting himself to it also annexes and submits to the community whatever possessions he has, or shall acquire, that do not already belong to some other government. For it would be a flat contradiction for anyone to enter into society with others for the securing and regulating of property, and yet to suppose that his land — the very property that is to be regulated by the laws of the society — should be exempt from the jurisdiction of the government to which he himself, the owner of the land, is a subject. By the same act, therefore, by which anyone unites his person, previously free, to any commonwealth, he unites his possessions, previously free, to it as well; and both of them — person and possessions — become subject to the government and dominion of that commonwealth for as long as it exists. Whoever thereafter, by inheritance, purchase, permission, or any other means, enjoys any part of the land thus annexed to and under the government of that commonwealth, must take it with the condition it carries: namely, submission to the government of the commonwealth under whose jurisdiction it lies, as fully as any subject of it.

### § 121

But since the government has a direct jurisdiction only over the land, and reaches its possessor (before he has actually incorporated himself into the society) only insofar as he dwells on and enjoys that land, the obligation anyone is under, by virtue of such enjoyment, to submit to the government begins and ends with the enjoyment. So whenever an owner who has given nothing more than this tacit consent to the government chooses, by gift, sale, or otherwise, to give up that possession, he is at liberty to go and incorporate himself into any other commonwealth, or to agree with others to begin a new one *in vacuis locis* — in any part of the world they can find free and unclaimed. By contrast, a man who has once, by actual agreement and express declaration, given his consent to belong to any commonwealth is perpetually and unavoidably obliged to be and remain its subject, unalterably, and can never again be in the liberty of the state of nature — unless, through some calamity, the government he was under comes to be dissolved, or unless some public act cuts him off from continuing as a member of it.

### § 122

But submitting to the laws of a country, living quietly, and enjoying privileges and protection under them does not make a man a member of that society. It is only the local protection owed to, and the local homage owed by, all those who, not being in a state of war, come within the territories of a government, throughout which the force of its laws extends. This no more makes a man a member of that society — a perpetual subject of that commonwealth — than it would make a man the subject of another person in whose household he found it convenient to stay for a time, even though, while he remained there, he was obliged to comply with the rules and submit to the government of the family he found there. And so we see that foreigners, though they live all their lives under another government and enjoy its privileges and protection, and though they are bound, even in conscience, to submit to its administration as fully as any resident, do not thereby become subjects or members of that commonwealth. Nothing can make a man a member of it but his actually entering into it by positive engagement and express promise and compact. This, then, is what I think concerning the beginning of political societies, and concerning the consent that makes anyone a member of any commonwealth.
