# Chapter VI — Of Paternal Power

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 52–76
>
> [← Of Property](05-of-property.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Political or Civil Society →](07-of-political-or-civil-society.md)

## Overview

In this chapter Locke dismantles the strongest remaining prop of Sir Robert Filmer's patriarchal theory of kingship: the claim that political power is simply "paternal power" writ large, an absolute dominion that fathers hold over their children and that kings inherit over their subjects. Locke's opening move is to attack the very name. The power in question belongs equally to the mother — reason and Scripture both say so — and should really be called *parental* power. That correction alone is fatal to the patriarchalists, since an authority shared by two people cannot be the foundation of one-person monarchy.

Locke then explains what parental power actually is and why it exists at all. Children are born *to* equality and freedom but not *in* them: they lack the reason needed to know the law (natural or civil) that a free agent must govern himself by. Parental rule is therefore a temporary guardianship, tied to the child's immaturity, and it expires automatically when the child reaches the "age of discretion." Along the way Locke states one of the treatise's most famous doctrines: law, properly understood, does not abolish freedom but preserves and enlarges it — freedom is not doing whatever you please, but disposing of your person and property within a known law, free from the arbitrary will of others.

The rest of the chapter distinguishes carefully between the powers and duties bound up in the family. The parents' power of command ends with the child's minority; the child's duty of *honor* — respect, gratitude, support — lasts for life but confers no power to legislate or punish. A father's real leverage over grown children is merely his power to leave or withhold his estate, which is no more a right of fatherhood than a rich Frenchman's leverage over an English heir. Finally, Locke concedes the historical kernel of the patriarchal story: in the world's first ages, fathers of families often did become their princes — but only by the children's express or tacit *consent*, not by paternal right. If the mere historical fact that fathers ruled proved a natural right to political authority, it would equally prove that all princes ought to be priests. The chapter thus clears the ground for the next one, which defines political society proper as founded on consent.

## Key Concepts

- **Parental (not paternal) power** — The authority parents have over minor children. Locke insists the mother has an equal title to it, which by itself refutes those who derive absolute one-man monarchy from "fatherhood."
- **Freedom under law** — Freedom is not license to do whatever one pleases but liberty to dispose of one's person, actions, and property within the allowance of a standing law, free from another's arbitrary will. The purpose of law is to preserve and enlarge freedom, not restrain it.
- **Age of discretion** — The point of maturity at which a person can be presumed to know the law he lives under (fixed at twenty-one by English law). Reaching it makes son and father equally free; those who never reach it (the mentally incapable) remain under guardianship for life.
- **Temporary rule vs. perpetual honor** — Two distinct things are confused under "paternal power": the parents' right of tuition, which ends with the child's minority, and the child's duty of honor, gratitude, and support, which lasts for life but gives parents no power of command, legislation, or punishment.
- **Power tied to duty of care** — Parental power arises from, and is inseparable from, the duty to nourish and educate the child. A father who abandons that care loses the power; a foster-father who takes it up acquires it.
- **Inheritance as leverage** — A father's ordinary power over grown children rests only on his ability to bequeath his estate as he pleases; accepting an inheritance also means accepting the government the land lies under — a voluntary submission, not a natural tie.
- **Paternal vs. political power** — The two are perfectly distinct in foundation and purpose. Every subject who is a father has full paternal power over his children, and every prince owes his own parents filial duty — impossible if the two powers were the same.
- **Consensual origin of early monarchy** — In sparsely settled ages, children grown to adulthood tacitly consented to let their father keep exercising the executive power of the law of nature over the family, making him its monarch. The rule looks paternal but rests on consent.

## Modern Translation

### § 52

It may seem an irrelevant quibble, in a work of this kind, to find fault with words and names that are already established in the world. And yet it may be worthwhile to propose new ones when the old ones tend to lead people into mistakes — as the phrase "paternal power" probably has done. That phrase seems to place the power of parents over their children entirely in the father, as if the mother had no share in it; whereas if we consult either reason or revelation, we find that she has an equal title. This gives us grounds to ask whether it might not more properly be called *parental* power. For whatever obligation nature and the right of generation lay on children, it must surely bind them equally to both of the joint causes of their existence. And accordingly we see that God's positive law everywhere joins the two together, without distinction, when it commands the obedience of children: "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12); "Whoever curses his father or his mother" (Leviticus 20:9); "Each of you shall revere his mother and his father" (Leviticus 19:3); "Children, obey your parents," etc. (Ephesians 6:1). That is the consistent phrasing of both the Old and the New Testament.

### § 53

If this one point had been properly considered, without looking any deeper into the matter, it might have kept people from the gross mistakes they have made about this power of parents. As long as it went under the title of *paternal* power and so seemed to belong to the father alone, it could — without any great strain — be dressed up as absolute dominion and royal authority. But it would have sounded very odd, and the absurdity would have shown in the very name, if this supposed absolute power over children had been called *parental*; for that would have revealed that it belonged to the mother too. It hardly suits the purposes of those who argue so strenuously for the absolute power and authority of "fatherhood," as they call it, that the mother should have any share in it. And it would have given poor support to the monarchy they argue for, if the very name made it plain that the fundamental authority from which they derive their government by a single person was lodged not in one person but in two jointly. But let this matter of names pass.

### § 54

Although I said above, in Chapter II, that all men are by nature equal, I cannot be taken to mean equality of every sort. Age or virtue may give some people a just precedence. Outstanding ability and merit may raise others above the common level. Birth may oblige some, and alliance or favors received may oblige others, to show deference to those to whom nature, gratitude, or other considerations have made it due. Yet all of this is consistent with the equality all men share with respect to jurisdiction or dominion over one another. That was the equality I spoke of there, the kind relevant to the matter at hand: the equal right every man has to his natural freedom, without being subjected to the will or authority of any other man.

### § 55

Children, I admit, are not *born in* this full state of equality, though they are *born to* it. Their parents have a sort of rule and jurisdiction over them when they come into the world and for some time afterward — but it is only a temporary one. The bonds of this subjection are like the swaddling clothes children are wrapped and supported in during the weakness of infancy: age and reason loosen them as the child grows, until at last they drop off entirely and leave a man at his own free disposal.

### § 56

Adam was created a fully formed man, his body and mind in complete possession of their strength and reason, and so from the first instant of his existence he was able to provide for his own support and preservation, and to govern his actions by the dictates of the law of reason God had implanted in him. From him the world was peopled with his descendants, who are all born infants — weak, helpless, without knowledge or understanding. To make up for the defects of this imperfect state, until growth and age have removed them, Adam and Eve, and after them all parents, were placed by the law of nature under an obligation to preserve, nourish, and educate the children they had begotten — not as their own workmanship, but as the workmanship of their own Maker, the Almighty, to whom they would have to answer for them.

### § 57

The law that was to govern Adam was the same law that was to govern all his posterity: the law of reason. But his offspring entered the world by a different route than he did — by natural birth, which produced them ignorant and without the use of reason — and so they were not immediately under that law. No one can be under a law that has not been made known to him; and since this law is made known only by reason, someone who has not yet come to the use of his reason cannot be said to be under it. Adam's children, not being under this law of reason the moment they were born, were not immediately free. For law, in its true sense, is not so much the limitation as the *direction* of a free and intelligent agent toward his proper interest, and it prescribes nothing beyond what serves the general good of those under it. If people could be happier without it, the law, as a useless thing, would vanish of its own accord; and a fence that hedges us in only from bogs and precipices hardly deserves the name of confinement. So however the point may be misunderstood, the purpose of law is not to abolish or restrain freedom but to preserve and enlarge it. In every state of created beings capable of law, *where there is no law, there is no freedom*. Liberty means being free from restraint and violence from others, and that is impossible where there is no law. Freedom is not, as some tell us, "a liberty for every man to do whatever he pleases" — for who could be free, when every other man's whim might tyrannize over him? It is rather a liberty to dispose of and order, as he pleases, his person, actions, possessions, and his whole property, within the allowance of the laws he lives under — and in doing so not to be subject to the arbitrary will of another, but to follow his own will freely.

### § 58

The power that parents have over their children, then, arises from their duty to take care of their offspring during the imperfect state of childhood. What children need, and what parents are bound to provide, is to inform the mind and govern the actions of their still-ignorant minority, until reason takes over and relieves the parents of that burden. God gave man an understanding to direct his actions, and with it a freedom of will and liberty of acting that properly belong to it, within the bounds of the law he is under. But while a person is in a condition in which he has no understanding of his own to direct his will, he is not to have any will of his own to follow. Whoever understands for him must will for him too: that person must prescribe to his will and regulate his actions. But when he reaches the condition that made his father a free man, the son is a free man too.

### § 59

This holds under every law a man may be subject to, whether natural or civil. Is a man under the law of nature? What made him free under that law — what gave him the free disposal of his property according to his own will, within the bounds of that law? I answer: a state of maturity in which he can be presumed capable of knowing that law, so that he can keep his actions within its bounds. Once he has reached that state, he is presumed to know how far that law is to be his guide and how far he may use his freedom — and so he comes to have it. Until then, someone else must guide him, someone presumed to know how much liberty the law allows. If such a state of reason, such an age of discretion, made the father free, the same thing will make his son free. Is a man under the law of England? What made him free under that law — that is, gave him liberty to dispose of his actions and possessions according to his own will, within what that law permits? A capacity to know that law, which that law presumes at the age of twenty-one, and in some cases sooner. If this made the father free, it will make the son free too. Until then, we see, the law allows the son no will of his own: he is to be guided by the will of his father or guardian, who is to understand for him. And if the father dies and fails to appoint a deputy in his place — if he has not provided a tutor to govern his son during his minority, during his lack of understanding — the law takes care to do it: someone else must govern him and be a will for him, until he has reached a state of freedom and his understanding is fit to take over the government of his will. After that, father and son are equally free, just as tutor and pupil are after the pupil comes of age — equally subjects of the same law together, with no dominion left in the father over the life, liberty, or estate of his son, whether they live only in the state of nature and under its law, or under the positive laws of an established government.

### § 60

But if, through defects that can occur outside the ordinary course of nature, someone never reaches the degree of reason at which he could be presumed capable of knowing the law and living within its rules, he is never capable of being a free man. He is never turned loose to the disposal of his own will (because he knows no bounds to it, lacking understanding, its proper guide), but is kept under the tuition and government of others for as long as his own understanding is unequal to that charge. And so lunatics and idiots are never set free from the government of their parents. As Hooker puts it:

> "Children who have not yet reached the years at which they may have the use of right reason; innocents, who are excluded by a natural defect from ever having it; and thirdly, madmen, who for the present cannot possibly have the use of right reason to guide themselves — all these have for their guide the reason that guides other men who are tutors over them, to seek and procure their good for them." (Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 7)

All of which seems to be nothing more than the duty God and nature have laid on man, as on other creatures, to preserve their offspring until they can fend for themselves — and it hardly amounts to an example or proof of any royal authority in parents.

### § 61

Thus we are born free just as we are born rational — not that we actually have the exercise of either at birth: the age that brings the one brings the other with it. And so we can see how natural freedom and subjection to parents fit together, both resting on the same principle. A child is free by his father's title, by his father's understanding, which is to govern him until he has understanding of his own. The freedom of a man at years of discretion and the subjection of a child to his parents while still short of that age are so compatible, and so distinct, that even the most blinkered champions of monarchy by right of fatherhood cannot miss the difference; the most obstinate cannot help admitting that they are consistent. For suppose their doctrine were entirely true. Suppose the rightful heir of Adam were now known, and by that title seated as monarch on his throne, invested with all the absolute unlimited power Sir Robert Filmer talks of. If he died as soon as his own heir was born, wouldn't the child — however free, however sovereign he might be — have to remain in subjection to his mother and nurse, to tutors and governors, until age and education brought him the reason and ability to govern himself and others? The necessities of his life, the health of his body, and the instruction of his mind would require him to be directed by the will of others rather than his own. Yet would anyone think that this restraint and subjection were inconsistent with the liberty or sovereignty he had a right to, or that it stripped him of them, or that he gave away his empire to those who governed his minority? That government over him only prepared him better and sooner for it. If anyone were to ask me when my son is old enough to be free, I would answer: exactly when his monarch is old enough to govern. "But at what time," says the judicious Hooker (*Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 6), "a man may be said to have attained the use of reason sufficient to make him capable of those laws by which he is bound to guide his actions — this is much easier for common sense to discern than for anyone to determine by skill and learning."

### § 62

Commonwealths themselves take notice of this and allow for it: they recognize that there is a time when men are to begin acting as free men, and therefore until that time they do not require oaths of fealty or allegiance, or any other public acknowledgment of, or submission to, the government of their countries.

### § 63

The freedom of a man, then — his liberty of acting according to his own will — is grounded in his having reason, which can instruct him in the law he is to govern himself by and let him know how far he is left to the freedom of his own will. To turn him loose to unrestrained liberty before he has reason to guide him is not to grant him the privilege of his nature, which is to be free; it is to thrust him out among the brutes and abandon him to a condition as wretched, and as far beneath a man's, as theirs. This is what puts authority into the parents' hands to govern their children's minority. God has made it their business to bestow this care on their offspring, and has placed in them suitable inclinations of tenderness and concern to temper this power — to apply it, as His wisdom designed, to the children's good, for as long as they need to be under it.

### § 64

But what reasoning could inflate this care that parents owe their offspring into an absolute, arbitrary dominion of the father? His power reaches no further than this: to use whatever discipline he finds most effective to give his children strength and health of body and vigor and rectitude of mind, so as to fit them to be most useful to themselves and others; and, if his circumstances require it, to make them work for their own subsistence when they are able. And even in this power the mother has her share along with the father.

### § 65

Indeed, this power belongs so little to the father by any special right of nature — he holds it only as guardian of his children — that when he abandons his care of them, he loses his power over them. The power goes along with their nourishment and education, to which it is inseparably attached; and it belongs as fully to the foster-father of an abandoned child as to the natural father of another. So little power does the bare act of begetting give a man over his offspring, if all his care ends there and this is his only title to the name and authority of a father. And what becomes of this "paternal power" in that part of the world where one woman has more than one husband at a time? Or in those parts of America where, when husband and wife separate — which happens frequently — the children are all left with the mother, follow her, and are entirely under her care and provision? If the father dies while the children are young, don't they everywhere naturally owe the same obedience to their mother, during their minority, as they would to their father if he were alive? And will anyone say that the mother has a *legislative* power over her children — that she can make standing rules of perpetual obligation, by which they must regulate all the concerns of their property and have their liberty bounded for the whole course of their lives? Or that she can enforce those rules with capital punishments? For that is the distinctive power of the magistrate, of which the father has not even the shadow. His command over his children is only temporary, and does not reach their life or property. It is merely a help to the weakness and imperfection of their minority, a discipline necessary to their education. And though a father may dispose of his own possessions as he pleases, so long as his children are in no danger of perishing for want, his power does not extend to the lives or goods that their own industry, or another's generosity, has made theirs — nor to their liberty either, once they have arrived at the enfranchisement of the years of discretion. The father's empire then ends, and from that point on he can no more dispose of his son's liberty than of any other man's. And it must be far indeed from an absolute or perpetual jurisdiction, since a man may withdraw himself from it — having license from divine authority to "leave father and mother, and cleave to his wife."

### § 66

There comes a time, then, when a child becomes as free from subjection to the will and command of his father as the father himself is free from subjection to anyone else's will, and both are under no restraint except what is common to them both, whether the law of nature or the municipal law of their country. Yet this freedom does not exempt a son from the *honor* he owes his parents by the law of God and of nature. God made parents the instruments of His great design of continuing the human race, and the occasions of life to their children; and just as He laid on parents an obligation to nourish, preserve, and bring up their offspring, so He laid on children a perpetual obligation to honor their parents. This honor consists in an inward esteem and reverence, shown in every outward expression. It bars the child from anything that might ever injure or insult, disturb or endanger, the happiness or life of those from whom he received his own; and it binds him to every action of defense, relief, assistance, and comfort toward those by whose means he came into being and was made capable of any of life's enjoyments. From this obligation no condition, no freedom, can release children. But this is a very different thing from giving parents a power of command over their children, or an authority to make laws for them and dispose of their lives or liberties as they please. It is one thing to owe honor, respect, gratitude, and assistance; quite another to be required to give absolute obedience and submission. A monarch on his throne owes his mother the honor due to parents — and yet this neither lessens his authority nor subjects him to her government.

### § 67

The subjection of a minor places in the father a temporary government, which ends with the child's minority. The honor due from a child places in the parents a perpetual right to respect, reverence, support, and compliance too — more or less, according as the father's care, expense, and kindness in the child's upbringing have been more or less. This does not end with minority but holds through every stage and condition of a man's life. The failure to distinguish these two powers — namely, the father's right of tuition during minority, and his right to honor throughout his life — has probably caused much of the confusion about this matter. For, to speak precisely, the first of them is really the privilege of children and the duty of parents, rather than any prerogative of paternal power. The nourishment and education of their children is a charge so binding on parents, for their children's good, that nothing can excuse them from taking care of it. And although the power of commanding and disciplining them goes along with it, God has woven into the fabric of human nature such tenderness toward offspring that there is little fear of parents using their power too harshly; excess is seldom on the severe side, the strong bias of nature pulling the other way. That is why God Almighty, when He wished to express His gentle dealing with the Israelites, told them that though He chastened them, "He chastened them as a man chastens his son" (Deuteronomy 8:5) — that is, with tenderness and affection, keeping them under no harsher discipline than was absolutely best for them, and which it would have been less kindness to relax. This is the power to which children are commanded to be obedient, so that the pains and care of their parents may not be increased or ill repaid.

### § 68

On the other side, honor and support — everything gratitude requires a child to return for the benefits received by and from his parents — is the indispensable duty of the child and the proper privilege of the parents. This is intended for the parents' advantage, as the other is for the child's; though education, the parents' duty, seems to carry the most power, because the ignorance and infirmities of childhood need restraint and correction, which is a visible exercise of rule and a kind of dominion. And the duty comprehended in the word *honor* requires less obedience, though the obligation is stronger on grown children than on young ones. For who can suppose that the command "Children, obey your parents" requires of a man who has children of his own the same submission to his father that it requires of his own young children toward him — or that by this precept he would be bound to obey all his father's commands if the father, out of some conceit of authority, were indiscreet enough to go on treating him as a boy?

### § 69

The first part of paternal power, then — or rather duty — which is education, belongs to the father in such a way that it terminates at a set season: when the business of education is finished, it ceases of itself, and it can also be transferred beforehand. A man may put the tuition of his son into other hands; and a father who has apprenticed his son to another man has, for that period, released him from a great part of his obedience both to himself and to the boy's mother. But the whole duty of honor — the other part — remains entire to them both nonetheless; nothing can cancel it. It is so inseparable from both parents that the father's authority cannot strip the mother of this right, nor can any man release his son from honoring the woman who bore him. But both of these things fall far short of a power to make laws and enforce them with penalties reaching estate, liberty, limbs, and life. The power of commanding ends with minority; and although after that, honor and respect, support and defense, and whatever gratitude can oblige a man to render for the greatest benefits he is naturally capable of receiving, are always due from a son to his parents, none of this puts a scepter into the father's hand, no sovereign power of commanding. He has no dominion over his son's property or actions; nor any right that his will should dictate to his son's in all things — though it may well befit the son, in many things not too inconvenient to himself and his family, to defer to it.

### § 70

A man may owe honor and respect to an elder or a wise man; defense to his child or his friend; relief and support to the distressed; and gratitude to a benefactor — to such a degree that all he has and all he can do cannot adequately repay it. But none of these gives anyone any authority, any right, to make laws over the person who owes them. And plainly, all of this is due not merely to the bare title of *father* — not only because, as I have said, it is owed to the mother as well, but because these obligations to parents, and the degree of what is required of children, may vary with the different care and kindness, trouble and expense, that is often spent on one child more than another.

### § 71

This explains how it comes about that parents in political societies, where they are themselves subjects, retain a power over their children and have as much right to their subjection as parents in the state of nature. This could not possibly be the case if all political power were merely paternal, and the two were in truth one and the same thing: for then, all paternal power being in the prince, the subject could naturally have none of it. But these two powers, political and paternal, are so perfectly distinct and separate — built on such different foundations, and given for such different ends — that every subject who is a father has as much paternal power over his children as the prince has over his; and every prince who has parents owes them as much filial duty and obedience as the humblest of his subjects owe theirs. So paternal power cannot contain any part or degree of that kind of dominion which a prince or magistrate has over his subject.

### § 72

The obligation on parents to bring up their children, and the obligation on children to honor their parents, contain between them all the power on the one side and all the submission on the other that properly belong to this relationship. Yet there is another power ordinarily in the father's hands, by which he keeps a hold on his children's obedience. It is a power he shares with other men; but because the occasions for exercising it arise almost constantly for fathers within their private families, while instances of it elsewhere are rare and less noticed, it passes in the world for part of paternal jurisdiction. This is the power men generally have to bestow their estates on those who please them best. The father's possessions are the expectation and inheritance of his children, ordinarily in certain proportions fixed by the law and custom of each country; yet it is commonly within the father's power to bestow them with a more sparing or a more generous hand, according to how this or that child's behavior has suited his will and humor.

### § 73

This is no small hold on the obedience of children. And since the enjoyment of land always carries with it submission to the government of the country of which that land is a part, it has commonly been supposed that a father could bind his posterity to the government of which he himself was a subject, and that his compact obligated them. In fact, the submission is only a necessary condition attached to the land: the inheritance of an estate under a given government reaches only those who will take it on that condition, and so it is no natural tie or engagement but a voluntary submission. Every man's children, being by nature as free as he himself or any of his ancestors ever were, may, while they remain in that freedom, choose what society they will join and what commonwealth they will put themselves under. But if they want to enjoy their ancestors' inheritance, they must take it on the same terms their ancestors held it, and submit to all the conditions attached to such a possession. By this power, it is true, fathers do oblige their children to obey them even past minority, and most commonly also subject them to one political power or another. But neither of these comes from any special right of fatherhood; it comes from the reward they hold in their hands to enforce and repay such compliance. It is no more power than a Frenchman has over an Englishman who, in hopes of an estate the Frenchman will leave him, will certainly be strongly tied to his obedience; and if, when the estate is left to him, he wants to enjoy it, he must of course take it on the conditions attached to the possession of land in the country where it lies, whether France or England.

### § 74

To conclude, then. The father's power of commanding extends no further than his children's minority, and only to a degree fit for the discipline and government of that age. The honor and respect, and all that the Romans called *piety*, which children indispensably owe their parents all their lives and in every condition — together with all the support and defense due to them — gives the father no power of *governing*, that is, of making laws and enacting penalties on his children; and by none of this does he acquire any dominion over the property or actions of his son. Yet it is easy to see how, in the first ages of the world — and in places even now where the thinness of population lets families move apart into unclaimed territory, with room to relocate and settle in still-vacant land — the father of a family could become its *prince*.* He had been a ruler from the beginning of his children's infancy; and since it would be hard for them to live together without some government, it was most natural that government should, by the express or tacit consent of the children once they were grown, rest in the father — where it seemed simply to continue without any change. In fact nothing more was required for this than to permit the father to exercise alone, within his family, that executive power of the law of nature which every free man naturally has — and by that permission to resign to him a monarchical power for as long as they remained in the family. But that this rested not on any paternal right but only on his children's consent is evident from this: nobody doubts that if a stranger, brought to his family by chance or business, had killed one of his children there, or committed any other crime, the father could condemn him and put him to death or otherwise punish him, just as he could any of his children. Yet that he could not possibly do by virtue of any paternal authority over someone who was not his child; he could do it only by virtue of that executive power of the law of nature which, as a man, he had a right to. And he alone could punish the offender in his family only because his children, out of respect for him, had set aside the exercise of that power themselves, deferring to the dignity and authority they were willing should remain in him above the rest of the family.

> *"It is therefore no improbable opinion which the arch-philosopher held: that the chief person in every household was always, as it were, a king; so that when numbers of households joined together in civil societies, kings were the first kind of governors among them. This, it seems, is also why the name of *fathers* continued to be used for those who, from fathers, were made rulers; and likewise the ancient custom of governors doing as Melchizedek did — being kings, they exercised the office of priests, as fathers did at first — perhaps grew from the same source. However, this is not the only kind of government that has been received in the world. The inconveniences of one kind have caused various others to be devised; so that, in a word, all public government, of whatever kind, seems plainly to have arisen from deliberate advice, consultation, and agreement among men, judging it convenient and beneficial — there being no impossibility in nature, considered in itself, that men might have lived without any public government." (Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 10)*

### § 75

Thus it was easy, and almost natural, for children — by a tacit and scarcely avoidable consent — to make way for the father's authority and government. They had been accustomed in childhood to follow his direction and to refer their small disputes to him; and when they were grown men, who was better suited to rule them? Their small properties, and smaller covetousness, seldom produced any great controversies; and when one did arise, where could they find a fitter umpire than the man by whose care every one of them had been sustained and brought up, and who had tenderness for them all? It is no wonder they drew no line between minority and full age, and paid no attention to twenty-one or any other age that might have made them free disposers of themselves and their fortunes, when they could have no desire to be out of their pupilage. The government they had been under during it continued to be more their protection than their restraint; and nowhere could they find greater security for their peace, liberties, and fortunes than in the rule of a father.

### § 76

Thus the natural fathers of families, by an imperceptible change, became their political monarchs as well. And as they happened to live long and leave capable and worthy heirs for several successions — or otherwise — they laid the foundations of hereditary or elective kingdoms, under various constitutions and customs, according as chance, contrivance, or circumstance happened to shape them. But suppose someone argues that princes hold their titles by their fathers' right, and that the fact that fathers were commonly the ones in whose hands we find, *de facto*, the exercise of government is sufficient proof of a natural right of fathers to political authority. I say: if that argument is good, it will prove just as strongly that all princes — indeed, princes only — ought to be priests, since it is just as certain that in the beginning *the father of the family was priest* as that he was ruler in his own household.
