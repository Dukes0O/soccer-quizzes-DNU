# Chapter XIII — Of the Subordination of the Powers of the Commonwealth

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 149–158
>
> [← Of the Legislative, Executive, and Federative Power](12-of-the-legislative-executive-and-federative-power.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Prerogative →](14-of-prerogative.md)

## Overview

Having distinguished the legislative, executive, and federative powers in the previous chapter, Locke now ranks them. His central claim is that while a government stands, the legislative is supreme — whatever makes laws for others must be superior to them — and every other power in the commonwealth is derived from it and subordinate to it. But that supremacy is itself conditional. The legislative is only a *fiduciary* power, a trust granted for a specific end: the preservation of the community. Behind it therefore stands an even deeper supreme power, held perpetually by the people themselves, to remove or replace a legislature that acts against its trust. This popular power, Locke is careful to say, is not part of any form of government; it comes into play only when government is dissolved.

The middle sections work out the apparent exceptions. In monarchies where a single person holds the executive and also shares in the legislative, that person may loosely be called "supreme" — not as supreme lawmaker, but as supreme executor of the law. Oaths of allegiance are sworn to him only in that capacity: he is the "image, phantom, or representative of the commonwealth," with no will or power but that of the law. The moment he acts by his own private will instead, he degrades himself into a mere private person with no right to anyone's obedience. Any executive that does *not* share in the legislative is straightforwardly subordinate to it and removable at pleasure.

Locke then turns to a practical machinery question: the legislature need not — and should not — sit permanently, while the executive must always be in operation. So who decides when the legislature meets? Often the executive holds the power of convening and dismissing it. Locke insists this convening power is another fiduciary trust, lodged with the executive only because no framer of a constitution could foresee every future emergency; it gives the executive no superiority. And if an executive uses force to *prevent* the legislature from meeting when it ought to, that is force without authority — a state of war against the people, who may rightfully remove the obstruction by force.

The chapter closes with a striking application: England's "rotten boroughs." Time changes everything — cities decay, new places grow rich and populous — so a scheme of representation that once made sense becomes absurd, with a depopulated village returning as many representatives as a great county. Since no power inferior to the people can alter the constitution of the legislative, this seems incurable. Locke's answer is his first extended appeal to *prerogative* (the subject of the next chapter): if the executive re-regulates representation by true reason and fair proportion rather than outdated custom, it has not created a new legislature but restored the old and true one. Whatever is done manifestly for the good of the people, on just and equal measures, is and always will be just prerogative.

## Key Concepts

- **Subordination of powers** — The ranking of the commonwealth's powers: the legislative is supreme over the executive, federative, and all ministerial powers, which are derived from it and accountable to it; and the people are supreme over the legislative itself.
- **Fiduciary power / trust** — Locke's master idea in this chapter: political power is not owned but held in trust for a specified end (the people's preservation). Any power granted in trust is limited by its end, and is forfeited whenever that end is manifestly neglected or opposed, reverting to those who gave it.
- **The people's supreme power** — The community perpetually retains the ultimate power to remove or alter a legislature that betrays its trust. This power belongs to no form of government; it can operate only when the government is dissolved.
- **The king as "image of the commonwealth"** — A monarch who shares in the legislative may be called supreme as supreme *executor* of the law, not as supreme legislator. Allegiance is obedience according to law; when he acts by his private will instead of the public will declared in the laws, he becomes a mere private person with no right to obedience.
- **Convening power** — The executive's power to assemble and dismiss the legislature. It exists because fixed constitutional schedules cannot anticipate every emergency, and it is a trust for the people's safety, not a mark of superiority over the legislature.
- **Force without authority** — Using the commonwealth's force to block the legislature from meeting when the constitution or the public need requires it puts the executive into a state of war with the people, who may oppose force with force.
- **Prerogative** — Introduced here ahead of Chapter XIV: a power in the prince's hands to provide for the public good in cases that unforeseen circumstances make impossible to regulate by fixed, unalterable law. Whatever is done manifestly for the people's good is just prerogative.
- **Fair and equal representation** — Representation ought to be proportioned to the assistance each place gives the public, not to obsolete custom. Correcting decayed representation restores the true legislature rather than erecting a new one.

## Modern Translation

### § 149

In a constituted commonwealth — one standing on its own foundation and acting according to its own nature, that is, acting for the preservation of the community — there can be only one supreme power, the legislative, to which all the rest are and must be subordinate. Yet the legislative is only a fiduciary power, a power held in trust to act for certain ends. So there still remains in the people a supreme power to remove or alter the legislative whenever they find it acting contrary to the trust placed in it. For all power given in trust for attaining an end is limited by that end; whenever that end is manifestly neglected or opposed, the trust is necessarily forfeited, and the power devolves back into the hands of those who gave it, who may place it anew wherever they think best for their safety and security. In this way the community perpetually retains a supreme power of saving itself from the attempts and schemes of anybody — even of its own legislators — whenever they are foolish enough, or wicked enough, to lay and carry out designs against the liberties and properties of the subject. No man and no society of men has the power to surrender their own preservation — or, what comes to the same thing, the means of it — to the absolute will and arbitrary dominion of another. So whenever anyone tries to reduce them to such a slavish condition, they will always have a right to preserve what they lack the power to part with, and to rid themselves of those who invade this fundamental, sacred, and unalterable law of self-preservation, for whose sake they entered into society in the first place. In this respect the community may be said to be always the supreme power — but not as considered under any form of government, because this power of the people can never take effect until the government is dissolved.

### § 150

In all cases, as long as the government subsists, the legislative is the supreme power. Whatever can give laws to another must necessarily be superior to it. The legislative is the legislative of the society only by virtue of its right to make laws for all the parts and for every member of the society, prescribing rules for their actions and giving power to execute those rules when they are broken. Therefore the legislative must be supreme, and all other powers, lodged in any members or parts of the society, are derived from it and subordinate to it.

### § 151

In some commonwealths the legislative is not always in session, and the executive is vested in a single person who also has a share in the legislative. There, that single person may in a quite tolerable sense also be called supreme — not because he has in himself all the supreme power, which is the power of lawmaking, but because he holds the supreme *execution*, from which all inferior magistrates derive their various subordinate powers, or at least the greatest part of them. Since he also has no legislative superior to him — no law can be made without his consent, and he can hardly be expected to consent to a law that would subject him to the other part of the legislative — he is, in this sense, properly enough called supreme. But note this: although oaths of allegiance and fealty are sworn to him, they are not sworn to him as supreme legislator, but as supreme executor of the law, which was made by a power he holds jointly with others. Allegiance is nothing but obedience according to law. When he violates the law, he has no right to obedience, and he can claim it only as the public person vested with the power of the law. He is thus to be regarded as the image, phantom, or representative of the commonwealth, animated by the will of the society as declared in its laws; he has no will and no power except that of the law. But when he abandons this representation, this public will, and acts by his own private will, he degrades himself. He is then merely a single private person, without power and without any will that has a right to obedience — for the members of the society owe obedience to nothing but the public will of the society.

### § 152

Executive power placed anywhere except in a person who also shares in the legislative is visibly subordinate and accountable to the legislative, and may be changed and displaced at pleasure. So it is not supreme executive power as such that is exempt from subordination; it is supreme executive power vested in someone who, having a share in the legislative, has no distinct superior legislature to be subordinate and accountable to — beyond whatever he himself joins in and consents to. He is thus no more subordinate than he himself thinks fit, which we may safely conclude will be very little indeed. Of the other ministerial and subordinate powers in a commonwealth there is no need to speak. They are multiplied with such infinite variety in the different customs and constitutions of distinct commonwealths that it is impossible to give a detailed account of them all. Only this much, which our present purpose requires, need be noted about them: none of them has any kind of authority beyond what is delegated to it by positive grant and commission, and all of them are accountable to some other power in the commonwealth.

### § 153

It is not necessary — no, not even convenient — that the legislative be always in session; but it is absolutely necessary that the executive power should be, because there is not always need of new laws to be made, but there is always need of execution of the laws already made. When the legislative has put the execution of the laws it makes into other hands, it still retains the power to take that execution back out of those hands when it finds cause, and to punish any maladministration against the laws. The same holds for the federative power: both it and the executive are ministerial and subordinate to the legislative, which, as has been shown, is supreme in a constituted commonwealth. The legislative in this case is assumed to consist of several persons (for if it were a single person, it could not help but be always in being, and would then, as supreme, naturally hold the supreme executive power along with the legislative). It may assemble and exercise its lawmaking at the times its original constitution appoints, or at times set by its own adjournment, or whenever it pleases — if neither of these has fixed a time and no other way of convening it has been prescribed. For since the supreme power was placed in it by the people, it is always in it, and its members may exercise it whenever they please, unless their original constitution limits them to certain seasons, or unless by an act of their own supreme power they have adjourned to a certain time — and when that time comes, they have a right to assemble and act again.

### § 154

Suppose the legislative, or any part of it, is made up of representatives chosen for a term by the people, who afterwards return to the ordinary condition of subjects and have no share in the legislature except upon a new election. Then this power of choosing must also be exercised by the people — either at certain appointed seasons, or when they are summoned to it. In the latter case, the power of convening the legislative is ordinarily placed in the executive, with one of two limitations as to timing. Either the original constitution requires the representatives to assemble and act at fixed intervals — in which case the executive does nothing but ministerially issue the directions for electing and assembling them according to due forms; or else it is left to the executive's prudence to call them by new elections whenever the occasions or emergencies of the public require the amendment of old laws or the making of new ones, or the redress or prevention of any harms that press upon or threaten the people.

### § 155

Here it may be asked: what if the executive, possessed as it is of the force of the commonwealth, uses that force to prevent the legislative from meeting and acting when the original constitution or the public emergencies require it? I answer: using force upon the people without authority, and contrary to the trust placed in the one who does it, is a state of war with the people, who have a right to reinstate their legislative in the exercise of its power. Having erected a legislative with the intent that it should exercise the power of making laws — either at certain set times or when there is need of it — the people, when they are hindered by any force from something so necessary to the society, something in which their safety and preservation consist, have a right to remove that force by force. In all states and conditions, the true remedy for force without authority is to oppose force to it. The use of force without authority always puts the one who uses it into a state of war as the aggressor, and makes him liable to be treated accordingly.

### § 156

The power of assembling and dismissing the legislative, when placed in the executive, does not give the executive superiority over it. It is a fiduciary trust placed in the executive for the safety of the people, in a matter where the uncertainty and changeableness of human affairs could not bear a steady, fixed rule. It was not possible for the first framers of the government to have such foresight — to be such masters of future events — that they could fix in advance, for all times to come, schedules of return and duration for the assemblies of the legislative that would exactly answer every emergency of the commonwealth. The best remedy that could be found for this defect was to entrust the matter to the prudence of someone who would always be present, and whose business it was to watch over the public good. Constant, frequent meetings of the legislative, and long continuations of its sessions without necessary occasion, could not help but be burdensome to the people, and would necessarily in time produce more dangerous harms. Yet the rapid turn of affairs might sometimes require the legislature's immediate help; any delay in convening it might endanger the public. And sometimes its business might be so great that the limited time of its sitting would be too short for its work, robbing the public of the benefit that only its mature deliberation could provide. What, then, could be done in this situation — to keep the community from being exposed, at some time or other, to imminent hazard on one side or the other by fixed intervals and schedules set for the meeting and acting of the legislative — except to entrust it to the prudence of someone who, being present and acquainted with the state of public affairs, could use this prerogative for the public good? And where could this be better placed than in the hands of the person already entrusted with the execution of the laws for the same end? So, supposing the regulation of times for the assembling and sitting of the legislative was not settled by the original constitution, it naturally fell into the hands of the executive — not as an arbitrary power depending on his good pleasure, but with this standing trust: that it be exercised only for the public good, as the events of the times and the change of affairs might require. Which arrangement carries the least inconvenience — settled periods of convening, or a liberty left to the prince to convoke the legislative, or perhaps a mixture of both — it is not my business here to inquire. My point is only to show that although the executive may have the prerogative of convening and dissolving such assemblies of the legislative, it is not thereby superior to it.

### § 157

The things of this world are in so constant a flux that nothing remains long in the same state. People, riches, trade, and power change their locations; flourishing, mighty cities fall to ruin and in time prove to be neglected, desolate corners, while other unfrequented places grow into populous regions filled with wealth and inhabitants. But things do not always change at the same rate, and private interest often keeps up customs and privileges after the reasons for them have ceased. So it often happens, in governments where part of the legislative consists of representatives chosen by the people, that over the course of time this representation becomes very unequal and out of all proportion to the reasons on which it was first established. To see what gross absurdities the following of custom, after reason has abandoned it, can lead to, we need only observe this: the bare name of a town — of which not even the ruins remain, where scarcely as much housing as a sheep-pen, or more inhabitants than a single shepherd, can be found — sends as many representatives to the grand assembly of lawmakers as a whole county numerous in people and powerful in riches. Strangers stand amazed at this, and everyone must confess it needs a remedy. Yet most think a remedy hard to find, because the constitution of the legislative is the original and supreme act of the society, prior to all positive laws within it and depending wholly on the people, so that no inferior power can alter it. And therefore, since the people — once the legislative is constituted — have, in such a government as we have been describing, no power to act as long as the government stands, this inconvenience is thought incapable of a remedy.

### § 158

*Salus populi suprema lex* — "the safety of the people is the supreme law" — is so just and fundamental a rule that whoever sincerely follows it cannot dangerously go wrong. Suppose, then, that the executive, who has the power of convening the legislative, attends to the true *proportion* of representation rather than its mere *fashion*, and regulates — not by old custom but by true reason — the number of members from every place that has a right to be distinctly represented. (No part of the people, however incorporated, can claim such a right except in proportion to the assistance it affords to the public.) That executive cannot be judged to have set up a new legislative; rather, it has restored the old and true one, and rectified the disorders that the succession of time had introduced imperceptibly and inevitably. For since it is the interest as well as the intention of the people to have a fair and equal representative body, whoever brings it nearest to that is an undoubted friend to the government and an establisher of it, and cannot fail to have the consent and approval of the community. Prerogative is nothing but a power in the hands of the prince to provide for the public good in cases which, depending on unforeseen and uncertain occurrences, fixed and unalterable laws could not safely direct. Whatever is done manifestly for the good of the people, and for establishing the government upon its true foundations, is, and always will be, just prerogative. The power of creating new corporations, and with them new representatives, carries with it the assumption that in time the measures of representation may change: that places may acquire a just right to be represented which had none before, and by the same reasoning, places which had that right may cease to hold it, becoming too inconsiderable for such a privilege. What makes an inroad upon the government is not a change from the present state of things — which perhaps corruption or decay has introduced — but the *tendency* of a change to injure or oppress the people, and to set up one part or party with a distinction from, and an unequal subjection of, the rest. Whatever must be acknowledged to be to the advantage of the society and the people in general, based on just and lasting measures, will always justify itself once done. And whenever the people choose their representatives on just and undeniably equal measures, suitable to the original frame of the government, there can be no doubt that this is the will and act of the society, whoever permitted or caused them to do it.
