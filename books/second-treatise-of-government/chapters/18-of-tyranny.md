# Chapter XVIII — Of Tyranny

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 199–210
>
> [← Of Usurpation](17-of-usurpation.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the Dissolution of Government →](19-of-the-dissolution-of-government.md)

## Overview

Having defined usurpation in the previous chapter as the exercise of power that rightfully belongs to someone else, Locke now defines tyranny as something worse: the exercise of power *beyond* right — power that no one could ever have a right to. A ruler becomes a tyrant the moment he substitutes his own will for the law and uses his position for his private advantage rather than the good of his people. Strikingly, Locke builds this definition on the authority of King James I himself, quoting the king's own speeches to Parliament to show that even a monarch acknowledged that a king who stops ruling by his laws "degenerates into a tyrant." Locke also insists that tyranny is not unique to monarchy — assemblies and councils can be tyrants too.

The core argumentative move of the chapter comes in § 202: wherever law ends, tyranny begins. An official who exceeds his legal authority ceases, in that act, to be a magistrate at all, and may be resisted like any private trespasser. Locke points out that everyone already accepts this for lesser officers — a constable with a warrant to arrest you in the street may still be resisted if he tries to break into your house — and he challenges the reader to explain why the same logic should not apply to the highest magistrate. Greatness of station, he argues, aggravates rather than excuses abuse of power.

The rest of the chapter answers the obvious objection (§ 203): won't a right of resistance dissolve all government into anarchy? Locke's answer (§ 204–208) is a series of limiting conditions. Only unjust and unlawful force may be resisted; where the king's person is legally sacred, resistance targets only his agents; a commission from the king cannot authorize what the law forbids; force is justified only where appeal to the law is cut off (illustrated by the famous highwayman example in § 207); and isolated victims of tyranny, however wronged, cannot realistically shake a government the people at large still trust.

But the final sections (§ 209–210) carry the chapter's real sting. When illegal acts touch the majority, or when a pattern of precedents convinces the people that their laws, estates, liberties, and religion are all in danger, nothing can stop them from resisting — and rulers who bring themselves under such general suspicion deserve little pity, since the remedy (visibly governing for the public good) is so easy. The closing image of the ship's captain persistently steering for Algiers, whatever temporary detours the weather forces on him, tells readers how to recognize a settled design of tyranny behind fair pretences — and sets up the doctrine of dissolution of government in the chapter that follows.

## Key Concepts

- **Tyranny** — The exercise of power beyond right, which nobody can have a right to: using the power in one's hands not for the good of those under it, but for one's own private, separate advantage. It occurs whenever a ruler makes his will, rather than the law, the rule.
- **Tyranny is not confined to monarchy** — Any form of government — one ruler or many — becomes tyrannical when power entrusted for the people's good is turned to impoverishing, harassing, or subduing them, as with the Thirty Tyrants at Athens and the Decemviri at Rome.
- **Where law ends, tyranny begins** — An official who exceeds his legal authority and uses force the law does not allow ceases in that act to be a magistrate, and may be opposed like any other man who invades another's rights by force.
- **Authority versus commission** — It is authority, not a commission, that gives the right to act; and there can be no authority against the law. A king cannot empower anyone to act illegally, so his agents' unlawful acts may be resisted even under royal commission.
- **The sacredness of the king's person** — In some constitutions the prince's person is by law exempt from all force and judicial censure. Locke accepts this as a wise arrangement (the harm one man can do alone is limited) while insisting it does not shield the king's subordinate agents from resistance.
- **Force only where appeal to law is barred** — Resistance is lawful only against force that cuts off all remedy at law; where the injured party can be relieved by legal appeal, there is no pretence for force. Hence one may kill a highwayman threatening one's life but not the man who wrongfully withholds one's money.
- **The limits of private resistance** — One or a few oppressed men have a right to defend themselves, but they cannot disturb a government the body of the people still trusts, any more than a raving madman could overturn a settled state.
- **General suspicion and the design of tyranny** — When illegal acts reach the majority, or a long train of actions reveals a settled design against the people's laws, liberties, and religion, the people cannot be kept from resisting — the most dangerous condition a government can put itself in, and the least pitiable, because so easily avoided.

## Modern Translation

### § 199

Just as usurpation is the exercise of power that someone else has a right to, tyranny is the exercise of power *beyond* right — power that nobody can have a right to. Tyranny means using whatever power one holds not for the good of those under it, but for one's own private, separate advantage. It exists whenever a ruler — whatever his title — makes his will, and not the law, the rule, and directs his commands and actions not toward preserving his people's property, but toward satisfying his own ambition, revenge, greed, or some other unruly passion.

### § 200

If anyone doubts that this is true or reasonable because it comes from the obscure hand of a mere subject, I hope the authority of a king will make it acceptable to him. King James I, in his speech to Parliament in 1603, told them this:

> "I will always prefer the well-being of the public and of the whole commonwealth, in making good laws and constitutions, over any particular and private ends of my own — always considering the wealth and well-being of the commonwealth to be my greatest happiness and worldly felicity. That is the point on which a lawful king directly differs from a tyrant. For I acknowledge that the special and greatest point of difference between a rightful king and a usurping tyrant is this: whereas the proud and ambitious tyrant thinks his kingdom and people exist only to satisfy his desires and unreasonable appetites, the righteous and just king acknowledges, on the contrary, that he himself exists to secure the wealth and property of his people."

And again, in his speech to Parliament in 1609, he said this:

> "The king binds himself by a double oath to observe the fundamental laws of his kingdom: tacitly, by the very fact of being a king, and so bound to protect both the people and the laws of his kingdom; and expressly, by his oath at his coronation. So every just king in a settled kingdom is bound to observe the pact he has made with his people, through his laws, framing his government accordingly — just like the pact God made with Noah after the flood: 'From now on, seed-time and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night shall not cease while the earth remains.' And therefore a king governing in a settled kingdom stops being a king, and degenerates into a tyrant, as soon as he stops ruling according to his laws."

And a little later:

> "Therefore all kings who are not tyrants, or perjurers, will be glad to bind themselves within the limits of their laws; and those who persuade them otherwise are vipers and pests, both to the kings themselves and to the commonwealth."

So that learned king, who well understood the nature of things, located the difference between a king and a tyrant in this alone: the one makes the laws the boundaries of his power and the good of the public the goal of his government; the other makes everything give way to his own will and appetite.

### § 201

It is a mistake to think this fault belongs only to monarchies. Other forms of government are just as liable to it. For wherever the power placed in anyone's hands for governing the people and preserving their property is applied to other ends — used to impoverish, harass, or subdue them to the arbitrary and irregular commands of those who hold it — there it immediately becomes tyranny, whether those using the power that way are one person or many. Thus we read of the Thirty Tyrants at Athens as well as the one at Syracuse; and the intolerable domination of the Decemviri at Rome was no better.

### § 202

Wherever law ends, tyranny begins, if the law is transgressed to another's harm. Whoever in authority exceeds the power the law gives him, and uses the force under his command to inflict on a subject what the law does not allow, ceases in that act to be a magistrate; and, since he is acting without authority, he may be opposed like any other man who forcibly invades another's rights. This is already admitted for subordinate magistrates. A man who has authority to arrest me in the street may be resisted as a thief and a robber if he tries to break into my house to execute a writ — even though I know he carries a warrant and legal authority that would empower him to arrest me outdoors. I would gladly be told why this principle should not hold for the highest magistrate as well as the lowest. Is it reasonable that the eldest brother, because he inherited the greatest part of his father's estate, should thereby have a right to take away any part of his younger brothers' shares? Or that a rich man who owned a whole region should thereby have a right to seize, whenever he pleased, his poor neighbor's cottage and garden? Being rightfully possessed of great power and riches, far beyond most of the sons of Adam, is so far from being an excuse — much less a justification — for plunder and oppression (which is what harming another without authority amounts to) that it is a great aggravation of it. Exceeding the bounds of authority is no more a right in a great officer than in a petty one; no more justifiable in a king than in a constable. Indeed it is that much worse in the king, because greater trust has been placed in him, he already has a far larger share than the rest of his brethren, and he is supposed — given the advantages of his education, position, and counselors — to know better the measures of right and wrong.

### § 203

May a prince's commands, then, be opposed? May he be resisted whenever anyone feels aggrieved, and merely imagines he has not been treated rightly? That would unhinge and overturn all political societies, and instead of government and order leave nothing but anarchy and confusion.

### § 204

To this I answer: force is to be opposed to nothing but unjust and unlawful force. Whoever offers resistance in any other case draws on himself just condemnation from both God and man; so no such danger or confusion will follow as is often suggested. For:

### § 205

First: in some countries the person of the prince is by law sacred, so that whatever he commands or does, his person remains free from all questioning or violence — not liable to force, or to any judicial censure or condemnation. Yet opposition may still be made to the illegal acts of any inferior officer, or anyone else commissioned by him — unless the prince will actually put himself into a state of war with his people, dissolve the government, and leave them to the self-defense that belongs to everyone in the state of nature. For who can tell where such things will end? And a neighboring kingdom has shown the world a strange example. In all other cases the sacredness of his person exempts him from all harm; as long as the government stands, he is secure from all violence whatsoever — and no wiser arrangement could be devised. For the harm he can do in his own person is not likely to happen often, nor to extend very far; nor can he, by his single strength, subvert the laws or oppress the body of the people. So even if some prince should be weak and ill-natured enough to want to do it, the inconvenience of a few particular injuries that may sometimes occur, when a rash prince comes to the throne, is well repaid by the public peace and the security of the government that come from placing the person of the chief magistrate out of danger's reach. It is safer for the body politic that a few private men should occasionally be exposed to suffering than that the head of the republic should be easily exposed, and on slight occasions.

### § 206

Secondly: this privilege belongs only to the king's person, and so it does not prevent those who use unjust force from being questioned, opposed, and resisted, even though they claim a commission from him that the law does not authorize. This is plain in the case of a man who holds the king's writ to arrest someone — a full commission from the king — and yet he may not break open a man's house to do it, nor execute this royal command on certain days or in certain places, even though the commission contains no such exceptions. Those limits are set by the law, and if anyone transgresses them, the king's commission does not excuse him. For since the king's authority is given to him only by the law, he cannot empower anyone to act against the law, nor justify him in doing so by his commission. The commission or command of any magistrate, where he has no authority, is as void and meaningless as that of any private man. The only difference between the magistrate and the private man is that the magistrate has some authority, up to a certain point and for certain ends, while the private man has none at all. For it is not the commission but the authority that gives the right to act — and against the laws there can be no authority. Yet notwithstanding such resistance, the king's person and authority both remain secure, so there is no danger to governor or government.

### § 207

Thirdly: suppose a government in which the person of the chief magistrate is *not* thus sacred. Even so, this doctrine — that it is lawful to resist all unlawful exercises of his power — will not endanger him or embroil the government on every slight occasion. For where the injured party can be relieved and his damages repaired by appeal to the law, there can be no pretext for force, which is to be used only where a man is cut off from appealing to the law. Nothing is to be counted hostile force except what leaves no remedy of such an appeal; and only that kind of force puts the man who uses it into a state of war, and makes it lawful to resist him. A man with a sword in his hand demands my purse on the highway, when perhaps I have not twelve pence in my pocket: this man I may lawfully kill. To another man I hand a hundred pounds to hold only while I dismount; he refuses to return it once I have remounted, and draws his sword to defend his possession of it by force if I try to take it back. The harm this second man does me is a hundred, perhaps a thousand times greater than what the first perhaps intended (I killed him before he actually did me any harm); and yet I could lawfully kill the one, while I cannot lawfully so much as hurt the other. The reason is plain. The one used force that threatened my life, and I could not have time to appeal to the law to protect it — and once life is gone, it is too late to appeal. The law could not restore life to my dead body; the loss would have been irreparable. To prevent that, the law of nature gave me a right to destroy the man who had put himself into a state of war with me and threatened my destruction. But in the other case my life was not in danger, so I could have the benefit of appealing to the law and recovering my hundred pounds that way.

### § 208

Fourthly: but suppose the magistrate's unlawful acts are maintained by the power he has acquired, and the remedy owed by law is obstructed by that same power. Even then, the right of resistance — even against such manifest acts of tyranny — will not suddenly, or on slight occasions, disturb the government. For if the abuse reaches no further than a few private men's cases, then although those men have a right to defend themselves and to recover by force what was taken from them by unlawful force, the right to do so will not easily draw them into a contest in which they are sure to perish. It is as impossible for one man, or a few oppressed men, to disturb the government where the body of the people does not feel itself concerned, as it is for a raving madman or a rash malcontent to overturn a well-settled state — the people being as little inclined to follow the one as the other.

### § 209

But suppose these illegal acts have extended to the majority of the people; or suppose the mischief and oppression has fallen only on a few, but in cases where the precedent and its consequences seem to threaten everyone, and the people are persuaded in their consciences that their laws — and with them their estates, liberties, and lives, and perhaps their religion too — are in danger. How they will then be kept from resisting illegal force used against them, I cannot say. This is an inconvenience, I confess, that attends all governments whatsoever, once the governors have brought things to the point of being generally suspected by their people. It is the most dangerous condition rulers can possibly put themselves in — and one in which they deserve the less pity, because it is so easy to avoid. For it is as impossible for a governor who really intends the good of his people, and the preservation of them and their laws together, not to make them see and feel it, as it is for the father of a family not to let his children see that he loves and takes care of them.

### § 210

But suppose the whole world observes pretences of one kind and actions of another: tricks used to evade the law; the trust of prerogative (which is an arbitrary power left in the prince's hands, in certain matters, to do good to the people, not harm) employed contrary to the end for which it was given; the people finding ministers and subordinate magistrates chosen to suit such designs, and favored or dismissed in proportion as they promote or oppose them; repeated experiments in arbitrary power; the religion readiest to introduce such power secretly favored (though publicly denounced), its agents supported as far as possible, and — where support is impossible — still approved and liked the better; and a long train of actions showing that all the councils of state tend in that direction. In such a case, how can a man keep himself from being persuaded in his own mind which way things are going, or from looking around for ways to save himself? He could no more do so than a passenger could keep from believing that the captain of his ship was carrying him and the rest of the company to Algiers, when he found the captain always steering that course — even though contrary winds, leaks in his ship, and shortages of men and provisions often forced him to change direction for a while, a course he steadily returned to as soon as the wind, the weather, and other circumstances allowed.
