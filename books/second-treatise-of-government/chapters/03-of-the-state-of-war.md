# Chapter III — Of the State of War

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 16–21
>
> [← Of the State of Nature](02-of-the-state-of-nature.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Slavery →](04-of-slavery.md)

## Overview

Having described the state of nature in Chapter II as a condition of freedom under the law of nature, Locke now defines its opposite: the state of war. A state of war exists whenever one person shows a settled, deliberate intention to destroy another or to bring him under absolute power. Because the fundamental law of nature commands that humanity be preserved, anyone who threatens another's life forfeits the protection of that law and may be destroyed like a dangerous predator — a wolf or a lion — since he has abandoned reason, the common rule among people, for force.

Locke's crucial extension of this idea is that an attempt to enslave counts the same as an attempt to kill. Whoever tries to get me into his absolute power without my consent must be presumed to intend everything else, including my life, because freedom is the fence that protects my preservation. This is why it is lawful to kill a thief who uses force against you, even if he has not threatened your life directly.

The chapter then carefully distinguishes the state of nature from the state of war — two conditions that other writers (Locke has Hobbes chiefly in view, though he does not name him) had run together. The state of nature is simply living together according to reason without a common earthly judge; the state of war is force, or a declared design of force, on another's person where there is no common authority to appeal to. War can therefore break out inside society (when force strikes too fast for law to help, or when courts are corrupted into instruments of violence) and it persists in the state of nature until the aggressor offers reparation and security.

Locke closes by drawing the chapter's political payoff: the desire to escape the state of war — where the only appeal is "to heaven," that is, to God through force — is one great reason people leave the state of nature and enter civil society, where an earthly authority can settle disputes. The example of Jephthah's appeal to God against the Ammonites illustrates what remains when no earthly judge exists. This argument sets up the following chapters on slavery and on the origins of political society.

## Key Concepts

- **State of war** — A condition of enmity and destruction created when someone declares, by word or action, a calm and settled design against another person's life or freedom. It is defined by intention and force, not by the mere absence of government.
- **State of nature vs. state of war** — Locke insists these are as different as peace is from violence. The state of nature is people living by reason without a common judge; the state of war is force without right upon a person, and it can occur either inside or outside civil society.
- **Enslavement as war** — Attempting to get another person into your absolute power is, in itself, a declaration of war against him, because whoever would take away freedom must be presumed to intend everything else, freedom being the foundation of all other security.
- **Forfeiture of the protection of reason** — An aggressor who abandons the common law of reason for force may be treated like a beast of prey and destroyed, because the safety of the innocent takes priority when not everyone can be preserved.
- **Absence of appeal** — What gives a person the right of war, even against a fellow citizen, is the lack of any effective appeal to a common judge — either because there is no judge (state of nature) or because force strikes before law can intervene, or because justice is openly perverted.
- **Appeal to heaven** — When no earthly remedy exists, the injured party's only recourse is to God — in practice, resistance by force, leaving the judgment of the cause to heaven, as Jephthah did (Judges 11:27).
- **Reason for entering society** — Avoiding the state of war, where every small dispute can end in violence for want of an umpire, is one great reason people quit the state of nature and set up a common earthly authority.

## Modern Translation

### § 16

The state of war is a state of hostility and destruction. So when someone declares, by word or action, a design on another person's life — not a passionate, hasty outburst, but a calm and settled intention — he puts himself into a state of war with the person against whom he has declared it. He thereby exposes his own life to be taken by that person, or by anyone who joins in that person's defense and takes up his cause. It is reasonable and just that I should have a right to destroy whatever threatens me with destruction. For by the fundamental law of nature, human beings are to be preserved as far as possible; and when everyone cannot be preserved, the safety of the innocent comes first. One may destroy a man who makes war on him, or who has revealed hostility to his very existence, for the same reason he may kill a wolf or a lion. Such men are not bound by the ties of the common law of reason; they have no rule but force and violence, and so they may be treated as beasts of prey — dangerous and destructive creatures that will certainly kill him whenever he falls into their power.

### § 17

It follows that whoever attempts to get another person into his absolute power puts himself, by that very attempt, into a state of war with him; the attempt must be understood as a declared design on his life. For I have reason to conclude that whoever would get me into his power without my consent would use me however he pleased once he had me there, and would destroy me too whenever the whim took him. Nobody can want to have me in his absolute power unless it is to compel me by force into something that violates my right to freedom — in other words, to make me a slave. Being free from such force is the only security for my preservation, and reason tells me to regard anyone who would take away that freedom — the fence that protects my preservation — as an enemy to my preservation itself. So whoever attempts to enslave me thereby puts himself into a state of war with me. Anyone who, in the state of nature, would take away the freedom that belongs to everyone in that state must necessarily be supposed to have a design to take away everything else as well, since that freedom is the foundation of all the rest. Likewise, anyone who, in the state of society, would take away the freedom belonging to the members of that society or commonwealth must be supposed to intend to take everything else from them too, and so must be regarded as being in a state of war with them.

### § 18

This is what makes it lawful for a man to kill a thief who has not hurt him in the slightest, nor declared any design on his life, beyond using force to get him into his power in order to take his money, or whatever else he pleases. Because he is using force, where he has no right, to get me into his power — whatever justification he claims — I have no reason to suppose that someone who would take away my liberty would not also, once he had me in his power, take away everything else. And so it is lawful for me to treat him as someone who has put himself into a state of war with me — that is, to kill him if I can. Whoever introduces a state of war and is the aggressor in it justly exposes himself to that risk.

### § 19

Here we have the plain difference between the state of nature and the state of war. Although some men have confused the two, they are as far apart as a state of peace, good will, mutual assistance, and preservation is from a state of enmity, malice, violence, and mutual destruction. People living together according to reason, without a common superior on earth with authority to judge between them — that, properly speaking, is the state of nature. But force, or a declared design of force, against another's person, where there is no common superior on earth to appeal to for relief — that is the state of war. And it is precisely the lack of such an appeal that gives a man the right of war even against an aggressor who lives in society and is a fellow subject. Thus I may not harm a thief who has stolen everything I own except by appealing to the law; yet I may kill him when he attacks me to rob me of nothing more than my horse or my coat. The reason is this: the law, which was made for my preservation, cannot step in to secure my life against present force — and life, once lost, admits of no repair. So the law permits me my own defense and grants me the right of war, a liberty to kill the aggressor, because the aggressor allows no time to appeal to our common judge, or to the law's decision, in a case where the harm may be beyond remedy. The lack of a common judge with authority puts all people in a state of nature; force without right against a person's body creates a state of war, whether there is a common judge or not.

### § 20

But when the actual use of force is over, the state of war ends between people who live in society and are equally subject, on both sides, to the fair determination of the law — because the remedy of appeal then lies open, both for the past injury and to prevent future harm. Where no such appeal exists, however — as in the state of nature, for lack of enacted laws and judges with authority to appeal to — the state of war, once begun, continues. The innocent party then has a right to destroy the other whenever he can, until the aggressor offers peace and seeks reconciliation on terms that will repair whatever wrongs he has already done and secure the innocent party against harm in the future. Indeed, even where an appeal to the law and to established judges lies open, but the remedy is denied through an obvious perversion of justice and a shameless twisting of the laws to protect or shield the violence or injuries of certain men or a certain party, it is hard to imagine anything there but a state of war. For wherever violence is used and injury is done — even by hands appointed to administer justice — it is still violence and injury, however it is dressed up with the name, pretenses, or forms of law. The purpose of law is to protect and give redress to the innocent by applying it impartially to everyone under it; wherever that is not done in good faith, war is made upon the sufferers, who, having no appeal on earth to set things right, are left to the only remedy available in such cases: an appeal to heaven.

### § 21

Avoiding this state of war — in which the only appeal is to heaven, and into which even the smallest disagreement is apt to end when there is no authority to decide between the contending parties — is one great reason why people put themselves into society and quit the state of nature. For where there is an authority, a power on earth from which relief can be obtained by appeal, the continuation of the state of war is excluded, and the controversy is decided by that power. Had there been any such court, any superior jurisdiction on earth, to determine the right between Jephthah and the Ammonites, they would never have come to a state of war; but instead, as we see, he was forced to appeal to heaven. "The Lord the Judge," he says, "be judge this day between the children of Israel and the children of Ammon" (Judges 11:27) — and then, pursuing and relying on his appeal, he leads out his army to battle. So in such controversies, when the question is asked, "Who shall be judge?", it cannot mean "Who shall decide the controversy?" Everyone knows what Jephthah tells us here: that "the Lord the Judge" shall judge. Where there is no judge on earth, the appeal lies to God in heaven. The question, then, cannot mean: who shall judge whether another person has put himself into a state of war with me, and whether I may, as Jephthah did, appeal to heaven over it? Of that, only I myself can be judge, in my own conscience, as I will answer for it on the great day to the supreme judge of all men.
