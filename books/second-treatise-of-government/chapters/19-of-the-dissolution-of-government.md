# Chapter XIX — Of the Dissolution of Government

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 211–243
>
> [← Of Tyranny](18-of-tyranny.md) · [Book index](../README.md)

## Overview

This is the treatise's climactic chapter, and its longest: here Locke draws together everything he has argued — the state of nature, consent, trust, property, the state of war — into an account of when a government ceases to exist and what a people may then do. He begins with a crucial distinction: the dissolution of the *society* (which normally happens only through foreign conquest) is not the same as the dissolution of the *government*. A society can outlive its government, and when the government dissolves, political power falls back to the people, who may set up a new one.

Locke then catalogs how governments dissolve from within. The first route is the alteration of the legislative — the "soul" of the commonwealth. Using a constitution that any English reader would recognize (a single hereditary executive, a hereditary house of nobles, and an elected assembly), he lists the ways a prince destroys the legislative: substituting his own will for law, preventing the assembly from meeting or acting freely, rigging the electors or the manner of elections, or handing the people over to a foreign power. The second route is breach of trust: when the legislative or the prince invades the property of the subjects or tries to seize arbitrary power over their lives, liberties, and estates, the power they held on trust is forfeited and devolves back to the people.

The rest of the chapter answers the obvious objection — that this doctrine is a recipe for perpetual rebellion. Locke's replies are among the most famous passages in political philosophy: peoples are naturally slow to revolt and endure "a long train of abuses" before stirring; the doctrine of forfeiture is actually the best *deterrent* against rebellion; and the true rebels (from *rebellare*, to bring back the state of war) are the rulers who use force against law, not the people who resist them. He skewers the doctrine of passive obedience with the image of Ulysses preaching quiet submission in the cyclops Polyphemus's den, and he turns the royalist writer William Barclay — "that great assertor of the power and sacredness of kings" — into a witness for his own side, showing that even Barclay concedes cases in which a king "un-kings" himself and may be resisted.

The chapter closes with the question that royalists thought unanswerable — "who shall be judge whether the prince or legislative act contrary to their trust?" Locke answers flatly: the people shall be judge, as any principal judges his own trustee; and where no earthly judge is possible, the appeal lies to heaven, as it did for Jephthah. The final section states the constitutional bottom line: as long as society lasts, the power individuals gave it never returns to them; but when the legislative's time expires or its trust is forfeited, power reverts to the society, and the people may place it in new hands or a new form, as they think good.

## Key Concepts

- **Dissolution of society vs. dissolution of government** — Two distinct events that must not be confused. Society dissolves almost only by foreign conquest, and then its government necessarily falls with it; but a government can dissolve from within while the society survives, in which case power reverts to the people, not to a lawless free-for-all.
- **Alteration of the legislative** — The legislative is the commonwealth's "soul," the depository of the society's one will. Whoever changes it without the people's authorization — a prince ruling by decree, blocking the assembly, corrupting elections, or surrendering the nation to a foreign power — has in effect destroyed the government itself.
- **Breach of trust and forfeiture** — All political power is held on trust for the people's good. When legislators or the prince invade the people's property or grasp at arbitrary power, they forfeit the power entrusted to them, and it "devolves to the people," who may erect a new legislative.
- **Rebellion redefined** — Rebellion (*rebellare*) means bringing back the state of war against law and authority. Since rulers with force in their hands are the likeliest to do this, it is oppressive rulers, not resisting peoples, who are "truly and properly rebels."
- **The right to prevent tyranny** — Men need not wait until their chains are on to act like freemen. Because a remedy that comes only after enslavement is no remedy, the people have a right not merely to escape tyranny but to prevent it.
- **The people as judge** — Against the question "who shall judge whether rulers have betrayed their trust?", Locke answers that the trustor judges the trustee: the people, who deputed their rulers, retain the power to judge and discard them.
- **Appeal to heaven** — Where no earthly judge exists or the ruler refuses arbitration, the contest becomes a state of war in which the injured party, like Jephthah, may appeal to God — that is, may lawfully resort to force, answering for it to the Supreme Judge.
- **Reversion of power** — While society lasts, the power individuals surrendered to it never returns to them singly. But when a legislative's set term expires or its power is forfeited by misconduct, supreme power reverts to the society as a body, which may keep it, reshape it, or lodge it in new hands.

## Modern Translation

### § 211

Anyone who wants to speak clearly about the dissolution of government must first distinguish between the dissolution of the *society* and the dissolution of the *government*. What makes a community — what brings men out of the loose state of nature into one political society — is the agreement each person makes with the rest to incorporate and act as one body, and so be one distinct commonwealth. The usual, and almost the only, way this union is dissolved is the invasion of a foreign force that conquers them. In that case, since they can no longer maintain and support themselves as one whole and independent body, the union that constituted that body must necessarily end, and everyone returns to the condition he was in before, free to shift for himself and provide for his own safety, as he thinks fit, in some other society. Whenever the society is dissolved, it is certain that the government of that society cannot survive. In this way conquerors' swords often cut up governments by the roots and mangle societies to pieces, cutting the subdued or scattered multitude off from the protection of, and dependence on, the society that ought to have preserved them from violence. The world knows this method of dissolving governments too well — and is too ready to practice it — for anything more to need saying about it. Nor does it take much argument to prove that where the society is dissolved, the government cannot remain: that is as impossible as for the frame of a house to stand when its materials have been scattered and dispersed by a whirlwind, or jumbled into a confused heap by an earthquake.

### § 212

Besides this overturning from outside, governments are dissolved from within.

First, when the legislative is altered. Civil society is a state of peace among its members: the state of war is excluded from it by the arbitration they have provided in their legislative for settling all disputes that may arise among them. It is in their legislative that the members of a commonwealth are united and combined into one coherent living body. The legislative is the soul that gives form, life, and unity to the commonwealth; from it the several members derive their mutual influence, sympathy, and connection. Therefore when the legislative is broken or dissolved, dissolution and death follow. For the essence and union of the society consist in having one will, and the legislative, once established by the majority, declares and, as it were, keeps that will. The constitution of the legislative is the first and fundamental act of society. It provides for the continuation of their union under the direction of persons, and within the bonds of laws, made by persons authorized to make them by the consent and appointment of the people — without which no one man, and no number of men, among them can have authority to make laws binding on the rest. When any person or persons take it upon themselves to make laws whom the people have not appointed to do so, they make laws without authority, and the people are therefore not bound to obey them. By this the people come once more to be out of subjection, and may constitute a new legislative for themselves, as they think best, being at full liberty to resist the force of those who, without authority, would impose anything on them. Everyone is left to the direction of his own will when those who, by the society's delegation, had the declaring of the public will are shut out of it, and others usurp their place who have no such authority or delegation.

### § 213

Since this is usually brought about by people within the commonwealth who misuse the power they hold, it is hard to assess it correctly, and to know at whose door to lay the blame, without knowing the form of government in which it happens. So let us suppose a legislative placed in the joint hands of three distinct persons:

1. A single hereditary person who holds the constant, supreme executive power, and with it the power of convening and dissolving the other two within certain periods of time.

2. An assembly of hereditary nobility.

3. An assembly of representatives chosen, for the time being, by the people.

Given such a form of government, it is evident that:

### § 214

First, when such a single person, or prince, sets up his own arbitrary will in place of the laws — which are the will of the society, declared by the legislative — then the legislative is changed. For the effective legislative is whatever body's rules and laws are actually put into execution and required to be obeyed; so when other laws are set up, and other rules are asserted and enforced, than those the legislative constituted by the society has enacted, it is plain the legislative has been changed. Whoever introduces new laws without being authorized to do so by the fundamental appointment of the society, or subverts the old ones, repudiates and overturns the power by which those laws were made, and so sets up a new legislative.

### § 215

Secondly, when the prince prevents the legislative from assembling at its proper time, or from acting freely for the purposes for which it was constituted, the legislative is altered. For the legislative does not consist in a certain number of men — no, nor even in their meeting — unless they also have freedom to debate and time to perfect what is for the good of the society. When these are taken away or altered so as to deprive the society of the proper exercise of the legislative's power, the legislative is truly altered. It is not names that constitute governments, but the use and exercise of the powers that were meant to accompany them. So whoever takes away the legislative's freedom, or hinders it from acting in its due seasons, in effect takes away the legislative itself and puts an end to the government.

### § 216

Thirdly, when the prince's arbitrary power alters the electors, or the methods of election, without the consent of the people and contrary to their common interest, there too the legislative is altered. For if the choosing is done by persons other than those the society has authorized, or in a way other than the society has prescribed, then those chosen are not the legislative appointed by the people.

### § 217

Fourthly, the delivery of the people into subjection to a foreign power, whether by the prince or by the legislative, is certainly a change of the legislative, and so a dissolution of the government. For the purpose for which people entered into society was to be preserved as one whole, free, independent society, governed by its own laws; and this is lost whenever they are given up into the power of another.

### § 218

Why, in a constitution like this, the dissolution of the government in these cases should be blamed on the prince is evident. He has the force, treasury, and offices of the state at his disposal; he often persuades himself, or is flattered by others into believing, that as supreme magistrate he cannot be checked. He alone is in a position to make great advances toward such changes under the pretense of lawful authority, and he has it in his power to intimidate or suppress those who oppose him, branding them factious, seditious, and enemies of the government. No other part of the legislative, and no part of the people, is capable of attempting any alteration of the legislative by itself without open and visible rebellion — easy enough to notice, and which, when it succeeds, produces effects little different from foreign conquest. Besides, since the prince in such a form of government has the power to dissolve the other parts of the legislative, and thereby reduce their members to private persons, they can never alter the legislative by law in opposition to him or without his concurrence, since his consent is required to give any of their decrees the force of law. Yet insofar as the other parts of the legislative contribute in any way to an attack on the government — whether by promoting such designs, or by failing to hinder them as far as they can — they are guilty, and share in what is certainly the greatest crime one man can commit against another.

### § 219

There is one more way in which such a government may be dissolved: when the holder of the supreme executive power neglects and abandons his charge, so that the laws already made can no longer be put into execution. This plainly reduces everything to anarchy, and so effectively dissolves the government. For laws are not made for their own sake, but to be, through their execution, the bonds of the society, keeping every part of the body politic in its proper place and function. When that ceases entirely, the government visibly ceases, and the people become a confused multitude without order or connection. Where there is no longer any administration of justice to secure men's rights, and no remaining power within the community to direct its force or provide for the public's needs, there is certainly no government left. Where the laws cannot be executed, it is the same as if there were no laws; and a government without laws is, I suppose, a mystery in politics — inconceivable to human understanding, and incompatible with human society.

### § 220

In these and similar cases, when the government is dissolved, the people are at liberty to provide for themselves by erecting a new legislative, differing from the old in its persons, or its form, or both, as they find most conducive to their safety and good. For the society can never, through someone else's fault, lose its native and original right to preserve itself — which can only be done through a settled legislative and a fair and impartial execution of the laws it makes. But mankind's condition is not so miserable that they are incapable of using this remedy until it is too late to look for any. To tell people they may provide for themselves by erecting a new legislative once oppression, trickery, or delivery to a foreign power has destroyed their old one is only to tell them they may expect relief when it is too late and the evil is past cure. It amounts to telling them first to be slaves, and then to look after their liberty; and, once their chains are on, telling them they may act like free men. If that is all, it is mockery rather than relief; and men can never be safe from tyranny if there is no way to escape it until they are completely under it. Therefore they have not only a right to get out of tyranny, but a right to prevent it.

### § 221

There is, therefore, a second way in which governments are dissolved: when the legislative, or the prince — either of them — acts contrary to their trust.

First, the legislative acts against the trust placed in it when it attempts to invade the property of the subject, and to make itself, or any part of the community, master and arbitrary disposer of the lives, liberties, or fortunes of the people.

### § 222

The reason men enter into society is the preservation of their property; and the purpose for which they choose and authorize a legislative is so that laws may be made, and rules set, as guards and fences around the property of all the members of the society, to limit the power and moderate the dominion of every part and member of it. It can never be supposed to be the will of the society that the legislative should have power to destroy the very thing everyone intends to secure by entering society — the thing for which the people submitted themselves to legislators of their own making. Whenever the legislators attempt to take away and destroy the property of the people, or to reduce them to slavery under arbitrary power, they put themselves into a state of war with the people, who are thereupon released from any further obedience, and are left to the common refuge God has provided for all men against force and violence. Whenever, therefore, the legislative transgresses this fundamental rule of society, and — through ambition, fear, folly, or corruption — tries to grasp for itself, or to put into anyone else's hands, an absolute power over the lives, liberties, and estates of the people, by this breach of trust it forfeits the power the people had put into its hands for quite contrary ends. That power devolves to the people, who have a right to resume their original liberty and, by establishing a new legislative (such as they think fit), to provide for their own safety and security — which is the purpose for which they are in society.

What I have said here about the legislative in general holds true also of the supreme executor. He has a double trust placed in him — both to have a part in the legislative and to carry out the supreme execution of the law — and he acts against both when he sets about making his own arbitrary will the law of the society. He also acts contrary to his trust when he uses the force, treasury, and offices of the society to corrupt the representatives and win them over to his purposes; or when he openly pre-commits the electors and dictates their choice, prescribing candidates whom he has won to his designs by solicitations, threats, promises, or other means, and uses them to install men who have promised in advance what they will vote for and what they will enact. To manage candidates and electors this way, and to remodel the methods of election — what is that but to cut up the government by the roots and poison the very fountain of public security? The people reserved to themselves the choice of their representatives as the fence around their property, and they could have done so for no other purpose than that those representatives should always be freely chosen, and, once chosen, should freely act and advise as the needs of the commonwealth and the public good should, upon examination and mature debate, be judged to require. Men who cast their votes before they have heard the debate and weighed the arguments on all sides are incapable of doing that. To prepare such a packed assembly, and to try to pass off the declared promoters of his own will as the true representatives of the people and the lawmakers of the society, is certainly as great a breach of trust, and as complete a declaration of a design to subvert the government, as can possibly be found. And if to this you add rewards and punishments visibly employed to the same end, and every device of perverted law used to remove and destroy all who stand in the way of such a design and will not comply and consent to betray the liberties of their country, there can be no doubt what is going on. What power such men ought to have in the society — men who use power contrary to the trust that accompanied it at its first institution — is easy to determine; and no one can fail to see that a man who has once attempted anything of this kind can never again be trusted.

### § 223

To this it will perhaps be said that since the people are ignorant and always discontented, to build the foundation of government on the unsteady opinion and uncertain mood of the people is to expose it to certain ruin; no government will be able to last long if the people may set up a new legislative whenever they take offense at the old one. To this I answer: quite the contrary. People are not so easily pried out of their old forms as some like to suggest. They can hardly be persuaded to amend even the acknowledged faults in the frame they have grown used to. And if there are any original defects, or accidental ones introduced by time or corruption, it is no easy thing to get them changed, even when all the world sees there is an opportunity for it. This slowness and reluctance in the people to abandon their old constitutions has, in the many revolutions seen in this kingdom, in this and former ages, still kept us to — or, after some interval of fruitless attempts, still brought us back to — our old legislative of king, lords, and commons. And whatever provocations have caused the crown to be taken from some of our princes' heads, they never carried the people so far as to place it in another line.

### § 224

But it will be said that this hypothesis lays a foundation for frequent rebellion. To this I answer:

First, no more than any other hypothesis. For when the people are made miserable, and find themselves exposed to the abuse of arbitrary power, then — cry up their governors as much as you please as sons of Jupiter; let them be sacred and divine, descended or authorized from heaven; give them out to be whoever or whatever you like — the same thing will happen. A people generally mistreated, and contrary to right, will be ready on any occasion to relieve themselves of a burden that sits heavy upon them. They will wish for, and seek, the opportunity — which, amid the change, weakness, and accidents of human affairs, seldom delays long in offering itself. A man must have lived only a little while in the world if he has not seen examples of this in his own time; and he must have read very little if he cannot produce examples of it in every kind of government the world has known.

### § 225

Secondly, I answer that such revolutions do not happen upon every little mismanagement of public affairs. Great mistakes on the part of rulers, many wrong and inconvenient laws, and all the slips of human frailty will be borne by the people without mutiny or murmur. But if a long train of abuses, prevarications, and tricks, all tending the same way, makes the design visible to the people — so that they cannot help feeling what they lie under and seeing where they are headed — it is no wonder they should then rouse themselves and try to put the rule into hands that can secure to them the ends for which government was first erected. Without those ends, ancient names and specious forms are so far from being better than the state of nature or pure anarchy that they are much worse: the inconveniences are all just as great and just as near, but the remedy is farther off and harder to reach.

### § 226

Thirdly, I answer that this doctrine — that the people have a power to provide anew for their safety, by a new legislative, when their legislators have acted contrary to their trust by invading their property — is the best fence against rebellion, and the likeliest means to prevent it. For rebellion is opposition not to persons but to *authority*, which is founded only in the constitutions and laws of the government. Those, whoever they may be, who by force break through those laws, and by force justify their violation of them, are truly and properly rebels. For when men, by entering into society and civil government, have excluded force and introduced laws for the preservation of property, peace, and unity among themselves, those who set up force again in opposition to the laws *rebellare* — that is, they bring back the state of war — and are properly rebels. And since those who are in power are the likeliest to do this (given the pretense they have to authority, the temptation of force in their hands, and the flattery of those around them), the most fitting way to prevent the evil is to show the danger and injustice of it to those who are under the greatest temptation to run into it.

### § 227

In both the cases mentioned above — when the legislative is changed, or when the legislators act contrary to the end for which they were constituted — those responsible are guilty of rebellion. For if anyone by force takes away the established legislative of any society, and the laws made by it pursuant to its trust, he thereby takes away the arbitration to which everyone had consented for the peaceable settlement of all their controversies, and which stood as a bar to the state of war among them. Those who remove or change the legislative take away this power of decision, which nobody can hold except by the appointment and consent of the people. In destroying the authority which the people set up, and which nobody else can set up, and in introducing a power the people have not authorized, they actually introduce a state of war — which is force without authority. By removing the legislative established by the society (in whose decisions the people acquiesced and were united as in their own will), they untie the knot and expose the people anew to the state of war. And if those who take away the legislative by force are rebels, the legislators themselves, as has been shown, deserve the name no less, when they — who were set up for the protection and preservation of the people, their liberties, and their properties — by force invade and try to take these away. Putting themselves thus into a state of war with those who made them the protectors and guardians of their peace, they are properly, and with the greatest aggravation, *rebellantes* — rebels.

### § 228

But suppose those who say this doctrine lays a foundation for rebellion mean that it may cause civil wars or internal strife to tell the people that they are released from obedience when illegal attempts are made on their liberties or properties, and that they may oppose the unlawful violence of those who were their magistrates when these invade their property contrary to the trust placed in them — and that therefore this doctrine is not to be permitted, being so destructive to the peace of the world. On the same grounds they may as well say that honest men may not oppose robbers or pirates, because that may cause disorder or bloodshed. If any harm comes in such cases, the blame falls not on the man who defends his own right, but on the man who invades his neighbor's. If the innocent, honest man must, for peace's sake, quietly surrender everything he has to whoever will lay violent hands on it, I ask people to consider what kind of peace there will be in the world — a peace consisting only of violence and plunder, maintained solely for the benefit of robbers and oppressors. Who would not think it an admirable peace between the mighty and the lowly when the lamb, without resistance, yielded its throat to be torn by the imperious wolf? Polyphemus's den gives us a perfect model of such a peace, and such a government: Ulysses and his companions had nothing to do there but quietly let themselves be devoured. And no doubt Ulysses, being a prudent man, preached passive obedience, and exhorted them to quiet submission, by pointing out how important peace was to mankind, and by showing them what inconveniences might follow if they should offer to resist Polyphemus, who now had power over them.

### § 229

The end of government is the good of mankind. And which is better for mankind: that the people should always be exposed to the boundless will of tyranny, or that rulers should sometimes be liable to be opposed when they grow extravagant in the use of their power and employ it for the destruction, and not the preservation, of the property of their people?

### § 230

Nor let anyone say that harm can arise from this doctrine whenever some busybody or turbulent spirit takes it into his head to desire the alteration of the government. It is true that such men may stir whenever they please — but it will be only to their own just ruin and destruction. For until the mischief has become general, and the wicked designs of the rulers have become visible, or their attempts palpable to the greater part of the people, the people — who are more disposed to suffer than to right themselves by resistance — are not inclined to stir. Isolated examples of injustice, or the oppression of an unfortunate man here and there, do not move them. But if they come to a general conviction, grounded on manifest evidence, that designs are being carried on against their liberties, and the general course and tendency of things cannot help but give them strong suspicions of the evil intentions of their governors — who is to be blamed for it? Who can help it, if those who could have avoided such suspicion bring it upon themselves? Are the people to be blamed for having the sense of rational creatures, and for being unable to think of things otherwise than as they find and feel them? And is it not rather the fault of those who put things into such a posture that they would rather not have them thought to be what they are? I grant that the pride, ambition, and turbulence of private men have sometimes caused great disorders in commonwealths, and that factions have been fatal to states and kingdoms. But whether the mischief has more often begun in the people's willfulness and a desire to cast off the lawful authority of their rulers, or in the rulers' insolence and their attempts to obtain and exercise arbitrary power over their people — whether oppression or disobedience first gave rise to the disorder — I leave to impartial history to determine. Of this I am sure: whoever, ruler or subject, sets about by force to invade the rights of either prince or people, and lays the foundation for overturning the constitution and frame of any just government, is guilty of the greatest crime, I think, of which a man is capable. He must answer for all the mischiefs of blood, plunder, and desolation that the breaking of governments to pieces brings upon a country; and whoever does it is justly to be regarded as the common enemy and pest of mankind, and is to be treated accordingly.

### § 231

That subjects or foreigners who attempt by force to seize the property of any people may be resisted with force is agreed on all hands. But that *magistrates* doing the same thing may be resisted has lately been denied — as if those who enjoyed the greatest privileges and advantages under the law thereby acquired a power to break the very laws that alone set them in a better position than their brethren. In fact their offense is the greater on that account: both because they are ungrateful for the larger share the law gives them, and because they break the trust their brethren have placed in their hands.

### § 232

Whoever uses force without right — as everyone does in society who uses it without law — puts himself into a state of war with those against whom he uses it. In that state all former ties are cancelled, all other rights cease, and everyone has a right to defend himself and to resist the aggressor. This is so evident that Barclay himself — that great champion of the power and sacredness of kings — is forced to admit that it is lawful for the people, in some cases, to resist their king; and this in the very chapter in which he claims to show that divine law forbids the people every kind of rebellion. It is evident, then, even by his own doctrine, that since the people may resist in some cases, not all resistance to princes is rebellion.

[Locke here quotes Barclay's Latin original — Barclay, *Contra Monarchomachos*, book 3, chapter 8 — and then supplies his own English translation, which follows as § 233.]

### § 233

Locke's English rendering of Barclay's words:

"But if someone should ask: must the people, then, always lay themselves open to the cruelty and rage of tyranny? Must they watch their cities pillaged and reduced to ashes, their wives and children exposed to the tyrant's lust and fury, and themselves and their families brought by their king to ruin and all the miseries of want and oppression — and still sit quiet? Must men alone be denied the common privilege of opposing force with force, which nature grants so freely to all other creatures for their preservation from injury? I answer: self-defense is part of the law of nature, and it cannot be denied to the community, even against the king himself. But to take *revenge* on him must by no means be allowed them, for that is not consistent with that law. Therefore, if the king shows hatred not merely toward certain particular persons, but sets himself against the body of the commonwealth of which he is the head, and with intolerable abuse cruelly tyrannizes over the whole people or a considerable part of them, in that case the people have a right to resist and defend themselves from injury. But it must be with this caution: they may only defend themselves; they must not attack their prince. They may repair the damages they have received, but they must not, under any provocation, exceed the bounds of due reverence and respect. They may repel the present attempt, but must not avenge past violence: for it is natural for us to defend life and limb, but for an inferior to punish a superior is against nature. The mischief that is intended against them the people may prevent before it is done; but once it is done, they must not avenge it on the king, even though he is the author of the villainy. This, then, is the privilege of the people in general, above what any private person has: particular men are allowed, even by our adversaries themselves (Buchanan alone excepted), no remedy but patience; but the body of the people may, with respect, resist intolerable tyranny — for when it is only moderate, they ought to endure it."

### § 234

So far, then, does that great advocate of monarchical power allow resistance.

### § 235

It is true he attaches two limitations to it — to no purpose:

First, he says, resistance must be *with reverence*.

Secondly, it must be *without retribution or punishment*; and the reason he gives is that an inferior cannot punish a superior.

First: how to resist force without striking back, or how to strike *with reverence*, will take some skill to make intelligible. A man who meets an assault with nothing but a shield to catch the blows, or in some still more respectful posture, without a sword in his hand to check the confidence and force of his attacker, will quickly reach the end of his resistance, and will find that such a defense serves only to bring worse treatment down on him. This is as ridiculous a way of resisting as Juvenal thought his a way of fighting: *ubi tu pulsas, ego vapulo tantum* — "where you do the beating, and I merely take the blows." And the outcome of the contest will inevitably be just as he describes it:

> *Libertas pauperis haec est:*
> *Pulsatus rogat, et pugnis concisus, adorat,*
> *Ut liceat paucis cum dentibus inde reverti.*
>
> "This is a poor man's liberty: beaten, he begs; pounded with fists, he pleads to be allowed to leave with a few teeth still in his head." (Juvenal)

This will always be the outcome of such an imaginary resistance, where men may not strike back. Whoever may resist, therefore, must be allowed to strike. And then let our author, or anyone else, deliver a knock on the head or a cut on the face with as much reverence and respect as he thinks fit. A man who can reconcile blows with reverence deserves, for all I know, to receive a civil, respectful cudgeling for his pains, wherever he can find one.

Secondly, as to his second limitation — that an inferior cannot punish a superior — that is true, generally speaking, *while he remains his superior*. But resisting force with force is the state of war, which levels the parties and cancels every former relation of reverence, respect, and superiority. The only advantage that then remains is on the side of the one who opposes the unjust aggressor: he has this superiority over him, that when he prevails he has a right to punish the offender, both for the breach of the peace and for all the evils that followed from it. Barclay, therefore, in another place, more consistently with himself, denies that it is lawful to resist a king *in any case* — but there he specifies two cases in which a king may *un-king himself*.

### § 236

[The second Latin passage from Barclay, giving the second case — Barclay, *Contra Monarchomachos*, book 3, chapter 16. Locke's English translation follows as § 238.]

### § 237

Locke's English rendering of Barclay continues:

"What then — can no case ever arise in which the people may rightfully, and by their own authority, help themselves, take up arms, and attack their king when he domineers over them imperiously? None at all, so long as he remains a king. 'Honor the king,' and 'he that resists the power resists the ordinance of God,' are divine oracles that will never permit it. The people, therefore, can never acquire a power over him unless he does something that makes him cease to be a king. For then he strips himself of his crown and dignity and returns to the condition of a private man, and the people become free and superior — the power they held in the interregnum, before they crowned him king, devolving to them again. But there are only a few kinds of misdeed that bring matters to this state. After considering the question well on every side, I can find only two. There are two cases, I say, in which a king, by the act itself (*ipso facto*), makes himself no king, and loses all power and royal authority over his people; and these are also noted by Winzerus.

"The first is if he attempts to overturn the government — that is, if he has a purpose and design to ruin the kingdom and commonwealth. So it is recorded of Nero that he resolved to cut off the senate and people of Rome, to lay the city waste with fire and sword, and then to move to some other place. And of Caligula, that he openly declared he would no longer be head to the people or the senate; that he had it in mind to cut off the worthiest men of both orders and then retire to Alexandria; and that he wished the people had but one neck, so that he might dispatch them all at a single blow. When any king harbors designs like these in his thoughts and seriously pursues them, he at once gives up all care and thought for the commonwealth, and consequently forfeits the power of governing his subjects — just as a master forfeits dominion over slaves he has abandoned."

### § 238

"The other case is when a king makes himself the dependent of another, and subjects his kingdom — which his ancestors left him, and which the people put free into his hands — to the dominion of another. For although it may perhaps not be his intention to harm the people, yet by doing this he has lost the principal part of royal dignity, namely, to be, next and immediately under God, supreme in his kingdom; and he has also betrayed or forced his people — whose liberty he ought to have carefully preserved — into the power and dominion of a foreign nation. By this alienation, as it were, of his kingdom, he himself loses the power he formerly had in it, without transferring the least right to those on whom he tried to bestow it; and so, by this act, he sets the people free and leaves them at their own disposal. One example of this is found in the Scottish Annals." (Barclay, *Contra Monarchomachos*, book 3, chapter 16.)

### § 239

In these cases Barclay, the great champion of absolute monarchy, is forced to allow that a king may be resisted, and that he ceases to be a king. In short — not to multiply cases — wherever he has no authority, there he is no king, and may be resisted; for wherever the authority ceases, the king ceases too, and becomes like other men who have no authority. And the two cases he instances differ little from those I mentioned above as destructive to governments — except that he has omitted the principle from which his doctrine flows: namely, the breach of trust, in failing to preserve the form of government agreed upon, and in failing to aim at the end of government itself, which is the public good and the preservation of property. When a king has dethroned himself and put himself into a state of war with his people, what is to prevent them from prosecuting a man who is no longer a king, just as they would any other man who has put himself into a state of war with them? Barclay, and those of his opinion, would do well to tell us. I would also like this further point from Barclay to be noticed: he says that *the mischief that is designed them, the people may prevent before it be done* — by which he allows resistance when tyranny is still only in design. "When any king harbors such designs in his thoughts and seriously promotes them," he says, "he immediately gives up all care and thought of the commonwealth" — so that, on his own account, neglect of the public good is to be taken as evidence of such a design, or at least as sufficient cause for resistance. And the reason for all of it he gives in these words: *because he betrayed or forced his people, whose liberty he ought carefully to have preserved.* What he adds — *into the power and dominion of a foreign nation* — signifies nothing: the fault and the forfeiture lie in the loss of their liberty, which he ought to have preserved, not in any distinction among the persons to whose dominion they were subjected. The people's right is equally invaded, and their liberty equally lost, whether they are made slaves to any of their own nation or to a foreign one; the injury lies in this, and against this alone they have the right of defense. And there are examples to be found in all countries showing that it is not a change in the nationality of their governors, but a change of *government*, that gives the offense.

Bilson, a bishop of our church, and a great advocate of the power and prerogative of princes, does, if I am not mistaken, acknowledge in his treatise on Christian subjection that princes may forfeit their power and their title to the obedience of their subjects. And if authority were needed in a case where reason is so plain, I could refer my reader to Bracton, Fortescue, the author of the *Mirrour*, and others — writers who cannot be suspected of ignorance of our government, or of enmity to it. But I thought Hooker alone might be enough to satisfy those men who, while relying on him for their ecclesiastical polity, are by some strange fate carried to deny the very principles on which he builds it. Whether in this they are being made the tools of cleverer craftsmen, to pull down their own building, they had best consider. This much I am sure of: their civil policy is so new, so dangerous, and so destructive to both rulers and people, that former ages could never bear the broaching of it; and it may be hoped that ages to come, redeemed from the impositions of these Egyptian under-taskmasters, will abhor the memory of such servile flatterers, who, while it seemed to serve their turn, resolved all government into absolute tyranny, and would have had all men born to what their own mean souls fitted them for — slavery.

### § 240

Here, most likely, the common question will be raised: *Who shall be judge* whether the prince or the legislative act contrary to their trust? Ill-disposed and factious men, it may be said, may spread this question among the people even when the prince is only making use of his rightful prerogative. To this I reply: *the people shall be judge.* For who should judge whether his trustee or deputy acts well, and in accordance with the trust placed in him, but the man who deputed him — and who must, by having deputed him, still retain the power to discard him when he fails in his trust? If this is reasonable in the private affairs of particular men, why should it be otherwise in a matter of the greatest moment, where the welfare of millions is at stake, and where the evil, if not prevented, is greater, and the redress very difficult, costly, and dangerous?

### § 241

But further: this question — *who shall be judge?* — cannot mean that there is no judge at all. For where there is no court on earth to decide controversies among men, God in heaven is judge. He alone, it is true, is judge of the *right*. But every man is judge for himself — in this case, as in all others — whether another has put himself into a state of war with him, and whether he should appeal to the Supreme Judge, as Jephthah did.

### § 242

If a controversy arises between a prince and some of the people in a matter where the law is silent or doubtful, and the thing is of great consequence, I should think the proper umpire in such a case would be the body of the people. For in matters where the prince holds a trust, and is exempted from the common, ordinary rules of the law, if any men find themselves aggrieved, and think the prince is acting contrary to that trust or beyond it, who is so fit to judge as the body of the people — who first lodged that trust in him — how far they meant it to extend? But if the prince, or whoever holds the administration, refuses that way of settling the matter, then the appeal lies nowhere but to heaven. Force between persons who have no known superior on earth, or force that allows no appeal to a judge on earth, is properly a state of war, in which the appeal lies only to heaven; and in that state the injured party must judge for himself when he thinks fit to make use of that appeal and commit himself to it.

### § 243

To conclude: the power that every individual gave the society when he entered into it can never revert to the individuals again as long as the society lasts, but will always remain in the community — because without this there can be no community, no commonwealth, which would be contrary to the original agreement. Likewise, when the society has placed the legislative in any assembly of men, to continue in them and their successors, with direction and authority for providing such successors, the legislative can never revert to the people while that government lasts; for having provided a legislative with power to continue forever, they have given up their political power to the legislative and cannot resume it. But if they have set limits to the duration of their legislative, and made this supreme power in any person or assembly only temporary — or if, by the misdeeds of those in authority, it is forfeited — then, upon that forfeiture, or at the expiration of the time set, the power reverts to the society, and the people have a right to act as supreme, and to continue the legislative in themselves, or to erect a new form, or, under the old form, to place it in new hands, as they think good.
