# Chapter VII — Of Political or Civil Society

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 77–94
>
> [← Of Paternal Power](06-of-paternal-power.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the Beginning of Political Societies →](08-of-the-beginning-of-political-societies.md)

## Overview

This chapter carries out a careful process of elimination. Locke surveys every kind of society human beings form naturally — husband and wife, parents and children, master and servant, even master and slave — and shows that none of them, alone or combined into a household, amounts to political society. Each of these relationships has its own limited purpose, and the power each confers is limited by that purpose: the marriage compact exists for procreation and mutual support, the servant's subjection extends no further than the wage contract, and even the head of a large family holds only a patchwork of narrow, differently bounded powers. A family may look like a little commonwealth in its structure, but it is nothing of the kind.

Having cleared away the false candidates, Locke gives his positive definition: political society exists only where every member has given up his natural power to judge and punish breaches of the law of nature, and resigned it into the hands of the community. The community then becomes a common umpire, acting through settled standing laws and authorized judges. This single test — is there a common, authoritative appeal on earth? — marks the boundary between civil society and the state of nature, and it generates the chapter's most famous and politically explosive conclusion: absolute monarchy is not a form of civil government at all. Since the absolute prince is judge in his own case and his subjects have no appeal against him, prince and subject remain in the state of nature with respect to each other — and the subject is actually worse off than in the ordinary state of nature, because he faces a man armed with power and corrupted by flattery, yet is denied even the liberty to defend his own rights.

Locke closes with a piece of conjectural history: early peoples may have trusted rule to one good man out of deference to his virtue, but when worse successors made their property insecure, people could never think themselves in civil society until lawmaking was placed in collective bodies, so that every person — ruler included — became equally subject to the laws. The chapter thus sets up the argument of the next: how political societies actually begin, namely by consent.

## Key Concepts

- **Conjugal society** — The voluntary compact between man and woman. Its chief end is procreation, but it also involves mutual support and a communion of interests for the sake of the children. Because its power is limited by its ends, it gives the husband nothing like absolute sovereignty, and it need not even be lifelong in principle.
- **Master and servant vs. master and slave** — A servant is a free man who sells his service for a time in exchange for wages; the master's power over him is temporary and defined by the contract. A slave (a captive taken in a just war) is under absolute, arbitrary dominion, is incapable of property, and therefore cannot be part of civil society at all.
- **The family is not a commonwealth** — Though a household may resemble a little commonwealth in its order and offices, the paterfamilias holds only distinct, limited powers over each member, differing in duration and extent; he has no legislative power of life and death, so his rule cannot constitute political society.
- **Property (in the wide sense)** — In this chapter Locke uses "property" to mean a man's "life, liberty and estate" together. The preservation of property in this broad sense is the chief end of civil society.
- **Political (civil) society defined** — Political society exists where, and only where, every member has quit his natural power to judge and punish violations of the law of nature and resigned it to the community, which becomes umpire through standing laws and authorized judges. Whoever lacks such a common appeal on earth remains in the state of nature.
- **Legislative and executive power** — From the members' surrendered powers arise the two great powers of a commonwealth: to judge by standing laws how domestic offences shall be punished (legislative), and to judge by occasional judgments how injuries from outside shall be redressed (the power of war and peace), employing the united force of all the members for both.
- **Absolute monarchy inconsistent with civil society** — Because the absolute prince unites all legislative and executive power in himself, his subjects have no appeal against him; he therefore remains in the state of nature with respect to them, and absolute monarchy is no form of civil government at all.

## Modern Translation

### § 77

God made man a creature who, in God's own judgment, ought not to be alone. He therefore put man under strong pressures of necessity, convenience, and inclination that drive him into society, and equipped him with understanding and language so that he could sustain and enjoy it. The first society was between man and wife; that gave rise to the society between parents and children; and in time the society between master and servant was added to these. All of these might, and commonly did, come together to make up a single family, in which the master or mistress had some sort of authority appropriate to a family. Yet each of these relationships, and even all of them together, falls short of political society — as we shall see if we consider the different ends, ties, and limits of each.

### § 78

Conjugal society is created by a voluntary compact between a man and a woman. Although it consists chiefly in that communion and right in one another's bodies which is necessary to its chief end, procreation, it also carries with it mutual support and assistance, and a communion of interests. These are necessary not only to unite the couple's care and affection, but also for the sake of their common offspring, who have a right to be nourished and maintained by them until they can provide for themselves.

### § 79

The purpose of the union between male and female is not merely procreation but the continuation of the species. So this union ought to last, even after procreation, as long as is needed for the nourishment and support of the young, who must be sustained by those who produced them until they can manage and provide for themselves. This rule, which the infinitely wise Maker has set for the works of his hands, we find the lower creatures steadily obeying. Among grass-eating animals that bear live young, the union of male and female lasts no longer than the act of copulation itself, because the mother's teat is enough to nourish the young until it can feed on grass; the male merely begets, and takes no further interest in the female or the young, to whose sustenance he can contribute nothing. But among beasts of prey the union lasts longer, because the mother cannot both subsist herself and nourish her numerous offspring by her own hunting alone — a more laborious and more dangerous way of living than grazing — so the male's assistance is necessary to maintain their common family, which cannot survive until the young can hunt for themselves except through the joint care of male and female. The same thing can be observed in all birds (except some domestic ones, where abundant food excuses the cock from feeding and caring for the brood): since the young need food in the nest, the cock and hen remain mates until the young can use their wings and provide for themselves.

### § 80

And here, I think, lies the chief — perhaps the only — reason why male and female in mankind are bound to a longer union than other creatures: the female is capable of conceiving again, and in fact commonly is pregnant again and gives birth to a new child, long before the previous one has outgrown its dependence on its parents' help, can fend for itself, and has received all the assistance owed to it by its parents. So the father, who is bound to care for those he has fathered, is obliged to remain in conjugal society with the same woman longer than other creatures are, whose young can subsist on their own before the season of procreation comes around again — at which point the conjugal bond dissolves of itself and they are free, until Hymen, at his usual anniversary season, summons them to choose new mates. In this one cannot help admiring the wisdom of the great Creator. Having given man foresight and the ability to store up for the future as well as to supply the present need, he made it necessary that the society of man and wife should be more lasting than that of male and female among other creatures — so that their industry might be encouraged and their interests better united, to make provision and lay up goods for their common children. Casual pairing, or easy and frequent dissolutions of conjugal society, would greatly disrupt all this.

### § 81

These are ties on mankind that make the conjugal bond firmer and more lasting in humans than in other species of animals. Even so, one might reasonably ask why this compact — once procreation and education are secured and inheritance provided for — should not be terminable, either by consent, or at a set time, or on set conditions, just like any other voluntary compact. There is nothing in the nature of the thing, nor in its purposes, that requires it always to be for life. (I mean, of course, for people who are under no constraint from any positive law that declares all such contracts perpetual.)

### § 82

Husband and wife have only one common concern, yet since they have different understandings, they will inevitably sometimes have different wills as well. It is therefore necessary that the final decision — that is, the rule — be lodged somewhere; and it naturally falls to the man's share, as the abler and the stronger. But this extends only to the things of their common interest and property. It leaves the wife in full and free possession of whatever is her exclusive right by contract, and it gives the husband no more power over her life than she has over his. Indeed, the husband's power is so far from that of an absolute monarch that the wife in many cases has the liberty to separate from him, where natural right or their contract allows it — whether that contract was made by the two of them in the state of nature, or by the customs or laws of the country they live in. On such a separation the children go to the father or to the mother, as the contract determines.

### § 83

All the ends of marriage can be achieved under civil government just as well as in the state of nature. The civil magistrate therefore does not curtail the right or power of either spouse that is naturally necessary to those ends — namely procreation, and mutual support and assistance while they are together — but only settles any dispute that may arise between man and wife about them. If it were otherwise — if absolute sovereignty and the power of life and death naturally belonged to the husband, and were necessary to the society of man and wife — then there could be no marriage in any of those countries where the husband is allowed no such absolute authority. But the ends of matrimony require no such power in the husband, and the nature of conjugal society does not confer it on him, since it is not at all necessary to that state. Conjugal society could subsist and achieve its ends without it. Indeed, community of goods and the power over them, mutual assistance and maintenance, and the other things belonging to conjugal society could all be varied and regulated by the contract that unites man and wife, so long as this is consistent with procreation and with bringing up the children until they can fend for themselves. For nothing is necessary to any society except what is necessary to the ends for which it was made.

### § 84

The society between parents and children, and the distinct rights and powers belonging to each, I treated so fully in the previous chapter that I need say nothing about it here. And I think it is plain that it is very different from a political society.

### § 85

"Master" and "servant" are names as old as history, but they are given to people in very different conditions. A free man makes himself a servant to another by selling him, for a fixed time, the service he undertakes to perform, in exchange for wages he is to receive. Though this ordinarily brings him into his master's household and under its ordinary discipline, it gives the master only a temporary power over him, and no greater power than the contract between them contains. But there is another sort of servant, whom we call by the special name of slaves. These, being captives taken in a just war, are by the right of nature subjected to the absolute dominion and arbitrary power of their masters. Such men, having (as I have said) forfeited their lives, and with them their liberties, and having lost their estates — being in the state of slavery, incapable of any property — cannot in that state be considered any part of civil society, whose chief end is the preservation of property.

### § 86

Let us then consider a master of a family with all these subordinate relations — wife, children, servants, and slaves — united under the domestic rule of a family. Whatever resemblance this may bear to a little commonwealth in its order, its offices, and even its numbers, it is very far from being one, in its constitution, its power, and its end. Or, if it must be called a monarchy, with the paterfamilias as its absolute monarch, then absolute monarchy will have a very fragmented and short-lived power. For it is plain from what has been said that the master of the family has a very distinct and differently limited power, in both duration and extent, over the several persons in it. Setting aside the slave — and the family is just as much a family, and his power as paterfamilias just as great, whether there are any slaves in it or not — he has no legislative power of life and death over any of them, and no power that a mistress of a family might not have as well as he. And he certainly cannot have absolute power over the whole family when he has only a very limited power over every individual in it. But how a family, or any other society of men, differs from what is properly political society, we shall best see by considering what political society itself consists in.

### § 87

Man is born, as has been proved, with a title to perfect freedom and an unrestricted enjoyment of all the rights and privileges of the law of nature, equally with any other man or number of men in the world. He therefore has by nature a power not only to preserve his property — that is, his life, liberty, and estate — against the injuries and attacks of other men, but also to judge and punish breaches of that law by others, as he is convinced the offence deserves, even with death itself, in crimes whose heinousness, in his opinion, requires it. Now, no political society can exist or survive without having in itself the power to preserve property, and, to that end, to punish the offences of all its members. Therefore political society exists there, and only there, where every one of the members has given up this natural power and resigned it into the hands of the community, in all cases that do not bar him from appealing to the law it has established for protection. All private judgment of every particular member being thus excluded, the community becomes the umpire — acting by settled standing rules, impartial and the same for all parties, and through men given authority by the community to execute those rules. It decides all the disputes that may arise between any members of that society about any matter of right, and punishes the offences any member commits against the society, with the penalties the law has established. By this it is easy to tell who are, and who are not, in political society together. Those who are united into one body and have a common established law and judicature to appeal to, with authority to decide disputes between them and punish offenders, are in civil society with one another. But those who have no such common appeal — I mean on earth — are still in the state of nature, each man being, where there is no one else, judge for himself and executioner; which is, as I have shown before, the perfect state of nature.

### § 88

In this way the commonwealth acquires the power to lay down what punishment shall attach to the various transgressions, committed among the members of the society, that its members think deserve it — which is the power of making laws. It likewise has the power to punish any injury done to any of its members by anyone who is not a member — which is the power of war and peace. And all of this is for the preservation of the property of all the members of that society, as far as possible. Every man who has entered civil society and become a member of a commonwealth has thereby given up his power to punish offences against the law of nature according to his own private judgment. But along with the judging of offences, which he has surrendered to the legislative in all cases where he can appeal to the magistrate, he has also given the commonwealth a right to employ his force in executing the commonwealth's judgments whenever he is called on to do so. And these judgments are really his own, being made by himself or by his representative. Here we have the origin of the legislative and executive power of civil society: to judge by standing laws how far offences committed within the commonwealth are to be punished; and also to determine, by occasional judgments based on the present circumstances of the case, how far injuries from outside are to be redressed; and in both to employ all the force of all the members whenever there is need.

### § 89

Wherever, therefore, any number of men are so united into one society that each gives up his executive power of the law of nature and resigns it to the public, there — and only there — is a political, or civil, society. This happens whenever any number of men in the state of nature enter into society to make one people, one body politic, under one supreme government; or when anyone joins himself to, and incorporates himself with, a government already established. For by doing so he authorizes the society — or, what comes to the same thing, its legislative — to make laws for him as the public good of the society requires, and he owes his own assistance in executing them, as he would to his own decrees. This is what takes men out of a state of nature and into a commonwealth: setting up a judge on earth with authority to decide all controversies and redress the injuries that may befall any member of the commonwealth — that judge being the legislative, or the magistrates it appoints. And wherever there are any number of men, however associated, who have no such decisive power to appeal to, they are still in the state of nature.

### § 90

From this it is evident that absolute monarchy — which some men count the only government in the world — is in fact inconsistent with civil society, and so can be no form of civil government at all. The end of civil society is to avoid and remedy those inconveniences of the state of nature which necessarily follow from every man's being judge in his own case, by setting up a known authority to which everyone in the society may appeal upon any injury received or any controversy that arises, and which everyone in the society ought to obey.* Wherever there are persons who have no such authority to appeal to for the decision of any difference between them, those persons are still in the state of nature. And so is every absolute prince, with respect to those who are under his dominion.

> \* "The public power of all society is above every soul contained in that same society; and the principal use of that power is to give laws to all who are under it — laws which in such cases we must obey, unless a reason can be shown that necessarily proves the law of reason, or of God, commands the contrary." — Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 16.

### § 91

For since the absolute prince is supposed to hold all power, both legislative and executive, in himself alone, there is no judge to be found, and no appeal lies open to anyone, who could fairly, impartially, and with authority decide a case, and from whose decision relief and redress might be expected for any injury or harm suffered at the prince's hands or by his order. So such a man — whatever he is titled, Czar, or Grand Seignior, or whatever you please — is as much in the state of nature with everyone under his dominion as he is with the rest of mankind. For wherever any two men are who have no standing rule and no common judge on earth to appeal to for the settlement of disputes of right between them, they are still in the state of nature,* and subject to all its inconveniences — with only this dismal difference for the subject, or rather slave, of an absolute prince: in the ordinary state of nature a man has the liberty to judge of his own right and, as best he can, to maintain it. But now, whenever his property is invaded by the will and order of his monarch, he not only has no appeal, as those in society ought to have; he is, as if degraded from the common rank of rational creatures, denied even the liberty to judge of his right or to defend it. And so he is exposed to all the misery and inconveniences a man can fear from someone who, being in the unrestrained state of nature, is nevertheless corrupted with flattery and armed with power.

> \* "To take away all such mutual grievances, injuries and wrongs" — that is, those that attend men in the state of nature — "there was no way but only by growing into composition and agreement among themselves: by ordaining some kind of public government, and by yielding themselves subject to it, so that those to whom they granted authority to rule and govern might procure the peace, tranquillity, and happy condition of the rest. Men always knew that where force and injury was offered, they might defend themselves; they knew that however much men may seek their own advantage, if this were done with injury to others it was not to be tolerated, but was to be resisted by all men and all good means. Finally, they knew that no man might reasonably take it upon himself to determine his own right, and proceed to maintain it according to his own determination, since every man is partial toward himself and toward those he greatly favors; and therefore that strife and troubles would be endless, unless they gave their common consent to be ordered, all of them, by some persons on whom they should agree — without which consent there would be no reason for one man to take upon himself to be lord or judge over another." — Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 10.

### § 92

Anyone who thinks that absolute power purifies men's blood and corrects the baseness of human nature need only read the history of this age, or any other, to be convinced of the contrary. A man who would have been insolent and injurious in the woods of America would probably not be much better on a throne — where, perhaps, learning and religion will be found to justify everything he does to his subjects, and the sword will promptly silence anyone who dares to question it. As for what the protection of absolute monarchy amounts to, what kind of fathers of their countries it makes princes, and to what degree of happiness and security it carries civil society where this sort of government has grown to perfection — whoever cares to look into the recent account of Ceylon may easily see.

### § 93

In absolute monarchies, it is true, as in the other governments of the world, the subjects do have an appeal to the law, and judges to decide any controversies and restrain any violence that may occur among the subjects themselves, one with another. Everyone thinks this necessary, and believes that anyone who tried to abolish it would deserve to be considered a declared enemy of society and of mankind. But whether this arrangement comes from a true love of mankind and society, and from the charity we owe one another, there is reason to doubt. For it is no more than what any man who loves his own power, profit, or greatness may — and naturally must — do: keep those animals from hurting or destroying one another who labor and drudge only for his pleasure and advantage, and who are looked after not out of any love the master has for them, but out of love of himself and of the profit they bring him. For if it is asked what security, what fence, there is in such a state against the violence and oppression of this absolute ruler, the very question can scarcely be tolerated. They are ready to tell you that merely to ask after safety deserves death. Between subject and subject, they will grant, there must be measures, laws, and judges, for their mutual peace and security. But as for the ruler, he ought to be absolute, and is above all such considerations; because he has the power to do more hurt and wrong, it is right when he does it. To ask how you may be protected from harm or injury on the very side where the strongest hand stands ready to inflict it is instantly denounced as the voice of faction and rebellion. As if, when men quit the state of nature and entered into society, they agreed that all of them but one should be under the restraint of laws, while that one should retain all the liberty of the state of nature, enlarged with power and made licentious by impunity. This is to think men so foolish that they take care to avoid whatever mischief polecats or foxes might do them, but are content — indeed, think it safety — to be devoured by lions.

### § 94

But whatever flatterers may say to beguile people's understandings, it does not stop men from feeling. And when they perceive that some man, in whatever station, is outside the bounds of the civil society they belong to, and that they have no appeal on earth against any harm they may receive from him, they are apt to consider themselves in the state of nature with respect to him — and to take care, as soon as they can, to secure for themselves that safety and security in civil society for which it was first instituted, and for which alone they entered into it. At first, perhaps (as will be shown more fully later in this discourse), some one good and excellent man, having gained a preeminence among the rest, had such deference paid to his goodness and virtue — as to a kind of natural authority — that the chief rule, along with the arbitration of their disputes, passed by tacit consent into his hands, with no other safeguard than the assurance they had of his uprightness and wisdom. Yet when time — which lends authority, and (as some men would persuade us) sacredness, to customs begun by the careless and unforeseeing innocence of the earliest ages — had brought in successors of another stamp, the people, finding their properties insecure under government as it then stood (whereas government has no other end but the preservation of property*), could never be safe or at rest, nor think themselves in civil society, until the legislature was placed in collective bodies of men — call them senate, parliament, or what you please. By this means every single person, equally with the meanest of men, became subject to those laws which he himself, as part of the legislative, had established. Nor could anyone, by his own authority, evade the force of the law once made, or plead exemption by any pretence of superiority, so as to license his own misdeeds or those of any of his dependents.** No man in civil society can be exempted from its laws. For if any man may do whatever he thinks fit, and there is no appeal on earth for redress or security against any harm he may do — I ask whether he is not still completely in the state of nature, and so can be no part or member of that civil society? Unless someone will say that the state of nature and civil society are one and the same thing — and I have never yet found anyone so great a patron of anarchy as to affirm that.

> \* "At first, when some certain kind of regime had once been appointed, it may be that nothing further was thought about the manner of governing, but everything was left to the wisdom and discretion of those who were to rule — until by experience men found this in every respect highly inconvenient, so that the thing they had devised as a remedy did in fact only increase the sore it should have cured. They saw that to live by one man's will became the cause of all men's misery. This forced them to come to laws, in which all men might see their duty beforehand and know the penalties for transgressing them." — Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 10.

> \*\* "Civil law, being the act of the whole body politic, therefore overrules each several part of that same body." — Hooker, ibid.
