# Chapter XVII — Of Usurpation

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 197–198
>
> [← Of Conquest](16-of-conquest.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Tyranny →](18-of-tyranny.md)

## Overview

This is the shortest chapter in the treatise, a hinge between the long chapter on conquest that precedes it and the chapter on tyranny that follows. Locke defines usurpation by analogy: conquest is usurpation from outside, and usurpation is conquest from within. The crucial point of the definition is that a usurper, by the very meaning of the word, can never have right on his side — usurpation just *is* taking possession of a power that rightfully belongs to someone else. Locke also draws a careful boundary: usurpation strictly speaking changes only *who* rules, not the form or rules of the government. If the intruder goes further and stretches the power beyond what the lawful rulers were entitled to, that is a second and distinct wrong — tyranny piled on top of usurpation — which sets up the next chapter.

Section 198 supplies the argument for why the usurper has no claim to obedience. In any lawful government, the method of designating who is to rule is as essential a part of the constitution as the form of government itself, and like the form, it was originally established by the people. A society that agreed to be a monarchy but fixed no way of identifying the monarch would be as good as anarchic. So every commonwealth has settled rules for appointing officeholders and for transmitting authority to them. Anyone who comes into power by a route other than the one the community's laws prescribe is simply not the person the laws appoint — and therefore not the person the people have consented to, even if every institution of the government is left standing.

The chapter closes by applying Locke's master principle, consent, to the usurper's future: neither he nor anyone who derives a claim through him can ever acquire a genuine title until the people are genuinely free to consent and have actually consented to confirm the power in him. The argument thus keeps the people's consent, not mere possession or the passage of time, as the only source of legitimate political authority — a direct rebuke to any theory that success in seizing power eventually ripens into right.

## Key Concepts

- **Usurpation** — Seizing possession of a power that another person has the right to; a "domestic conquest." By definition it can never be rightful, since if the seizure were rightful it would not be usurpation at all.
- **Usurpation vs. tyranny** — Usurpation proper changes only the *person* who holds power, leaving the form and rules of government intact. Extending the power beyond what the lawful rulers were entitled to is a further wrong: tyranny added to usurpation.
- **Designation of rulers** — The settled method by which a community identifies who is to hold power. Locke argues it is as natural and necessary a part of a constitution as the form of government itself, and equally owes its establishment to the people.
- **Consent as the source of title** — A person who takes power outside the legally prescribed route is not the person the people consented to and has no right to be obeyed, even if the constitution's forms survive. Only the people's free and actual consent can ever convert usurped power into a rightful title.

## Modern Translation

### § 197

Just as conquest can be called a foreign usurpation, usurpation is a kind of domestic conquest — with this difference: a usurper can never have right on his side, because it only counts as usurpation when someone takes possession of what another person has a right to. Usurpation, insofar as it is only usurpation, is a change merely of persons, not of the forms and rules of the government. For if the usurper extends his power beyond what rightfully belonged to the lawful princes or governors of the commonwealth, that is tyranny added to usurpation.

### § 198

In every lawful government, the designation of the persons who are to rule is as natural and necessary a part as the form of the government itself, and it, too, was originally established by the people. It would be virtually the same anarchy to have no form of government at all as to agree that the government shall be monarchical but appoint no way to know and identify the person who is to hold the power and be the monarch. That is why all commonwealths, along with their established form of government, also have rules for appointing those who are to have any share in the public authority, and settled methods of conveying the right to them.

Whoever comes into the exercise of any part of that power by ways other than those the laws of the community prescribe has no right to be obeyed, even if the form of the commonwealth is still preserved — since he is not the person the laws have appointed, and consequently not the person the people have consented to. Nor can such a usurper, or anyone deriving a claim from him, ever have a title until the people are both at liberty to consent and have actually consented to allow and confirm in him the power that until then he has merely usurped.
