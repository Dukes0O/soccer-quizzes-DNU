# Chapter XII — Of the Legislative, Executive, and Federative Power of the Commonwealth

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 143–148
>
> [← Of the Extent of the Legislative Power](11-of-the-extent-of-the-legislative-power.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the Subordination of the Powers of the Commonwealth →](13-of-the-subordination-of-the-powers.md)

## Overview

This short chapter is Locke's classic statement of the separation of powers. Having established in Chapter XI what the legislative power may and may not do, Locke now distinguishes the different powers a commonwealth exercises and explains why they tend to fall into different hands. His argument is not a formal constitutional blueprint but a practical one, grounded in his view of human nature: people who make laws should not also execute them, because the temptation to exempt themselves from their own laws — and to bend both the making and the enforcing of law to private advantage — is too great for human frailty.

The argument moves in two steps. First (§§ 143–144), Locke separates legislative from executive power on functional grounds: laws can be made quickly but must be enforced continuously, so the legislature need not sit permanently while the executive must always be "in being." Second (§§ 145–148), he identifies a third power he names "federative" — the power over war, peace, leagues, alliances, and all dealings with people and communities outside the commonwealth. This power is "natural," because toward the rest of the world the commonwealth remains a single body in the state of nature, just as each individual was before joining society.

Locke's treatment of the federative power contains a notable concession: unlike domestic execution of law, foreign affairs cannot be governed by standing, antecedent laws, because it depends on the shifting designs and interests of foreigners. It must therefore be left largely to the prudence and skill of those who hold it — an early acknowledgment of executive discretion that prepares the ground for his doctrine of prerogative in Chapter XIV. He closes by noting that while executive and federative power are conceptually distinct, they are almost always united in the same hands, since both command the force of the society, and dividing that force under separate, independent commands would invite disorder and ruin.

## Key Concepts

- **Legislative power** — The right to direct how the force of the commonwealth shall be used to preserve the community and its members. Because durable laws can be made quickly, this power need not be permanently in session, and in well-ordered commonwealths it is placed in an assembly whose members, once they disperse, live under the laws they made.
- **Executive power** — The power that must always exist to see that the laws, once made, are continuously enforced. Its permanence, contrasted with the intermittent work of lawmaking, is one reason legislative and executive power come to be separated.
- **Federative power** — Locke's name (he says the label doesn't matter) for the power of war and peace, leagues and alliances, and all transactions with persons and communities outside the commonwealth. It is "natural" because it corresponds to the power each person had in the state of nature before entering society.
- **The commonwealth as one body in the state of nature** — Internally, members of a society are distinct persons governed by its laws; externally, toward the rest of mankind, they form a single body that remains in the state of nature with respect to all other states and persons. This is why injuries to any member engage the whole public.
- **Separation of powers and human frailty** — Locke's core reason for separating lawmaking from law-enforcing: those who both make and execute laws will be tempted to exempt themselves from obedience and shape the law to their private advantage, acquiring an interest distinct from the community's, contrary to the ends of society and government.
- **Discretion in foreign affairs** — The federative power, unlike the executive, cannot be much directed by prior standing laws; because it depends on the variable actions and designs of foreigners, it must be entrusted to the prudence and wisdom of those who hold it, to be managed for the public good.
- **Union of executive and federative power** — Though really distinct, these two powers are almost impossible to place in different, independent hands, since both require the force of the society; putting the public force under different commands would sooner or later cause disorder and ruin.

## Modern Translation

### § 143

The legislative power is the power that has the right to direct how the force of the commonwealth shall be used to preserve the community and its members. But the laws that are to be constantly enforced, and whose force is to continue indefinitely, can be made in a short time; so there is no need for the legislature to be permanently in session, since it does not always have work to do. And there is another consideration. It may be too great a temptation for human frailty — which is prone to grasp at power — if the same persons who have the power to make laws also hold the power to execute them. They could then exempt themselves from obedience to the laws they make, and tailor the law, both in its making and in its enforcement, to their own private advantage. In that way they would come to have an interest distinct from the rest of the community, contrary to the purpose of society and government. Therefore, in well-ordered commonwealths, where the good of the whole is given the consideration it deserves, the legislative power is placed in the hands of a number of different persons who, duly assembled, have — by themselves or jointly with others — the power to make laws. Once they have done so and have dispersed again, they are themselves subject to the laws they have made. This is a fresh and immediate bond on them to take care that the laws they make are for the public good.

### § 144

But the laws that are made at once, in a short time, have a constant and lasting force, and they need perpetual execution — someone continually attending to them. So there must be a power always in existence to see to the enforcement of the laws that have been made and remain in force. And this is how the legislative and executive powers often come to be separated.

### § 145

There is another power in every commonwealth which one might call natural, because it corresponds to the power every man naturally had before he entered society. For although within a commonwealth its members remain distinct persons in relation to one another, and as such are governed by the laws of the society, in relation to the rest of mankind they make up one body — a body which, like every one of its members before, is still in the state of nature with respect to the rest of mankind. This is why disputes that arise between any man of the society and those outside it are handled by the public, and why an injury done to one member of the body commits the whole community to obtaining redress for it. Seen in this light, the whole community is a single body in the state of nature with respect to all other states or persons outside it.

### § 146

This power, then, covers war and peace, leagues and alliances, and all transactions with all persons and communities outside the commonwealth. It may be called "federative," if anyone likes. As long as the thing itself is understood, I am indifferent about the name.

### § 147

These two powers, executive and federative, are genuinely distinct in themselves. One covers the execution of the society's domestic laws within itself, upon everyone who is part of it; the other covers the management of the public's security and interest abroad, with all those from whom it may receive benefit or harm. Yet they are almost always united. And although whether this federative power is managed well or badly matters enormously to the commonwealth, it is far less capable than the executive power of being directed by prior, standing, positive laws. It must therefore necessarily be left to the prudence and wisdom of those in whose hands it lies, to be managed for the public good. Laws that concern subjects in their dealings with one another exist to direct their actions, and so they can well enough be made in advance. But what is to be done with regard to foreigners depends heavily on their actions, and on the shifting of designs and interests; it must therefore be left in large part to the prudence of those to whom this power is entrusted, to be managed by the best of their skill for the advantage of the commonwealth.

### § 148

Although, as I said, the executive and federative powers of every community are genuinely distinct in themselves, they can hardly be separated and placed at the same time in the hands of different persons. Both of them require the force of the society for their exercise, and it is almost unworkable to place the force of the commonwealth in distinct hands that are not subordinate to one another — or to place the executive and federative powers in persons who could act independently, which would put the force of the public under different commands. Sooner or later, that would be apt to cause disorder and ruin.
