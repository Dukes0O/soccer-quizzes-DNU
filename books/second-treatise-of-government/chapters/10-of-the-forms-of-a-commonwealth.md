# Chapter X — Of the Forms of a Commonwealth

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 132–133
>
> [← Of the Ends of Political Society and Government](09-of-the-ends-of-political-society-and-government.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the Extent of the Legislative Power →](11-of-the-extent-of-the-legislative-power.md)

## Overview

This short chapter is a hinge between the account of why political society exists (Chapter IX) and the long treatment of legislative power that follows (Chapter XI). Having established that when people first unite into society the whole power of the community rests naturally in the majority, Locke now asks what that majority can do with the power. His answer is a compact taxonomy of constitutional forms: the majority may keep the lawmaking power itself (democracy), lodge it in a few select men and their successors (oligarchy), or place it in one man (monarchy) — hereditary if it passes to his heirs, elective if the community keeps the right to nominate each successor. Any mixture or compound of these is equally legitimate, because the community may arrange its government however it thinks good.

The chapter's central analytical claim is that the form of a government is defined by where the supreme power — the legislative, the power of making laws — is placed. Since a lower power cannot dictate to a higher one, and no power but the supreme one can make laws, the location of the legislative power just is the constitution. A corollary follows: if the majority grants the legislative power only for a lifetime or a limited term, the power reverts to the community when the term ends, and the community may then dispose of it afresh and set up a whole new form of government.

Section 133 is a note on terminology. By "commonwealth" Locke does not mean democracy or any particular form of government; he means any independent political community — what the Latin writers called a *civitas*. English lacks a better word: "community" can name subordinate groups within a state, and "city" means something quite different. He borrows the usage from King James I and offers, with characteristic understatement, to trade the word for a better one if anyone objects.

## Key Concepts

- **Majority rule as the origin of constitutional form** — When people first unite into society, the whole power of the community naturally lies in the majority, which may then keep, delegate, or shape that power into any form of government it thinks good.
- **Democracy, oligarchy, and monarchy** — Locke's threefold classification by where lawmaking power sits: in the majority itself, in a few select men and their successors, or in one man. Monarchy subdivides into hereditary (power passes to heirs) and elective (power returns to the community at each death for the nomination of a successor).
- **Compounded and mixed forms** — The community is not confined to the pure types; it may combine them however it pleases, so the taxonomy is open-ended rather than exhaustive.
- **The legislative as the supreme power** — The power of making laws is the highest power in a commonwealth, since an inferior power cannot prescribe to a superior. The placement of this power defines the form of the government.
- **Reversion of power to the community** — If the legislative power is granted only for lives or for a limited time, it returns to the community when the term expires, and the community may then constitute an entirely new form of government.
- **Commonwealth (*civitas*)** — Locke's technical term for any independent political community, regardless of its form of government; deliberately distinguished from "democracy," "community," and "city."

## Modern Translation

### § 132

As I have shown, when people first unite into society, the whole power of the community rests naturally in the majority. The majority may use all that power to make laws for the community from time to time and to execute those laws through officers of its own appointing — and then the form of government is a pure democracy. Or it may put the power of making laws into the hands of a few select men and their heirs or successors — and then it is an oligarchy. Or into the hands of one man — and then it is a monarchy: if the power goes to him and his heirs, it is a hereditary monarchy; if it goes to him only for life, with the power of nominating a successor returning to the community at his death, it is an elective monarchy. And out of these, accordingly, the community may create compounded and mixed forms of government, as it thinks best. If the legislative power is at first given by the majority to one or more persons only for their lifetimes, or for any limited time, with the supreme power then to revert to the community — then, once it has so reverted, the community may dispose of it again anew, placing it in whatever hands it pleases, and so constitute a new form of government. For the form of a government depends on where the supreme power — which is the legislative — is placed. It is impossible to conceive of an inferior power prescribing to a superior, or of anyone but the supreme power making laws. So wherever the power of making laws is placed, that determines the form of the commonwealth.

### § 133

By "commonwealth" I must be understood throughout to mean not a democracy, or any particular form of government, but any independent community — what the Latin authors expressed by the word *civitas*. The word in our language that best corresponds to it is "commonwealth," which most properly describes such a society of men. "Community" or "city" in English does not: there can be subordinate communities within a government, and "city" among us carries a quite different meaning from "commonwealth." Therefore, to avoid ambiguity, I ask leave to use the word "commonwealth" in the sense in which I find it used by King James the First; and I take that to be its true meaning. If anyone dislikes the word, I am happy to join him in exchanging it for a better one.
