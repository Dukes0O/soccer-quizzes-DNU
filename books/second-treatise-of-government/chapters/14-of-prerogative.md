# Chapter XIV — Of Prerogative

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 159–168
>
> [← Of the Subordination of the Powers of the Commonwealth](13-of-the-subordination-of-the-powers.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Paternal, Political, and Despotical Power →](15-of-paternal-political-and-despotical-power.md)

## Overview

Having argued in the previous chapters that the legislative power is supreme and the executive subordinate to it, Locke now confronts an awkward fact of real government: laws cannot foresee everything, and legislatures are too slow and too rarely assembled to handle every emergency. Someone must be able to act for the public good where the law is silent — and sometimes even against the letter of the law. That discretionary power, lodged in the executive, is what Locke calls prerogative.

The chapter's central move is to define prerogative not as a personal right of kings but as a trust: "the power of doing public good without a rule." So long as it is genuinely used for the community's benefit, the people never question it — Locke points to English history, where the wisest and best princes enjoyed the widest prerogative precisely because their subjects trusted them. Trouble begins when weak or bad princes cite those good rulers as precedent, claiming the same latitude as a right of office to be used for their own separate interest. Then the people rightly step in and define prerogative by express laws, and Locke insists this is no "encroachment" on the crown: you cannot encroach on a power that only ever existed for your own good.

The chapter closes with the hard question that runs through the whole treatise: who judges whether prerogative has been abused, when the executive controls the very convening of the legislature? Locke's answer is that there is no judge on earth in such a conflict — the people must "appeal to heaven," that is, resist. This judgment, whether there is just cause for such an appeal, is one the people can never surrender, because no one can consent to be destroyed. Section 168 thus lays the theoretical foundation for the right of revolution developed in the final chapters, while Locke reassures readers that this remedy operates only when abuse is so great that the majority feels it — no danger at all to wise princes.

## Key Concepts

- **Prerogative** — The executive's power to act at discretion for the public good without the direction of the law, and sometimes even against its letter. Locke defines it as "nothing but the power of doing public good without a rule."
- **The fundamental law of nature and government** — That all members of society are to be preserved as far as possible. This law outranks even the written laws, which is why strict legality must sometimes yield to executive discretion (as when a house must be torn down to stop a fire).
- **Prerogative as trust, not right** — The people tacitly permit rulers to act beyond the law only for their good. When a prince uses that latitude for a private interest, the people may define and limit it by express laws — and doing so takes nothing that ever rightfully belonged to him.
- **The precedent problem** — Good, "god-like" princes are paradoxically the most dangerous to liberty, because their bad successors cite the enlarged prerogative of those reigns as a standard and a right, forcing contests and public disorder before the people recover their original right.
- **The power of calling parliaments** — Locke's concrete English example: fixing the time, place, and duration of parliaments is a genuine royal prerogative, but held on trust to be used as the good of the nation requires.
- **Appeal to heaven** — Where executive and legislature (or rulers and people) conflict and no earthly judge exists, the people retain, by a law prior to all human laws, the right to judge whether they have just cause to resist. This judgment cannot be given away, since no one can consent to his own destruction.

## Modern Translation

### § 159

Where the legislative and executive powers are in separate hands — as they are in all moderated monarchies and well-designed governments — the good of society requires that several things be left to the discretion of the person who holds the executive power. Legislators cannot foresee, and provide by law for, everything that might be useful to the community. So the executor of the laws, having the power in his hands, has by the common law of nature a right to use it for the good of society in the many cases where the domestic law gives no direction, until the legislature can conveniently be assembled to provide for them. There are many things the law simply cannot provide for, and these must necessarily be left to the discretion of the holder of executive power, to be handled as the public good and advantage require. Indeed, it is proper that in some cases the laws themselves should give way to the executive power — or rather, to this fundamental law of nature and government: that as far as possible, all the members of society are to be preserved. Many accidents can happen in which a strict and rigid observance of the laws would do harm — for example, refusing to pull down an innocent man's house to stop a fire when the house next to it is burning. And a man may sometimes fall within the reach of the law, which makes no distinction of persons, by an action that actually deserves reward and pardon. So it is proper that the ruler should have a power, in many cases, to soften the severity of the law and pardon some offenders. Since the purpose of government is the preservation of all, as far as possible, even the guilty should be spared where sparing them does no injury to the innocent.

### § 160

This power to act at discretion for the public good, without the prescription of the law and sometimes even against it, is what is called prerogative. In some governments the lawmaking power is not always in session; it is usually too numerous, and therefore too slow, for the speed that execution requires. It is also impossible to foresee — and so to provide for by law — every accident and necessity that may concern the public, or to make laws that will do no harm if they are executed with inflexible rigor on every occasion and against every person who comes in their way. For these reasons, a latitude is left to the executive power to do many things of its own choice that the laws do not prescribe.

### § 161

While this power is employed for the benefit of the community, and in keeping with the trust and purposes of the government, it is undoubted prerogative and is never questioned. The people are rarely if ever fussy or particular on this point. They are far from scrutinizing prerogative as long as it is employed, to any tolerable degree, for the use it was meant for — that is, for the good of the people, and not obviously against it. But if a question does arise between the executive power and the people about something claimed as a prerogative, the question is easily settled by asking whether the exercise of that prerogative tends to the good or the harm of the people.

### § 162

It is easy to see that in the infancy of governments, when commonwealths differed little from families in the number of their people, they also differed little from them in the number of their laws. Since the governors were like fathers to them, watching over them for their good, government was almost entirely prerogative. A few established laws did the job, and the discretion and care of the ruler supplied the rest. But when mistake or flattery led weak princes to use this power for private ends of their own rather than for the public good, the people found themselves obliged to have prerogative limited by express laws on the points where they suffered from it. In this way the people found it necessary to declare limits on a prerogative which they and their ancestors had left, in the fullest latitude, to the wisdom of princes who put it to no use but the right one — namely, the good of their people.

### § 163

So those who say that the people have "encroached upon the prerogative" whenever they have gotten any part of it defined by positive laws have a completely wrong idea of government. In doing so, the people have not taken from the prince anything that rightfully belonged to him. They have only declared that the power they left indefinitely in his hands, or his ancestors' hands, to be exercised for their good, was not something they intended him to have when he used it otherwise. Since the purpose of government is the good of the community, any change made in it that tends toward that end cannot be an encroachment on anyone — for no one in government can have a right directed at any other end. The only real encroachments are those that damage or hinder the public good. Those who say otherwise talk as if the prince had an interest distinct and separate from the good of the community, and was not made for that good — and that assumption is the root and source of almost all the evils and disorders that occur in kingly governments. And if that really were so, the people under such a government would not be a society of rational creatures who entered into a community for their mutual good; they would not be people who set rulers over themselves to guard and promote that good. They would have to be regarded as a herd of inferior creatures under the dominion of a master who keeps them and works them for his own pleasure or profit. If men were so devoid of reason, and so brutish, as to enter society on such terms, then prerogative might indeed be what some men would like it to be: an arbitrary power to do things harmful to the people.

### § 164

But a rational creature cannot be supposed, when free, to place himself in subjection to another for his own harm — though, where he finds a good and wise ruler, he may not think it necessary or useful to set precise limits on his power in every matter. So prerogative can be nothing but this: the people permitting their rulers to do various things of their own free choice where the law is silent, and sometimes even against the direct letter of the law, for the public good — and the people's acquiescing in it when it is done that way. A good prince, who is mindful of the trust placed in his hands and careful of his people's good, cannot have too much prerogative — that is, too much power to do good. But a weak and bad prince changes things. Suppose he claims the power his predecessors exercised without the direction of the law, treating it as a prerogative belonging to him by right of his office, which he may exercise at his pleasure to create or promote an interest distinct from the public's. That gives the people occasion to claim their right and to limit a power which, while it was exercised for their good, they were content to allow tacitly.

### § 165

Anyone who looks into the history of England will find that prerogative was always largest in the hands of our wisest and best princes. The people, observing that the whole tendency of their actions was the public good, did not contest what was done without law toward that end. Or if some human frailty or mistake appeared in small deviations from that end — for princes are only men, made like everyone else — it was still visible that the main body of their conduct aimed at nothing but the care of the public. The people, therefore, finding reason to be satisfied with these princes whenever they acted without the letter of the law or contrary to it, acquiesced in what they did and, without the least complaint, let them enlarge their prerogative as they pleased. They judged rightly that in this the princes did nothing to the prejudice of their laws, since they acted in conformity with the foundation and purpose of all laws: the public good.

### § 166

Such god-like princes did indeed have some title to arbitrary power — by the same argument that would prove absolute monarchy the best government because it is the form by which God himself governs the universe: such kings share in his wisdom and goodness. This is the basis of the saying that the reigns of good princes have always been the most dangerous to the liberties of their people. For when their successors, running the government with very different intentions, would cite the actions of those good rulers as precedent and make them the standard of their own prerogative — as if what had been done only for the good of the people were a right in them to do the people harm, if they so pleased — it has often caused contests, and sometimes public disorders, before the people could recover their original right and get it declared that this was not prerogative at all, which in truth it never was. For it is impossible that anyone in society should ever have a right to do the people harm — though it is entirely possible, and reasonable, that the people should not set out to place any limits on the prerogative of kings or rulers who did not themselves transgress the bounds of the public good. For prerogative is nothing but the power of doing public good without a rule.

### § 167

The power of calling parliaments in England — as to precise time, place, and duration — is certainly a prerogative of the king, but always with this trust attached: that it shall be used for the good of the nation, as the urgencies of the times and the variety of occasions require. Since it is impossible to foresee which will always be the fittest place for parliaments to assemble in, and what the best season, the choice of these was left to the executive power, to be exercised in whatever way would best serve the public good and best suit the purposes of parliaments.

### § 168

In this matter of prerogative, the old question will be asked: but who shall be judge of whether this power is being rightly used? I answer: between an executive power in being, holding such a prerogative, and a legislature that depends on his will for its convening, there can be no judge on earth — just as there can be none between the legislature and the people, should either the executive or the legislature, once they have the power in their hands, design or set about to enslave or destroy them. In this, as in all other cases where they have no judge on earth, the people have no remedy but to appeal to heaven. For rulers who make such attempts are exercising a power the people never put into their hands — for the people can never be supposed to consent that anyone should rule over them for their harm — and so are doing what they have no right to do. And where the body of the people, or any single man, is deprived of their right, or is subjected to the exercise of a power without right, and has no appeal on earth, then they are at liberty to appeal to heaven whenever they judge the cause to be of sufficient importance. Therefore, although the people cannot be judge in the sense of having, by the constitution of that society, any superior power to decide the case and give an effective sentence, they have nevertheless — by a law antecedent and paramount to all positive laws of men — reserved to themselves that final determination which belongs to all mankind where no appeal lies on earth: namely, to judge whether they have just cause to make their appeal to heaven. And this judgment they cannot give up, since it is beyond a man's power to submit himself to another in such a way as to give him liberty to destroy him. God and nature never allow a man to abandon himself so far as to neglect his own preservation; and since he cannot take away his own life, neither can he give another the power to take it. Nor should anyone think this lays a permanent foundation for disorder. This remedy does not operate until the harm is so great that the majority feel it, are weary of it, and find it necessary to have it corrected. But this is a danger the executive power, or wise princes, never need run — and it is the thing, above all others, that they most need to avoid, as of all things the most perilous.
