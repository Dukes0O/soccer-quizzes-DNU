# Chapter II — Of the State of Nature

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 4–15
>
> [← Introduction](01-introduction.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of the State of War →](03-of-the-state-of-war.md)

## Overview

Having promised in Chapter I to derive political power from its true origin, Locke begins here with his foundational thought experiment: the state of nature, the condition all human beings are in before (and outside of) government. He defines it by two features. It is a state of perfect *freedom* — everyone may order their actions and dispose of their possessions and persons as they see fit — and a state of perfect *equality*, since no one has natural authority over anyone else. But crucially, Locke insists this freedom is not lawlessness: the state of nature is governed by the law of nature, which is reason itself, and which forbids harming anyone's life, health, liberty, or possessions.

The chapter's most distinctive — and, as Locke admits, "strange" — doctrine follows: since a law is useless without someone to enforce it, and since no one in the state of nature stands above anyone else, *every person* has the right to punish violations of the law of nature. Locke carefully limits this power: punishment must be proportionate, aimed only at reparation (for the victim) and restraint/deterrence (which anyone may exact), never at the punisher's whim. He defends the doctrine with a pointed example — how else could any government punish a foreign visitor, who never consented to its laws?

Locke then answers two objections. First, that people are bad judges in their own cases: he concedes this is the great "inconvenience" of the state of nature and grants that civil government is its proper remedy — but turns the point against absolute monarchy, where one man judges his own case over a whole people with no accountability at all. Second, that no one has ever actually lived in a state of nature: he replies that all independent rulers and states are in a state of nature with respect to one another, and that only one specific compact — the mutual agreement to form one body politic — ends that state.

The chapter thus sets up the entire architecture of the treatise. The rights individuals hold in the state of nature (self-preservation, punishment, reparation) are exactly the powers they will later transfer to government by consent, and the "inconveniences" identified here supply the motive for that transfer.

## Key Concepts

- **State of nature** — The condition people are naturally in absent any common political authority: perfect freedom to act and dispose of one's person and property within the bounds of the law of nature, without asking anyone's permission. It is not a mere historical hypothesis for Locke: independent rulers, and any two people without a common government, are in it right now.
- **Natural equality** — Since all humans are creatures of the same species and rank, born to the same advantages and faculties, none has natural jurisdiction over another — unless God were to explicitly appoint one above the rest. All power in the state of nature is therefore reciprocal.
- **Law of nature** — Reason itself, functioning as a binding law that governs the state of nature. It teaches that no one may harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions, and grounds this in the claim that all people are God's workmanship and property, made to last during his pleasure, not one another's.
- **Liberty vs. licence** — Natural freedom is not permission to do anything whatever. A person may not destroy himself, or even a creature in his possession except for some nobler use than bare preservation, and is bound to preserve the rest of mankind when his own preservation is not at stake.
- **Executive power of the law of nature** — Locke's "strange doctrine": in the state of nature everyone has the right to enforce the law of nature by punishing transgressors, proportionately, so far as serves reparation and restraint. Without this universal enforcement right the law of nature would be pointless.
- **Reparation vs. restraint** — The two distinct rights that arise from a crime: the injured party alone has the right to seek reparation (and alone can waive it), while everyone shares the right to punish for deterrence and the security of mankind. This distinction explains why a magistrate can pardon a crime but cannot cancel a victim's claim to compensation.
- **The inconveniences of the state of nature** — The disorders that follow from people being judges in their own cases: partiality toward oneself and one's friends, and excess driven by passion and revenge. Locke grants that civil government is the remedy — while insisting absolute monarchy is worse than the disease.
- **Consent and the body politic** — Not every agreement ends the state of nature; only the one compact by which people mutually agree to form a single community and body politic. All other promises and bargains bind people who nonetheless remain in the state of nature with respect to each other.

## Modern Translation

### § 4

To understand political power correctly and trace it to its origin, we must consider what state all human beings are naturally in. It is a state of perfect freedom: freedom to order their actions and dispose of their possessions and their persons as they think fit, within the bounds of the law of nature, without asking anyone's permission or depending on any other person's will.

It is also a state of equality, in which all power and jurisdiction is reciprocal, no one having more than anyone else. Nothing is more evident than this: creatures of the same species and rank, born without distinction to all the same advantages of nature and the use of the same faculties, should also be equal among themselves, with no subordination or subjection — unless the lord and master of them all were, by some clear declaration of his will, to set one above the others and confer on him, by an evident and unmistakable appointment, an undoubted right to dominion and sovereignty.

### § 5

The judicious Hooker regards this natural equality of human beings as so self-evident and beyond all question that he makes it the foundation of the obligation of mutual love among people, on which he builds the duties they owe one another, and from which he derives the great maxims of justice and charity. His words are:

> "The same natural inducement has brought men to recognize that it is no less their duty to love others than themselves. For things that are equal must all be measured by one standard. If I cannot help wanting to receive good from every other person's hands — as much good as anyone can wish for his own soul — how can I expect to have any part of that desire satisfied unless I take care to satisfy the same desire, which undoubtedly exists in other people, since we are all of one and the same nature? To have anything offered to them contrary to this desire must grieve them in every respect as much as it would grieve me. So if I do harm, I must expect to suffer, since there is no reason others should show me a greater measure of love than I have shown to them. My desire, therefore, to be loved by my natural equals as much as possible imposes on me a natural duty to bear them fully the same affection. And from this relation of equality between ourselves and those who are like ourselves, no one is ignorant of the various rules and canons that natural reason has drawn for the direction of life." (Hooker, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1)

### § 6

But although this is a state of liberty, it is not a state of licence. Though a person in that state has an uncontrollable liberty to dispose of his person and possessions, he does not have liberty to destroy himself — nor even any creature in his possession, except where some nobler use than its bare preservation calls for it. The state of nature has a law of nature to govern it, which obliges everyone. And reason, which is that law, teaches all mankind who will only consult it that, since all are equal and independent, no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions.

The reason is this: all human beings are the workmanship of one omnipotent and infinitely wise Maker; all are the servants of one sovereign Master, sent into the world by his order and about his business. They are his property — the property of the one whose workmanship they are — made to last as long as *he* pleases, not as long as some other person pleases. And since we are all furnished with similar faculties and share in one common community of nature, no such rank ordering can be supposed among us as would authorize us to destroy one another, as if we were made for each other's use the way the lower ranks of creatures are made for ours.

Each person, just as he is bound to preserve himself and not abandon his post willfully, is by the same reasoning bound — when his own preservation is not at stake — to preserve the rest of mankind as much as he can. Except to do justice on an offender, he may not take away or damage another person's life, or anything that tends to preserve another's life: liberty, health, limb, or goods.

### § 7

So that all people may be restrained from invading others' rights and from hurting one another, and so that the law of nature — which wills the peace and preservation of all mankind — may be observed, the enforcement of the law of nature in that state is placed in every person's hands. Everyone has a right to punish those who break that law, to whatever degree may prevent its violation. For the law of nature, like every other law that concerns people in this world, would be pointless if no one in the state of nature had the power to execute it, and by doing so protect the innocent and restrain offenders. And if anyone in the state of nature may punish another for evil he has done, then everyone may do so. In that state of perfect equality, where by nature there is no superiority or jurisdiction of one person over another, whatever anyone may do to carry out that law, everyone must necessarily have a right to do.

### § 8

This is how, in the state of nature, one person comes to have power over another. But it is not absolute or arbitrary power — not power to deal with a criminal, once he has him in his hands, according to the passionate heat or boundless extravagance of his own will. It is power only to pay him back, so far as calm reason and conscience dictate, in proportion to his transgression — that is, so much as will serve for reparation and restraint. These two are the only reasons one person may lawfully do harm to another, which is what we call punishment.

In breaking the law of nature, the offender declares that he lives by some rule other than reason and common fairness, which is the standard God has set for human actions, for people's mutual security. He thereby becomes dangerous to mankind, because he has slighted and broken the bond that is supposed to protect people from injury and violence. Since this is an offense against the whole species, and against the peace and safety of the species that the law of nature provides for, every person — by the right he has to preserve mankind in general — may restrain, or where necessary destroy, things that are harmful to mankind. He may therefore inflict on anyone who has broken that law enough harm to make him repent having done it, and by doing so deter him — and, through his example, deter others — from doing the same mischief. On this ground, and in this case, EVERY MAN HAS A RIGHT TO PUNISH THE OFFENDER, AND TO BE EXECUTIONER OF THE LAW OF NATURE.

### § 9

I have no doubt this will seem a very strange doctrine to some people. But before they condemn it, I ask them to explain to me by what right any prince or state can execute or punish a foreigner for a crime he commits in their country. It is certain that their laws — by virtue of whatever sanction they receive from the declared will of the legislature — do not reach a stranger. The laws do not speak to him; and even if they did, he is not bound to listen to them. The legislative authority that makes those laws binding on the subjects of that commonwealth has no power over him. To an Indian, those who hold the supreme lawmaking power in England, France, or Holland are just like the rest of the world: men without authority. Therefore, if by the law of nature every person does not have a power to punish offenses against it, as he soberly judges the case requires, I do not see how the magistrates of any community can punish a foreigner from another country — since, with respect to him, they can have no more power than every person naturally has over another.

### § 10

Besides the *crime* — which consists in violating the law and departing from the right rule of reason, whereby a person becomes degenerate to that degree, declares that he has abandoned the principles of human nature, and shows himself to be a harmful creature — there is usually also an *injury* done to some particular person, someone who suffers damage from the transgression. In that case, the person who has suffered the damage has, in addition to the right of punishment he shares with everyone else, a particular right to seek reparation from the one who caused it. And anyone else who thinks it just may join the injured party and help him recover from the offender enough to make satisfaction for the harm he has suffered.

### § 11

From these two distinct rights — the right of punishing the crime, for restraint and to prevent similar offenses, which belongs to everyone; and the right of taking reparation, which belongs only to the injured party — it follows that the magistrate, who as magistrate holds the common right of punishing, can often, where the public good does not demand the law's execution, remit the punishment of criminal offenses by his own authority. But he cannot remit the satisfaction owed to any private person for the damage he has received. Only the one who suffered the damage has the right to demand that in his own name, and only he can waive it. The injured person has this power of appropriating the offender's goods or service to himself by the right of self-preservation, just as every person has a power to punish the crime, to prevent its being committed again, by the right he has of preserving all mankind and doing every reasonable thing he can toward that end.

This is why every person in the state of nature has the power to kill a murderer — both to deter others from committing the same injury (which no reparation can compensate) by the example of the punishment that everyone may inflict for it, and also to protect people from the attacks of a criminal who has renounced reason, the common rule and measure God has given to mankind. By the unjust violence and slaughter he has committed against one person, he has declared war against all mankind, and so may be destroyed like a lion or a tiger — one of those wild savage beasts with whom human beings can have no society or security. On this is grounded that great law of nature: "Whoever sheds man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed." And Cain was so fully convinced that everyone had a right to destroy such a criminal that after murdering his brother he cried out, "Everyone who finds me will kill me" — so plainly was this written in the hearts of all mankind.

### § 12

By the same reasoning, a person in the state of nature may punish lesser breaches of that law. Perhaps it will be asked: with death? I answer that each transgression may be punished to whatever degree, and with as much severity, as will suffice to make it a bad bargain for the offender, give him cause to repent, and frighten others away from doing the same. Every offense that can be committed in the state of nature may also be punished in the state of nature, equally and to the same extent as it could be in a commonwealth. It would be beside my present purpose to go into the details of the law of nature or its measures of punishment; but it is certain that there is such a law, and that it is as intelligible and plain to a rational creature who studies it as the positive laws of commonwealths — possibly plainer, in fact, by as much as reason is easier to understand than the fancies and intricate contrivances of men, put into words in pursuit of conflicting and hidden interests. For that is truly what a great part of the municipal laws of countries are — laws that are right only so far as they are founded on the law of nature, by which they must be regulated and interpreted.

### § 13

Against this strange doctrine — namely, that in the state of nature everyone has the executive power of the law of nature — I have no doubt it will be objected that it is unreasonable for people to be judges in their own cases; that self-love will make them partial to themselves and their friends; and that, on the other side, ill nature, passion, and revenge will carry them too far in punishing others; that nothing but confusion and disorder will follow from this; and that God has therefore certainly appointed government to restrain the partiality and violence of men. I readily grant that civil government is the proper remedy for the inconveniences of the state of nature, which must certainly be great where people may be judges in their own case — for it is easy to imagine that someone unjust enough to wrong his brother will hardly be just enough to condemn himself for it.

But I would ask those who make this objection to remember that absolute monarchs are only men. If government is to be the remedy for those evils that necessarily follow from people being judges in their own cases, so that the state of nature is not to be endured, then I want to know what kind of government that is — and how much better it is than the state of nature — in which one man, commanding a multitude, has the liberty to be judge in his own case and may do to all his subjects whatever he pleases, without the least liberty for anyone to question or control those who carry out his pleasure; a government in which whatever he does, whether led by reason, mistake, or passion, must be submitted to. Far better is the state of nature, where people are not bound to submit to another's unjust will, and where, if the one who judges judges wrongly — in his own case or anyone else's — he is answerable for it to the rest of mankind.

### § 14

It is often raised as a mighty objection: where are, or where ever were, any people in such a state of nature? For now, this answer should suffice: since all princes and rulers of independent governments throughout the world are in a state of nature, it is plain the world never was, and never will be, without numbers of people in that state. I say all rulers of *independent* communities, whether or not they are in league with others. For it is not every compact that ends the state of nature between people, but only this one: agreeing together, mutually, to enter into one community and form one body politic. People may make other promises and compacts with one another and still remain in the state of nature. The promises and bargains for barter and the like between the two men on the desert island mentioned by Garcilasso de la Vega in his history of Peru, or between a Swiss and an Indian in the woods of America, are binding on them, even though they are entirely in a state of nature with respect to one another. For truth and the keeping of faith belong to human beings as human beings, not as members of society.

### § 15

To those who say there were never any people in the state of nature, I will not only oppose the authority of the judicious Hooker (*Ecclesiastical Polity*, book 1, section 10), where he says:

> "The laws that have been mentioned so far — that is, the laws of nature — bind men absolutely, simply as men, even if they never have any settled fellowship or any solemn agreement among themselves about what to do or not to do. But because we are not, by ourselves, sufficient to furnish ourselves with an adequate store of the things needed for the kind of life our nature desires — a life fit for the dignity of man — therefore, to supply the defects and imperfections we have when living alone and solely by ourselves, we are naturally led to seek communion and fellowship with others. This was the cause of men first uniting themselves in politic societies."

I affirm more than this: all human beings are naturally in that state, and remain so until, by their own consent, they make themselves members of some politic society. And I have no doubt that in the rest of this discourse I will make that very clear.
