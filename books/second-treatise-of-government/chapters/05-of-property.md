# Chapter V — Of Property

> John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1690) · Sections § 25–51
>
> [← Of Slavery](04-of-slavery.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Of Paternal Power →](06-of-paternal-power.md)

## Overview

This is the most famous chapter of the *Second Treatise*, and it answers a puzzle Locke sets for himself: if God gave the earth to all of humanity in common, how can any individual ever come to own anything privately? Locke's rivals (especially followers of Filmer) claimed private property could only be explained if God had given the whole world to Adam and his royal heirs. Locke's answer cuts in the opposite direction: private property arises naturally, without any universal grant to one man and without needing the express consent of everyone else.

The engine of the argument is labor. Every person owns their own body and therefore owns their own labor. When someone mixes that labor with something in the common state of nature — gathering acorns, drawing water, killing a deer, tilling a field — they annex something of their own to it and thereby make it their property, at least so long as "enough, and as good" is left for others. Locke then extends the argument from gathered goods to land itself, and argues that appropriation harms no one, because cultivated land is vastly more productive than waste land: the man who encloses ten acres effectively gives ninety back to mankind.

The chapter's second movement concerns limits and their dissolution. Nature sets a boundary on property — you may take only what you can use before it spoils. In the early world this kept possessions small and quarrels rare. But the invention of money, a durable thing men agree to value, lets people fairly enlarge their possessions beyond what they can consume, because gold and silver do not perish in the owner's hands. Locke concludes that by tacitly consenting to money, people have consented to a disproportionate and unequal division of the earth — all of this happening outside the bounds of political society, before laws and government regulate property by positive constitutions.

The chapter sits at the heart of the treatise's overall project: it shows that property rights exist prior to government and independent of it, which is why (as later chapters argue) government exists to protect property, and forfeits its authority when it invades it.

## Key Concepts

- **Property in one's own person** — Every man owns himself; nobody else has any right to his body. From this follows ownership of his labor and the work of his hands, which is the foundation of all other property.
- **The labor theory of appropriation** — Whatever a person removes from the common state of nature and mixes his labor with becomes his property, because he has joined to it something that is unquestionably his own. No compact or express consent of mankind is needed.
- **The "enough, and as good" proviso** — Appropriation by labor is legitimate at least where enough, and as good, is left in common for others. He who leaves as much as another can use does as good as take nothing at all.
- **The spoilage limit** — The law of nature that gives property also bounds it: a person may take only as much as he can use to some advantage of life before it spoils. Anything beyond that is more than his share and belongs to others; nothing was made by God for man to spoil or destroy.
- **Labor as the source of value** — Labor, not nature, puts the difference of value on everything. Locke estimates that nine-tenths — really ninety-nine hundredths — of the value of things useful to human life is owing to labor rather than to nature.
- **Money** — A lasting thing (gold, silver) that men by mutual consent take in exchange for the truly useful but perishable supports of life. Because it does not spoil, hoarding it wrongs no one, and its adoption removes the practical ceiling on the size of possessions.
- **Consent to inequality** — By tacitly agreeing to put a value on money, men have consented, outside of society and without any compact, to a disproportionate and unequal possession of the earth. Within governments, by contrast, laws and positive constitutions regulate property.

## Modern Translation

### § 25

Consider either natural reason — which tells us that human beings, once born, have a right to their own preservation, and therefore to food and drink and whatever else nature provides for their subsistence — or revelation, which records the grants God made of the world to Adam, and to Noah and his sons. Either way, it is perfectly clear that God, as King David says (Psalm 115:16), "has given the earth to the children of men" — given it to mankind in common. Granting that, some people find it a very great difficulty to see how anyone could ever come to own anything privately. I will not settle for the reply that if it is hard to derive property from the supposition that God gave the world to Adam and his posterity in common, it is *impossible* for anyone but a single universal monarch to have any property on the supposition that God gave the world to Adam and his heirs in succession, excluding all the rest of his descendants. Instead, I will try to show how people could come to own individual parts of what God gave to mankind in common — and that without any express compact among all the joint owners.

### § 26

God, who gave the world to men in common, also gave them reason to make use of it to the best advantage of life and convenience. The earth and everything in it is given to men for the support and comfort of their existence. And though all the fruits it naturally produces and the animals it feeds belong to mankind in common — since they are produced by the spontaneous hand of nature, and nobody originally has a private dominion over any of them, excluding the rest of mankind, while they remain in their natural state — still, since they are given for men's *use*, there must necessarily be some way of appropriating them before they can be of any use or benefit to any particular person. The fruit or venison that feeds the wild Indian, who knows nothing of enclosures and is still a tenant in common, must be *his* — so much his, that is, so much a part of him — that no one else can any longer have any right to it, before it can do him any good in supporting his life.

### § 27

Though the earth and all inferior creatures are common to all men, every man has a *property in his own person*. Nobody has any right to that but himself. The labor of his body and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his. So whenever he removes something out of the state nature has provided and left it in, he has mixed his labor with it and joined to it something that is his own — and by that he makes it his property. Since he has removed it from the common state nature placed it in, his labor has attached something to it that excludes the common right of other men. This labor is the unquestionable property of the laborer, so no one but he can have a right to what it is once joined to — at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others.

### § 28

Someone who is nourished by the acorns he picked up under an oak, or the apples he gathered from the trees in the woods, has certainly appropriated them to himself. Nobody can deny that the nourishment is his. So I ask: when did they begin to be his? When he digested them? When he ate them? When he boiled them? When he brought them home? When he picked them up? Plainly, if the first gathering did not make them his, nothing else could. That labor put a distinction between them and the common stock. It added something to them beyond what nature, the common mother of all, had done — and so they became his private right. Will anyone say he had no right to those acorns or apples he appropriated this way, because he lacked the consent of all mankind to make them his? Was it robbery to take for himself what belonged to everyone in common? If a consent like that were necessary, man would have starved despite the plenty God gave him. Even in commons that remain common *by compact*, we see that it is the taking of some part of what is common — removing it from the state nature leaves it in — that begins the property; without that, the common is of no use at all. And taking this or that part does not depend on the express consent of all the commoners. Thus the grass my horse has bitten, the turf my servant has cut, and the ore I have dug, in any place where I have a right to these things in common with others, become my property without anyone's assignment or consent. The labor that was mine, removing them from the common state they were in, has fixed my property in them.

### § 29

If the explicit consent of every commoner were necessary before anyone could appropriate any part of what is given in common, children or servants could not cut the meat their father or master had provided for them in common without assigning each person his particular portion. Though the water running in the fountain belongs to everyone, who can doubt that the water in the pitcher belongs only to the person who drew it out? His labor has taken it out of the hands of nature, where it was common and belonged equally to all her children, and has thereby appropriated it to himself.

### § 30

Thus this law of reason makes the deer belong to the Indian who has killed it. It is acknowledged to be the goods of the man who bestowed his labor on it, even though before that it was the common right of everyone. And among those counted the civilized part of mankind — who have made and multiplied positive laws to determine property — this original law of nature governing the beginning of property in what was formerly common still holds. By virtue of it, whatever fish anyone catches in the ocean (that great common of mankind, which still remains common), or whatever ambergris anyone gathers there, is made the property of the person who takes those pains, by the labor that removes it from the common state nature left it in. Even among us, the hare someone is hunting is considered to belong to whoever pursues her during the chase: since she is an animal still regarded as common, no man's private possession, whoever has spent enough labor on such a creature to find and pursue her has thereby removed her from the state of nature in which she was common, and has begun a property.

### § 31

Perhaps it will be objected that if gathering acorns or other fruits of the earth and so on creates a right to them, then anyone may engross as much as he wants. To which I answer: not so. The same law of nature that gives us property in this way also *bounds* that property. "God has given us all things richly" (1 Tim. 6:12) — that is the voice of reason confirmed by inspiration. But how far has he given it to us? *To enjoy.* As much as anyone can use to any advantage of life before it spoils — that much he may fix a property in by his labor. Whatever is beyond this is more than his share and belongs to others. Nothing was made by God for man to spoil or destroy. And considering the plenty of natural provisions the world contained for a long time, and how few people there were to consume them; considering how small a part of that provision one man's industry could reach and engross to the prejudice of others — especially if he kept within the bounds reason set on what might serve his use — there could then be little room for quarrels or disputes about property established in this way.

### § 32

But the chief subject of property now is not the fruits of the earth and the animals that live on it, but *the earth itself*, since it contains and carries with it everything else. I think it is plain that property in land is acquired the same way as the property just described. As much land as a man tills, plants, improves, cultivates, and can use the product of — that much is his property. By his labor he, as it were, fences it off from the common. Nor does it invalidate his right to object that everyone else has an equal title to it, and that he therefore cannot appropriate or enclose it without the consent of all his fellow-commoners, that is, all mankind. God, when he gave the world in common to all mankind, also commanded man to labor, and the poverty of his condition required it of him. God and man's own reason commanded him to subdue the earth — that is, to improve it for the benefit of life — and in doing so to invest in it something that was his own: his labor. Whoever, in obedience to this command of God, subdued, tilled, and sowed any part of the earth thereby annexed to it something that was his property, which no one else had any title to, and which no one could take from him without injury.

### § 33

Nor did this appropriation of any parcel of land by improving it prejudice any other man, since there was still enough left, and as good — indeed more than the still-unprovided-for could use. In effect, there was never any less left for others because of one man's enclosure for himself: whoever leaves as much as another can make use of does as good as take nothing at all. No one could think himself injured by another man's drinking, even a long draught, if he had a whole river of the same water left to quench his own thirst. And the case of land and water, where there is enough of both, is exactly the same.

### § 34

God gave the world to men in common. But since he gave it to them for their benefit, and for the greatest conveniences of life they could draw from it, he cannot be supposed to have meant that it should always remain common and uncultivated. He gave it for the use of the industrious and rational (and labor was to be their title to it), not to the fancy or covetousness of the quarrelsome and contentious. Whoever had land as good left for his own improvement as what was already taken had no cause to complain and no business meddling with what another's labor had already improved. If he did, it is plain he wanted the benefit of another man's pains, to which he had no right — not the ground God had given him in common with others to labor on, of which there was as good left as what was already possessed, and more than he knew what to do with, or than his industry could reach.

### § 35

It is true that on common land in England, or in any other country with plenty of people living under government, with money and commerce, no one can enclose or appropriate any part without the consent of all his fellow-commoners. That is because such land is left common *by compact* — by the law of the land, which must not be violated. And though it is common with respect to certain men, it is not common to all mankind, but is the joint property of that country or that parish. Besides, the remainder after such an enclosure would not be as good for the rest of the commoners as the whole was when they could all use the whole — whereas at the beginning, at the first peopling of the great common of the world, things stood quite otherwise. The law man was then under actually *favored* appropriating. God commanded him to labor, and his needs forced him to it. Whatever he fixed his labor in was his property, which could not be taken from him. And so we see that subduing or cultivating the earth and having dominion are joined together: the one gave title to the other. So God, by commanding man to subdue the earth, gave him authority to appropriate to that extent; and the condition of human life, which requires labor and materials to work on, necessarily introduces private possessions.

### § 36

Nature has set the measure of property well, by the extent of men's labor and the conveniences of life. No man's labor could subdue or appropriate everything, nor could his enjoyment consume more than a small part; so it was impossible for anyone, in this way, to encroach on the right of another or acquire property to the prejudice of his neighbor, who would still have room for as good and as large a possession (after the other had taken his out) as before it was appropriated. This measure confined every man's possession to a very moderate proportion — such as he could appropriate to himself without injuring anybody — in the first ages of the world, when men were in more danger of being lost by wandering from their company in the vast wilderness the earth then was, than of being cramped for want of room to plant in. And the same measure may still be allowed today, without prejudice to anybody, full as the world seems. For suppose a man or a family in the condition of the first peopling of the world by the children of Adam or Noah: let him plant in some inland, vacant place in America. We will find that the possessions he could make for himself, on the measure we have given, would not be very large, and would not — even today — prejudice the rest of mankind, or give them any reason to complain or think themselves injured by this man's encroachment, though the human race has now spread to every corner of the world and vastly exceeds the small number there was at the beginning. Indeed, the mere extent of ground is of so little value without labor that I have heard it claimed that even in Spain a man may be permitted to plow, sow, and reap, undisturbed, on land to which he has no title other than his making use of it — and that, on the contrary, the inhabitants consider themselves indebted to the man who, by his industry on neglected and therefore waste land, has increased the stock of grain they lacked. But whether or not that is so — and I put no weight on it — this much I dare boldly affirm: the same rule of property, namely that every man should have as much as he can make use of, would still hold in the world without cramping anybody, since there is enough land in the world to suffice twice its inhabitants — had not the invention of money, and men's tacit agreement to put a value on it, introduced (by consent) larger possessions, and a right to them. How money did this I shall show at greater length shortly.

### § 37

This much is certain: in the beginning — before the desire to have more than a man needed had altered the intrinsic value of things, which depends only on their usefulness to human life; before men had agreed that a little piece of yellow metal, which would keep without wasting or decaying, should be worth a great piece of meat or a whole heap of grain — although men had a right to appropriate by their labor, each for himself, as much of the things of nature as he could use, this could not amount to much, nor be to the prejudice of others, since the same plenty was still left for anyone willing to apply the same industry. Let me add this: the man who appropriates land to himself by his labor does not lessen but *increases* the common stock of mankind. The provisions supporting human life produced by one acre of enclosed and cultivated land are — to put it very conservatively — ten times more than what an acre of equally rich land yields lying waste in common. So the man who encloses land, and gets a greater plenty of the conveniences of life from ten acres than he could get from a hundred left to nature, may truly be said to give ninety acres to mankind: his labor now supplies him from ten acres with provisions that would have been the product of a hundred lying in common. And here I have rated the improved land very low, putting its product at only ten to one, when it is really much nearer a hundred to one. For I ask: in the wild woods and uncultivated waste of America, left to nature without any improvement, tillage, or husbandry, do a thousand acres yield their needy and wretched inhabitants as many conveniences of life as ten acres of equally fertile land do in Devonshire, where they are well cultivated?

Before the appropriation of land, whoever gathered as much of the wild fruit, or killed, caught, or tamed as many of the beasts, as he could — whoever spent his pains on any of the spontaneous products of nature so as to alter them in any way from the state nature put them in, by placing any of his labor on them — thereby acquired a property in them. But if they perished in his possession without being put to their proper use — if the fruits rotted or the venison putrefied before he could consume it — he offended against the common law of nature and was liable to be punished. He had invaded his neighbor's share; for he had no right to any of these things beyond what his use called for, and beyond what could serve to afford him the conveniences of life.

### § 38

The same measures governed the possession of land too. Whatever a man tilled and reaped, stored up and made use of before it spoiled — that was his particular right. Whatever he enclosed and could feed and use, the livestock and the produce were also his. But if the grass of his enclosure rotted on the ground, or the fruit of his planting perished without being gathered and stored, then this part of the earth, despite his enclosure, was still to be regarded as waste, and might become the possession of anyone else. Thus, at the beginning, Cain could take as much ground as he could till and make it his own land, and still leave enough for Abel's sheep to feed on; a few acres would serve for both their possessions. But as families increased and industry enlarged their stocks, their possessions grew with their need of them — yet ordinarily without any fixed property in the ground they used, until they incorporated, settled down together, and built cities. Then, by consent, they came in time to set out the bounds of their distinct territories and agree on limits between themselves and their neighbors, and by laws among themselves they settled the properties of members of the same society. For we see that in the part of the world that was first inhabited, and therefore likely to be best peopled, even as late as Abraham's time, men wandered freely up and down with their flocks and herds, which were their substance — and Abraham did this in a country where he was a stranger. From this it is plain that at least a great part of the land lay in common; that the inhabitants did not value it, nor claimed property in any more of it than they made use of. But when there was not room enough in one place for their herds to feed together, they separated by consent, as Abraham and Lot did (Gen. 13:5), and enlarged their pasture wherever it suited them best. And for the same reason Esau left his father and his brother and settled in Mount Seir (Gen. 36:6).

### § 39

And so, without supposing any private dominion and property in Adam over the whole world, excluding all other men — which can in no way be proved, and from which no one's property can be derived — but supposing instead that the world was given, as it in fact was, to the children of men in common, we see how labor could give men distinct titles to separate parcels of it for their private use, in a way that left no doubt about right and no room for quarrel.

### § 40

Nor is it as strange as it may seem before you think about it, that the property created by labor should be able to outweigh the community of land. For it is labor, in fact, that puts the difference of value on everything. Let anyone consider the difference between an acre of land planted with tobacco or sugar, or sown with wheat or barley, and an acre of the same land lying in common with no husbandry on it, and he will find that the improvement of labor makes up by far the greater part of the value. I think it a very modest calculation to say that of the products of the earth useful to human life, nine-tenths are the effects of labor. Indeed, if we rightly estimate things as they come to our use, and add up the various expenses involved in them — what in them is purely owing to nature, and what to labor — we will find that in most of them ninety-nine hundredths must be set down entirely to the account of labor.

### § 41

There cannot be a clearer demonstration of this than several nations of the Americans, who are rich in land and poor in all the comforts of life. Nature has furnished them as generously as any other people with the materials of plenty — a fruitful soil, apt to produce in abundance whatever could serve for food, clothing, and delight — yet for want of improving it by labor, they have not one hundredth part of the conveniences we enjoy. A king of a large and fruitful territory there feeds, lodges, and is clothed worse than a day-laborer in England.

### § 42

To make this a little clearer, let us just trace some of the ordinary provisions of life through the several stages they pass through before they come to our use, and see how much of their value they receive from human industry. Bread, wine, and cloth are things of daily use and great plenty; yet acorns, water, and leaves or skins would have to be our bread, drink, and clothing, if labor did not furnish us with these more useful commodities. Whatever bread is worth more than acorns, wine more than water, and cloth or silk more than leaves, skins, or moss — all of that is entirely owing to labor and industry. The one set is the food and clothing that unassisted nature provides us; the other, the provisions that our industry and pains prepare for us. Once anyone has calculated how much the latter exceed the former in value, he will see how much labor makes up by far the greatest part of the value of the things we enjoy in this world. The ground that produces the raw materials is scarcely to be counted in at all — or at most as a very small part of it. So small, that even among us, land left wholly to nature, with no improvement of pasturage, tillage, or planting, is called — as indeed it is — *waste*; and we will find its benefit amounts to little more than nothing.

This shows how much numbers of men are to be preferred to largeness of territory, and that the increase of lands and the right employment of them is the great art of government. The prince who is wise and godlike enough to secure, by established laws of liberty, protection and encouragement for the honest industry of mankind, against the oppression of power and the narrowness of faction, will quickly be too strong for his neighbors. But this is by the way.

To return to the argument at hand.

### § 43

An acre of land that yields twenty bushels of wheat here, and another in America which with the same husbandry would do the same, are without doubt of the same natural, intrinsic value. Yet the benefit mankind receives from the one in a year is worth five pounds, and from the other possibly not worth a penny — if all the profit an Indian received from it were valued and sold here, then truly, I may say, not one thousandth as much. It is labor, then, that puts the greatest part of the value on land; without labor it would scarcely be worth anything. It is to labor that we owe the greatest part of all the land's useful products. Everything by which the straw, bran, and bread of that acre of wheat is worth more than the product of an acre of equally good land lying waste is entirely the effect of labor. And it is not merely the plowman's pains, the reaper's and thresher's toil, and the baker's sweat that must be counted into the bread we eat. The labor of those who broke the oxen, who dug and worked the iron and stones, who felled and framed the timber used in the plow, the mill, the oven, and all the other implements — a vast number of them — needed to bring this grain from seed sown to bread made, must all be charged to the account of labor and received as its effect. Nature and the earth furnished only the almost worthless materials as they are in themselves. It would be a strange catalogue of things that industry provided and made use of for every loaf of bread before it came to our use, if we could trace them: iron, wood, leather, bark, timber, stone, bricks, coal, lime, cloth, dyeing drugs, pitch, tar, masts, ropes, and all the materials used in the ship that brought any of the commodities used by any of the workmen to any part of the work — all of which it would be almost impossible, or at least far too long, to reckon up.

### § 44

From all this it is evident that although the things of nature are given in common, man — by being master of himself, and proprietor of his own person and of the actions or labor of it — still had in himself the great foundation of property. And once invention and the arts had improved the conveniences of life, whatever made up the greater part of what he applied to the support or comfort of his existence was perfectly his own, and did not belong in common to others.

### § 45

Thus labor, in the beginning, gave a right of property wherever anyone chose to employ it on what was common — and the common remained for a long while by far the greater part of the world, and is even now more than mankind makes use of. At first men mostly contented themselves with what unassisted nature offered for their necessities. Later, in some parts of the world — where the increase of people and livestock, along with the use of money, had made land scarce and therefore of some value — the various communities settled the bounds of their distinct territories, and by laws within themselves regulated the properties of the private men of their society, and so by compact and agreement settled the property that labor and industry had begun. And the leagues made between various states and kingdoms — expressly or tacitly disowning all claim and right to the land in one another's possession — have by common consent given up their pretensions to their natural common right, which they originally had to those countries, and so have by positive agreement settled property among themselves in distinct parts and parcels of the earth. Yet there are still great tracts of ground to be found which lie waste — their inhabitants not having joined the rest of mankind in consenting to the use of their common money — and which are more than the people who dwell on them do or can make use of, and so still lie in common; though this can scarcely happen among that part of mankind that has consented to the use of money.

### § 46

The greatest part of the things really useful to human life — the things the necessity of subsisting made the first commoners of the world seek after, as it makes the Americans seek them now — are generally things of short duration: things that, if not consumed by use, will decay and perish of themselves. Gold, silver, and diamonds are things that fancy or agreement has put the value on, more than any real use or necessary support of life. Now of the good things nature has provided in common, everyone had a right (as I have said) to as much as he could use, and a property in everything he could work on with his labor: everything his industry could extend to, so as to alter it from the state nature had put it in, was his. Whoever gathered a hundred bushels of acorns or apples thereby had a property in them; they were his goods as soon as gathered. He only had to see to it that he used them before they spoiled; otherwise he took more than his share and robbed others. And indeed it was a foolish thing, as well as a dishonest one, to hoard up more than he could make use of. If he gave part away to somebody else, so that it did not perish uselessly in his possession, that too counted as making use of it. And if he bartered away plums that would have rotted in a week for nuts that would keep good for his eating a whole year, he did no injury: he wasted nothing of the common stock, destroyed no part of the portion of goods that belonged to others, so long as nothing perished uselessly in his hands. Again, if he traded his nuts for a piece of metal whose color pleased him, or exchanged his sheep for shells, or his wool for a sparkling pebble or a diamond, and kept those by him all his life, he invaded no one's rights. He might pile up as much of these durable things as he pleased; for exceeding the bounds of his rightful property does not lie in the largeness of his possessions, but in anything perishing uselessly in them.

### § 47

And this is how the use of money came in: some lasting thing that men could keep without its spoiling, and that by mutual consent men would take in exchange for the truly useful but perishable supports of life.

### § 48

And as different degrees of industry tended to give men possessions in different proportions, so this invention of money gave them the opportunity to continue and enlarge those possessions. Imagine an island, cut off from all possible commerce with the rest of the world, with only a hundred families on it, but with sheep, horses, and cows, and other useful animals, wholesome fruits, and land enough for grain for a hundred thousand times as many — yet with nothing on the island, whether because it was too common or too perishable, fit to serve in place of money. What reason could anyone there have to enlarge his possessions beyond the use of his family and a plentiful supply for its consumption, whether from what their own industry produced or from what they could barter for other perishable, useful commodities? Where there is not something both lasting and scarce, and valuable enough to be hoarded up, men will not be inclined to enlarge their possessions of land, however rich the land, however free they are to take it. For I ask: what would a man value ten thousand or a hundred thousand acres of excellent land, ready cultivated and well stocked with cattle too, in the middle of the inland parts of America, where he had no hope of commerce with other parts of the world, to draw money to him by the sale of the produce? It would not be worth enclosing, and we would see him give back to the wild common of nature whatever exceeded what would supply the conveniences of life for himself and his family there.

### § 49

Thus in the beginning all the world was America — and more so than America is now, for no such thing as money was known anywhere. Find something that has the use and value of money among a man's neighbors, and you will see that same man promptly begin to enlarge his possessions.

### § 50

But since gold and silver, being of little use to human life compared with food, clothing, and transport, have their value only from the consent of men — of which value labor still, in great part, makes the measure — it is plain that men have *agreed* to a disproportionate and unequal possession of the earth. By a tacit and voluntary consent they have found a way in which a man may fairly possess more land than he himself can use the product of, by receiving gold and silver in exchange for the surplus — metals that may be hoarded up without injury to anyone, since they neither spoil nor decay in the possessor's hands. This division of things into an inequality of private possessions men have made practicable outside the bounds of society, and without any compact, simply by putting a value on gold and silver and tacitly agreeing to the use of money. For within governments, the laws regulate the right of property, and the possession of land is determined by positive constitutions.

### § 51

And thus, I think, it is very easy to conceive, without any difficulty, how labor could at first begin a title of property in the common things of nature, and how the spending of them on our own uses set the bound to it. There could then be no reason for quarreling about title, and no doubt about the size of possession that title gave. Right and convenience went together: since a man had a right to everything he could employ his labor on, he had no temptation to labor for more than he could make use of. This left no room for controversy about title, and no room for encroachment on the rights of others. What portion a man carved out for himself was easily seen; and it was useless, as well as dishonest, to carve out too much, or to take more than he needed.
