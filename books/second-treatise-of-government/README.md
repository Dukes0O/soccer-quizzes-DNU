# Second Treatise of Government — Modern English Edition

**John Locke, 1690** · Rendered into modern English, 2026

A chapter-by-chapter modern-English rendering of Locke's *Second Treatise of
Government* ("An Essay Concerning the True Original, Extent and End of Civil
Government"), produced for readers working through the Great Books of Western
Thought. This is a translation, not an abridgment: every numbered section
(§ 1–243) is rendered in full, with Locke's section numbers preserved so you
can cite and cross-reference against any scholarly edition.

Each chapter file contains:

- **Overview** — what the chapter argues and where it sits in the treatise
- **Key Concepts** — the primary ideas the chapter elucidates
- **Archaic Terms** — a glossary of words that have fallen out of use or
  shifted meaning since 1690
- **Modern Translation** — the complete text of the chapter, section by
  section, in contemporary English

## Why this book matters

Written in defense of the Glorious Revolution of 1688, the *Second Treatise*
is the founding document of liberal political philosophy. Locke argues that
legitimate government arises only from the consent of the governed, exists
only to protect life, liberty, and property, and may be dissolved by the
people when it betrays that trust. Its fingerprints are all over the American
Declaration of Independence and Constitution, and it is the standard point of
contrast with Hobbes's *Leviathan* and a key target for Rousseau, Hume, and
Marx — rich ground for cross-connections in a Great Books reading plan.

## The argument in one paragraph

Human beings are born free and equal in a state of nature governed by a law
of nature (chs. I–II). Because enforcing that law ourselves leads to war
(ch. III), and because we acquire property by our labor that needs protecting
(ch. V), we consent to form political societies (chs. VII–VIII) whose sole
purpose is the preservation of our lives, liberties, and estates (ch. IX).
Government's powers are therefore limited and held in trust (chs. XI–XIV);
absolute or arbitrary power is never legitimate (chs. IV, VI, XV–XVII); a
ruler who governs outside the law becomes a tyrant (ch. XVIII); and when the
trust is broken, power reverts to the people, who may establish a new
government (ch. XIX).

## Translation notes

The rendering modernizes syntax, sentence structure, and vocabulary while
preserving Locke's meaning, section numbering, first-person voice, and his
quotations of Richard Hooker and Scripture (themselves modernized, with
citations kept). Technical terms that remain standard in political philosophy
— *state of nature*, *law of nature*, *consent*, *prerogative*,
*commonwealth*, *legislative power* — are retained. When in doubt, consult
the original text in `source/7370.txt` by section number.
