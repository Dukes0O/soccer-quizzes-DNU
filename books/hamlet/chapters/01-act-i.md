# Act I

> William Shakespeare, *Hamlet, Prince of Denmark* (c. 1601) · Act I, Scenes 1–5
>
> [Book index](../README.md) · [Act II →](02-act-ii.md)

## Overview

Act I sets the whole machine of the tragedy in motion. On the freezing battlements of Elsinore, nervous sentries have twice seen a ghost that looks exactly like the recently dead King Hamlet, and they bring the skeptical scholar Horatio to verify it. The apparition appears, armed head to foot, but refuses to speak. Meanwhile Denmark is arming for war: young Fortinbras of Norway is raising troops to win back the lands his father lost to old Hamlet in single combat. The dead king's image stalking the night, and the state's feverish war preparations, together announce that something is deeply wrong in Denmark.

Inside the castle, the new king, Claudius — the dead king's brother — conducts business with smooth efficiency: he dispatches ambassadors to Norway, grants Laertes leave to return to France, and publicly justifies his hasty marriage to his brother's widow, Gertrude. Only Prince Hamlet stands apart, still in mourning black, sick with disgust that his mother remarried within a month of the funeral — and to her husband's brother. Left alone, he pours out the play's first great soliloquy: he wishes he could simply dissolve out of existence, but religion forbids suicide, so he must hold his tongue. Then Horatio brings him the news of the ghost, and Hamlet resolves to stand watch himself.

A domestic interlude in Polonius's house introduces the play's second family. Laertes and then Polonius both warn Ophelia away from Hamlet's courtship — a prince cannot choose for himself, they argue, and her honor is at stake — and Polonius flatly orders her to stop seeing him. Ophelia obeys. The scene plants the surveillance, the paternal control, and the poisoned love affair that will bear bitter fruit later.

On the platform that night, the ghost appears to Hamlet and beckons him away alone. In the act's climax it speaks at last: it is his father's spirit, walking by night and burning by day for its unpurged sins, and it charges Hamlet with revenge. Claudius, it reveals, murdered him — poured poison in his ear as he slept in his orchard — stealing his life, his crown, and his queen in one stroke, and sending him to judgment with all his sins on his head. Hamlet swears to remember, forces his companions to secrecy on his sword (the ghost crying "Swear!" from beneath the stage), and warns them he may put on an "antic disposition" — an act of madness. The act ends with his groan: the time is out of joint, and he was born to set it right. Every central question of the play — is the ghost honest? can revenge be righteous? where does acting end and being begin? — is now on the table.

## Key Concepts

- **The ghost's ambiguity** — Is it a "spirit of health or goblin damn'd"? Horatio approaches it as a scholar, Hamlet interrogates it as a son, and even after it speaks, its claim to come from purgatorial fires leaves open the possibility of a devil baiting a trap. The act establishes doubt about the ghost as the engine of everything that follows.
- **Revenge and its cost** — The ghost's command is absolute ("Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder") but comes with conditions: don't corrupt your own mind, and leave your mother to heaven. The impossibility of clean revenge is built into the assignment from the start.
- **Seeming vs. being** — Hamlet's first extended speech ("Seems, madam! Nay, it is; I know not seems") distinguishes the outward show of grief from the real thing; the ghost reveals Claudius as a "seeming-virtuous" fraud; and Hamlet writes in his notebook that "one may smile, and smile, and be a villain." Appearance versus reality is the act's obsessive theme.
- **Surveillance and control** — Denmark is a watched state: sentries on the walls, Polonius interrogating Ophelia about her private life, everyone reporting on everyone. The act builds the atmosphere of a court where nothing goes unobserved.
- **Corruption spreading from the head** — From Francisco's "sick at heart," through Hamlet's "unweeded garden," to Marcellus's "Something is rotten in the state of Denmark," the act develops the idea that the king's secret crime infects the whole nation, like the "vicious mole of nature" Hamlet describes that ruins an otherwise noble man.
- **Feigned madness** — Hamlet's advance warning that he may "put an antic disposition on" plants the play's central puzzle: from this point forward the audience must judge, moment by moment, how much of Hamlet's strangeness is performance.
- **Haste and incest in the royal marriage** — The "wicked speed" of Gertrude's remarriage — funeral leftovers serving the wedding table — is what poisons Hamlet's world even before he hears of murder; the ghost then confirms the marriage as literally adulterous and incestuous by the standards of the play.
- **Youth, desire, and paternal authority** — The Polonius household scenes counterpoint the royal plot: brother and father both treat Ophelia's love as a political and sexual danger to be managed, and both preach a prudence (Laertes's "best safety lies in fear," Polonius's precepts) that the play will test to destruction.

## Modern Translation

### Act I, Scene 1

> *Scene: Elsinore. A platform before the Castle.*

*[Francisco at his post. Enter to him Bernardo.]*

**BERNARDO:** Who's there?

**FRANCISCO:** No — you answer me. Stop and identify yourself.

**BERNARDO:** Long live the king!

**FRANCISCO:** Bernardo?

**BERNARDO:** That's me.

**FRANCISCO:** You've come right on your hour, very punctual.

**BERNARDO:** It just struck twelve. Get to bed, Francisco.

**FRANCISCO:** Thanks very much for relieving me. It's bitterly cold, and I'm sick at heart.

**BERNARDO:** Has your watch been quiet?

**FRANCISCO:** Not a mouse stirring.

**BERNARDO:** Well, good night. If you run into Horatio and Marcellus, my partners on the watch, tell them to hurry.

**FRANCISCO:** I think I hear them now. — Halt! Who's there?

*[Enter Horatio and Marcellus.]*

**HORATIO:** Friends of this country.

**MARCELLUS:** And loyal subjects of the Danish king.

**FRANCISCO:** Good night to you.

**MARCELLUS:** Oh, farewell, honest soldier. Who relieved you?

**FRANCISCO:** Bernardo has taken my place. Good night.

*[Exit.]*

**MARCELLUS:** Hello! Bernardo!

**BERNARDO:** Speak up — is that Horatio with you?

**HORATIO:** A piece of him.

**BERNARDO:** Welcome, Horatio. Welcome, good Marcellus.

**MARCELLUS:** Well — has that thing appeared again tonight?

**BERNARDO:** I've seen nothing.

**MARCELLUS:** Horatio says it's just our imagination, and he won't let himself believe a word about this dreadful sight we've now seen twice. That's why I've begged him to come stand watch with us through the night — so that if the apparition comes again, he can confirm what our eyes saw, and speak to it.

**HORATIO:** Nonsense, nonsense — it won't appear.

**BERNARDO:** Sit down a while, and let us assault your ears one more time — fortified as they are against our story — with what we've seen these past two nights.

**HORATIO:** Fine, let's sit down, and let's hear Bernardo tell it.

**BERNARDO:** Last night, just when that same star west of the pole had traced its course to light that very part of the sky where it's burning now, Marcellus and I, the bell just striking one—

**MARCELLUS:** Quiet, stop — look, here it comes again!

*[Enter Ghost, armed.]*

**BERNARDO:** In the same shape — just like the dead king.

**MARCELLUS:** You're the scholar — speak to it, Horatio.

**BERNARDO:** Doesn't it look like the King? Look hard at it, Horatio.

**HORATIO:** Exactly like him. It rakes me with fear and wonder.

**BERNARDO:** It wants to be spoken to.

**MARCELLUS:** Question it, Horatio.

**HORATIO:** What are you, that you seize this hour of the night, along with that noble, warlike form the buried majesty of Denmark once marched in? By heaven, I order you — speak!

**MARCELLUS:** It's offended.

**BERNARDO:** Look, it's stalking away!

**HORATIO:** Stay! Speak, speak! I command you, speak!

*[Exit Ghost.]*

**MARCELLUS:** It's gone, and it won't answer.

**BERNARDO:** Well now, Horatio! You're trembling and pale. Isn't this something more than imagination? What do you say now?

**HORATIO:** Before God, I would never have believed this without the direct, undeniable testimony of my own eyes.

**MARCELLUS:** Isn't it like the King?

**HORATIO:** As you are like yourself. That was the very armor he wore when he fought the ambitious king of Norway. That's exactly how he frowned once when, in a furious parley, he smashed the Poles in their sledges on the ice. It's strange.

**MARCELLUS:** Twice before, and precisely at this dead hour, he has marched past our watch with that same military stride.

**HORATIO:** What exact conclusion to draw, I don't know; but my overall opinion is that this means some strange upheaval is coming for our state.

**MARCELLUS:** All right then, sit down, and let anyone who knows tell me: why does this strict, hyper-alert watch wear out the subjects of this land night after night? Why the daily casting of bronze cannon, and the buying of weapons abroad? Why the conscription of shipwrights, whose grinding labor doesn't even pause for Sundays? What can be coming, that this sweating rush makes night a co-worker with the day? Who can explain it to me?

**HORATIO:** I can — at least, this is the rumor. Our late king, whose image appeared to us just now, was, as you know, challenged to single combat by Fortinbras of Norway, who was goaded to it by jealous pride. In that fight our valiant Hamlet — for that's how this whole side of the known world rated him — killed this Fortinbras; who, by a sealed contract, fully ratified by law and the code of heraldry, forfeited with his life all the lands he possessed to his conqueror. Our king had staked an equivalent portion, which would have passed to Fortinbras's inheritance had he won — just as, by the same agreement and the terms of the article drawn up, Fortinbras's lands fell to Hamlet. Now, sir: young Fortinbras, hot and full of untested mettle, has scraped together, here and there along the fringes of Norway, a gang of lawless desperadoes — paid in food and board — for some enterprise that takes real stomach. And that enterprise, as our government sees perfectly well, is nothing other than to recover from us, by force and extorted terms, those lands his father lost. This, I take it, is the main reason for our preparations, the source of this watch of ours, and the chief cause of all this frantic haste and commotion in the land.

**BERNARDO:** I think that's exactly what it is. And it fits, then, that this ominous figure comes armed through our watch, looking so like the king who was and still is the cause of these wars.

**HORATIO:** It's a speck of dust to vex the mind's eye. In the high and flourishing days of Rome, a little before mighty Julius Caesar fell, the graves stood empty and the dead in their shrouds squeaked and gibbered in the Roman streets; there were stars with trails of fire, dews of blood, ominous signs in the sun; and the moist star that governs Neptune's ocean empire was sick almost to doomsday with eclipse. And just such forerunners of terrible events — the advance messengers that always ride ahead of the fates, the prologue to the disaster coming on — heaven and earth together have shown to our own country and countrymen. — But wait, look! There — it's coming again!

*[Re-enter Ghost.]*

I'll cross its path, even if it destroys me. — Stop, illusion! If you have any voice, any power of speech, speak to me. If there's any good deed to be done that would give you ease and bring grace to me, speak to me. If you have secret knowledge of your country's fate — which, if we knew it in advance, we might avoid — oh, speak! Or if in your lifetime you hoarded extorted treasure in the womb of the earth — which, they say, is why you spirits often walk after death —

*[The cock crows.]*

speak of it! Stay, and speak! — Stop it, Marcellus!

**MARCELLUS:** Shall I strike at it with my spear?

**HORATIO:** Do it, if it won't stand still.

**BERNARDO:** It's here!

**HORATIO:** It's here!

**MARCELLUS:** It's gone!

*[Exit Ghost.]*

We wrong it — something so majestic — by offering it a show of violence. It's invulnerable, like the air, and our useless blows are just spiteful mockery.

**BERNARDO:** It was about to speak when the cock crowed.

**HORATIO:** And then it flinched, like a guilty thing at a terrifying summons. I've heard that the cock, trumpeter of the morning, wakes the god of day with that high, shrill throat of his; and at his warning every wandering, out-of-bounds spirit — whether in sea or fire, earth or air — hurries back to its confinement. What we just saw proves the truth of it.

**MARCELLUS:** It faded at the crowing of the cock. Some people say that always, around the season when our Savior's birth is celebrated, this bird of dawn sings all night long; and then, they say, no spirit dares to stir abroad; the nights are wholesome; then no planets strike men with disaster, no fairy bewitches, no witch has power to charm — that's how holy and full of grace that time is.

**HORATIO:** So I've heard, and I partly believe it. But look — the morning, dressed in a russet cloak, is walking over the dew of that high hill to the east. Let's end our watch; and my advice is that we tell young Hamlet what we've seen tonight. Because on my life, this spirit that's dumb to us will speak to him. Do you agree we should let him know about it — as our love requires and our duty demands?

**MARCELLUS:** Let's do it, please. And I know where we'll find him most conveniently this morning.

*[Exeunt.]*

### Act I, Scene 2

> *Scene: Elsinore. A room of state in the Castle.*

*[Enter the King, Queen, Hamlet, Polonius, Laertes, Voltimand, Cornelius, Lords, and Attendants.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Though the memory of our dear brother Hamlet's death is still fresh, and though it was proper for us to carry grief in our hearts and for our whole kingdom to contract into one furrowed brow of mourning — still, judgment has fought so far against feeling that we now think of him with the wisest sorrow, while also remembering ourselves. Therefore our former sister, now our queen, imperial co-heir of this warlike state, we have taken as wife — with what you might call a defeated joy: one eye bright and one eye dropping tears, cheer at the funeral and a dirge at the wedding, weighing delight and grief in equal measure. Nor have we shut out your better judgment in this — you freely endorsed the whole affair. For all of it, our thanks. Now to what you already know: young Fortinbras, holding a low estimate of our strength, or thinking that our late dear brother's death has left our state dislocated and out of joint, and feeding on this dream of his advantage, hasn't stopped pestering us with messages demanding the surrender of those lands his father lost — with full legal force — to our most valiant brother. So much for him. Now for ourselves and for this meeting's business, it is this: we have written to Norway, young Fortinbras's uncle — who, feeble and bedridden, has scarcely even heard of his nephew's plans — telling him to suppress any further steps in the matter, since the recruiting, the musters, and the full supplies are all drawn from his own subjects. And so we dispatch you, good Cornelius, and you, Voltimand, to carry this greeting to old Norway — granting you no more personal authority to negotiate with the king than the scope of these detailed instructions allows. Farewell — and let your speed prove your devotion.

**CORNELIUS and VOLTIMAND:** In that, as in all things, we will show our duty.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** We don't doubt it in the least. A hearty farewell.

*[Exeunt Voltimand and Cornelius.]*

And now, Laertes, what's your news? You spoke of some request — what is it, Laertes? You can't ask anything reasonable of the King of Denmark and waste your breath. What could you beg for, Laertes, that wouldn't be my offer before it was your asking? The head is not more naturally joined to the heart, the hand not more useful to the mouth, than the throne of Denmark is to your father. What would you have, Laertes?

**LAERTES:** My dread lord, your leave and blessing to return to France. I came from there to Denmark willingly, to show my duty at your coronation; but now, I confess, that duty done, my thoughts and wishes bend back toward France, and bow before your gracious leave and pardon.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Do you have your father's permission? What does Polonius say?

**POLONIUS:** He has, my lord, wrung my reluctant consent out of me by sheer relentless petitioning, and at last I stamped my grudging seal on his wishes. I do beg you — give him leave to go.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Seize your best hour, Laertes. Your time is your own — spend it however your finest qualities direct! — But now, my cousin Hamlet, and my son—

**HAMLET:** *[Aside.]* A little more than kin, and less than kind!

**KING CLAUDIUS:** How is it the clouds still hang over you?

**HAMLET:** Not at all, my lord — I'm too much in the sun.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Good Hamlet, throw off that black mourning color, and let your eye look on the King of Denmark like a friend. Don't keep your lids lowered forever, searching for your noble father in the dust. You know it's common: everything that lives must die, passing through nature into eternity.

**HAMLET:** Yes, madam — it is common.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** If it is, why does it seem so personal to you?

**HAMLET:** Seem, madam? No — it *is*. I don't know "seem." It's not just my inky cloak, good mother, nor the customary suits of solemn black, nor gusty sighs of forced breath — no, nor the flowing river in the eye, nor the dejected look on the face, nor all the forms, moods, and displays of grief put together, that can truthfully describe me. Those things really do *seem*, because they're actions a man could perform. But I have something inside me that's beyond showing; all the rest is just the costume and trappings of woe.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** It's sweet and commendable in your nature, Hamlet, to pay these mourning duties to your father. But you must understand: your father lost a father; that lost father lost his; and each survivor is bound by filial duty to do proper funeral mourning — for a while. But to persist in stubborn grieving is a course of irreverent obstinacy. It's unmanly grief. It shows a will in open defiance of heaven, an unfortified heart, an impatient mind, an understanding simple and untrained. For what we know must happen — what is as common as the most ordinary fact of the senses — why should we take it to heart in peevish resistance? Come! It's an offense against heaven, an offense against the dead, an offense against nature, and utterly absurd to reason, whose constant theme is the death of fathers, and which has always cried — from the first corpse to the man who died today — "This must be so." We beg you, throw this useless grief to the ground, and think of us as a father. For let the world take note: you are next in line to our throne, and I feel toward you a love no less noble than that which the dearest father bears his son. As for your intention to go back to school in Wittenberg — it runs completely against our wishes. We beg you: agree to stay here, in the cheer and comfort of our eye, our foremost courtier, our cousin, and our son.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Don't let your mother's prayers be wasted, Hamlet. I beg you, stay with us; don't go to Wittenberg.

**HAMLET:** I shall obey you as best I can, madam.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Why, that's a loving and handsome answer. Be as ourself in Denmark. — Madam, come. This gentle, unforced consent of Hamlet's sits smiling on my heart; and in honor of it, no cheerful toast the King of Denmark drinks today but the great cannon will proclaim it to the clouds, and heaven will echo the king's carousing, re-sounding earthly thunder. Come away.

*[Exeunt all but Hamlet.]*

**HAMLET:** Oh, if only this too, too solid flesh would melt, thaw, and dissolve itself into a dew! Or if only the Everlasting hadn't fixed his law against self-slaughter! O God! God! How weary, stale, flat, and unprofitable all the uses of this world seem to me! Damn it — oh, damn it! It's an unweeded garden gone to seed; things rank and gross in nature possess it utterly. That it should come to this! Only two months dead — no, not even that, not two. So excellent a king — who was, next to this one, Hyperion next to a satyr. So loving to my mother that he wouldn't let the winds of heaven touch her face too roughly. Heaven and earth! Must I remember? Why, she would hang on him as if her appetite grew by what it fed on. And yet, within a month — don't let me think about it — Frailty, thy name is woman! — a little month, before the shoes were even old in which she followed my poor father's body, like Niobe, all tears — why she, even she — O God! An animal without the power of reason would have mourned longer! — married my uncle, my father's brother, but no more like my father than I am like Hercules. Within a month! Before the salt of her utterly false tears had stopped stinging her rubbed-raw eyes, she married. Oh, most wicked speed — to rush with such agility into incestuous sheets! It is not good, and it cannot come to good. But break, my heart — for I must hold my tongue!

*[Enter Horatio, Marcellus, and Bernardo.]*

**HORATIO:** Hail to your lordship!

**HAMLET:** I'm glad to see you well — Horatio, or I've forgotten my own name.

**HORATIO:** The very same, my lord, and your poor servant always.

**HAMLET:** Sir, my good *friend* — I'll trade that title with you. What brings you away from Wittenberg, Horatio? — Marcellus?

**MARCELLUS:** My good lord—

**HAMLET:** I'm very glad to see you. — Good evening, sir. — But seriously, what brings you from Wittenberg?

**HORATIO:** A truant streak, my good lord.

**HAMLET:** I wouldn't let your enemy say that, and you're not going to do violence to my ear by making it believe your own testimony against yourself. I know you're no truant. But what's your business in Elsinore? We'll teach you some deep drinking before you leave.

**HORATIO:** My lord, I came to see your father's funeral.

**HAMLET:** Please don't mock me, fellow student. I think you came to see my mother's wedding.

**HORATIO:** Indeed, my lord, it did follow quickly.

**HAMLET:** Thrift, thrift, Horatio! The leftover funeral meats furnished the marriage tables cold. I'd rather have met my worst enemy in heaven than ever have seen that day, Horatio! My father — I think I see my father.

**HORATIO:** Where, my lord?

**HAMLET:** In my mind's eye, Horatio.

**HORATIO:** I saw him once. He was a fine king.

**HAMLET:** He was a man — take him all in all, I shall never look on his like again.

**HORATIO:** My lord, I think I saw him last night.

**HAMLET:** Saw? Who?

**HORATIO:** My lord — the king your father.

**HAMLET:** The king my father!

**HORATIO:** Hold your astonishment in check for a moment and listen carefully, until I can deliver this marvel to you — with these gentlemen as my witnesses.

**HAMLET:** For God's love, let me hear it.

**HORATIO:** Two nights in a row these gentlemen, Marcellus and Bernardo, on their watch, in the dead emptiness of the middle of the night, met with this: a figure like your father, armed precisely at every point, head to foot, appears before them, and with a solemn march goes slowly and grandly past. Three times he walked past their oppressed, fear-stricken eyes, within the length of his commander's staff, while they — melted almost to jelly by the act of fear — stood dumb and didn't speak to him. They told me this in dreadful secrecy, and on the third night I kept the watch with them; and there, exactly as they had reported — right on time, in the very form of the thing, every word proved true — the apparition comes. I knew your father. These two hands are not more alike.

**HAMLET:** But where was this?

**MARCELLUS:** My lord, on the platform where we stood watch.

**HAMLET:** Didn't you speak to it?

**HORATIO:** My lord, I did — but it made no answer. Yet once, I thought, it lifted its head and moved as though it were about to speak. But just then the morning cock crowed loud, and at the sound it shrank hurriedly away and vanished from our sight.

**HAMLET:** This is very strange.

**HORATIO:** As I live, my honored lord, it's true; and we felt it was written into our duty to let you know.

**HAMLET:** Indeed, indeed, sirs — but this troubles me. Are you on watch tonight?

**MARCELLUS and BERNARDO:** We are, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Armed, you say?

**BOTH:** Armed, my lord.

**HAMLET:** From top to toe?

**BOTH:** My lord, from head to foot.

**HAMLET:** Then you didn't see his face?

**HORATIO:** Oh yes, my lord — he wore his visor up.

**HAMLET:** What — did he look angry?

**HORATIO:** An expression more of sorrow than of anger.

**HAMLET:** Pale, or flushed?

**HORATIO:** No — very pale.

**HAMLET:** And he fixed his eyes on you?

**HORATIO:** Constantly.

**HAMLET:** I wish I had been there.

**HORATIO:** It would have astonished you.

**HAMLET:** Very likely, very likely. Did it stay long?

**HORATIO:** About as long as it would take to count to a hundred at a moderate pace.

**MARCELLUS and BERNARDO:** Longer, longer.

**HORATIO:** Not the time I saw it.

**HAMLET:** His beard was grizzled — no?

**HORATIO:** It was as I'd seen it in his life: black streaked with silver.

**HAMLET:** I'll stand watch tonight. Perhaps it will walk again.

**HORATIO:** I guarantee it will.

**HAMLET:** If it takes on my noble father's form, I'll speak to it, even if hell itself gapes open and orders me to be silent. I ask you all: if you've kept this sight concealed so far, keep it locked in your silence still; and whatever else happens tonight, take it in with your understanding but give it no tongue. I will repay your love. So farewell — I'll visit you on the platform between eleven and twelve.

**ALL:** Our duty to your honor.

**HAMLET:** Your *love*, as mine is to you. Farewell.

*[Exeunt Horatio, Marcellus, and Bernardo.]*

My father's spirit — in arms! All is not well. I suspect some foul play. I wish the night were already here! Until then, sit still, my soul. Foul deeds will rise to men's eyes, though all the earth were piled on top of them.

*[Exit.]*

### Act I, Scene 3

> *Scene: A room in Polonius's house.*

*[Enter Laertes and Ophelia.]*

**LAERTES:** My baggage is on board. Farewell. And, sister, whenever the winds are favorable and a ship is available, don't sleep on it — let me hear from you.

**OPHELIA:** Do you doubt that?

**LAERTES:** As for Hamlet and the trifle of his attention — treat it as a fashion, a toy of youthful blood; a violet in the springtime of young nature, early but not permanent, sweet but not lasting, the perfume and pastime of a minute. No more.

**OPHELIA:** No more than that?

**LAERTES:** Think of it as no more. For a growing nature doesn't develop only in muscle and bulk; as this temple of the body enlarges, the inner work of mind and soul grows wider along with it. Perhaps he loves you now, and right now no stain or trickery smudges the virtue of his intentions. But you must be afraid: given his greatness, his will is not his own, for he himself is subject to his birth. He may not, as ordinary people do, carve out his own choices — because on his choice depend the safety and health of this whole state. So his choice must be bounded by the voice and consent of that body of which he is the head. Then, if he says he loves you, it suits your wisdom to believe it only as far as he, in his particular position and rank, can back the saying with the deed — and that is no further than the general voice of Denmark will go along with it. Then weigh what loss your honor might suffer if you listen to his songs with too trusting an ear, or lose your heart, or open your chaste treasure to his uncontrolled insistence. Fear it, Ophelia — fear it, my dear sister — and keep yourself in the rear guard of your affections, out of the range and danger of desire. The most cautious girl is reckless enough if she unveils her beauty even to the moon. Virtue itself doesn't escape the strokes of slander. The cankerworm blights the newborns of spring too often before their buds have even opened, and in the morning's liquid dew of youth, contagious diseases are most threatening. Be wary, then; the best safety lies in fear. Youth rebels against itself, even with no tempter near.

**OPHELIA:** I'll keep the substance of this good lesson as a watchman over my heart. But, my good brother — don't do what some ungracious pastors do: show me the steep and thorny way to heaven, while you yourself, like a puffed-up, reckless libertine, stroll the primrose path of pleasure and ignore your own sermon.

**LAERTES:** Oh, don't worry about me. I've stayed too long — but here comes my father.

*[Enter Polonius.]*

A double blessing is a double grace; luck smiles on a second goodbye.

**POLONIUS:** Still here, Laertes! Aboard, aboard — for shame! The wind is sitting in the shoulder of your sail, and they're waiting for you. There — my blessing on you!

*[Laying his hand on Laertes's head.]*

And see that you engrave these few precepts in your memory. Give your thoughts no tongue, and no ill-proportioned thought any action. Be friendly, but never cheap. Those friends you have, once their loyalty is tested, clamp them to your soul with hoops of steel — but don't callous your palm shaking hands with every newly hatched, unfledged buddy. Beware of getting into a quarrel; but once you're in, carry it so the other man learns to beware of you. Give every man your ear, but few your voice. Take in every man's opinion, but reserve your own judgment. Dress as expensively as your purse can bear, but not gaudily — rich, not flashy — because the clothes often proclaim the man, and the best rank and station in France are the most refined and generous in exactly that. Neither a borrower nor a lender be, for a loan often loses both itself and the friend, and borrowing dulls the edge of thrift. This above all: to thine own self be true — and it must follow, as the night the day, you cannot then be false to any man. Farewell — and may my blessing make all this ripen in you!

**LAERTES:** Most humbly I take my leave, my lord.

**POLONIUS:** Your time is calling — go; your servants are waiting.

**LAERTES:** Farewell, Ophelia — and remember well what I've said to you.

**OPHELIA:** It's locked in my memory, and you yourself shall keep the key to it.

**LAERTES:** Farewell.

*[Exit.]*

**POLONIUS:** What is it, Ophelia, that he said to you?

**OPHELIA:** If you please — something concerning Lord Hamlet.

**POLONIUS:** Ah — good thinking, indeed. I'm told he has very often lately given his private time to you, and that you yourself have been very free and generous with your attention. If that's so — and it's been reported to me that way, as a warning — then I must tell you: you don't understand your own position as clearly as befits my daughter and your honor. What is between you two? Give me the truth.

**OPHELIA:** He has lately, my lord, made me many tenders of his affection.

**POLONIUS:** Affection! Pah! You talk like a green girl, unsifted by dangerous situations like this. Do you believe his "tenders," as you call them?

**OPHELIA:** I don't know, my lord, what I should think.

**POLONIUS:** By Mary, I'll teach you. Think of yourself as a baby, for taking these tenders as real payment when they're not genuine currency. Tender yourself at a higher price — or (not to run this poor pun completely out of breath) you'll tender *me* a fool.

**OPHELIA:** My lord, he has pressed his love on me in an honorable fashion.

**POLONIUS:** Yes — a "fashion" you may well call it. Go on, go on.

**OPHELIA:** And he has backed up his words, my lord, with almost all the holy vows of heaven.

**POLONIUS:** Yes — snares to catch stupid birds. I know how freely the soul lends vows to the tongue when the blood is burning. These flares, daughter, give more light than heat — extinguished in both even as the promise is being made — and you must not mistake them for fire. From now on, ration your maidenly presence more strictly. Set the terms of your company at a higher price than a mere summons to talk. As for Lord Hamlet, believe about him this much: that he is young, and that he's allowed a longer leash than can ever be given to you. In short, Ophelia, don't believe his vows, for they are brokers — not of the color their fine investments suggest, but mere solicitors for unholy propositions, breathing like sanctified and pious pimps the better to deceive. This is final: in plain terms, from this time forward I will not have you disgrace a single moment of your leisure by exchanging words or talk with the Lord Hamlet. See to it — that's an order. Come along.

**OPHELIA:** I shall obey, my lord.

*[Exeunt.]*

### Act I, Scene 4

> *Scene: The platform.*

*[Enter Hamlet, Horatio, and Marcellus.]*

**HAMLET:** The air bites sharply — it's very cold.

**HORATIO:** It's a nipping, biting air.

**HAMLET:** What time is it now?

**HORATIO:** Just short of twelve, I think.

**MARCELLUS:** No, it has struck.

**HORATIO:** Really? I didn't hear it. Then the hour is drawing near when the spirit was in the habit of walking.

*[A flourish of trumpets, and cannon shot off within.]*

What does that mean, my lord?

**HAMLET:** The King is staying up tonight, drinking his fill — holding a carouse, reeling through his swaggering wild dances; and every time he drains his cups of Rhine wine, the kettledrum and trumpet bray out the triumph of his toast like that.

**HORATIO:** Is it a custom?

**HAMLET:** Oh yes, indeed it is. But to my mind — though I'm a native here, and born to the manner — it's a custom more honored in the breach than the observance. This heavy-headed reveling gets us slandered and censured by other nations east and west. They call us drunkards, and soil our reputation with pig-epithets; and truly, it drains from our achievements, however high our performance, the very pith and marrow of our good name. It happens the same way in individual men: because of some vicious mole of nature in them — say, in their birth, where they're not guilty, since nobody chooses his own origin — or by the overgrowth of some temperament that keeps breaking down the fences and forts of reason, or by some habit that over-ferments the shape of acceptable manners — these men, carrying, I say, the stamp of one defect (nature's uniform, or fortune's star), though their other virtues be as pure as grace, as infinite as a man can bear, will be corrupted in the general judgment by that one particular fault. The tiny dram of evil dissolves all the noble substance into doubt, to its own disgrace.

**HORATIO:** Look, my lord — it's coming!

*[Enter Ghost.]*

**HAMLET:** Angels and ministers of grace defend us! Whether you're a spirit of health or a goblin damned, whether you bring airs from heaven or blasts from hell, whether your intentions are wicked or charitable — you come in such a questionable shape that I will speak to you. I'll call you Hamlet, King, father, royal Dane. Oh, answer me! Don't let me burst with ignorance — tell me why your sanctified bones, coffined in death, have burst their burial shroud; why the sepulchre in which we saw you quietly entombed has opened its ponderous marble jaws to cast you up again. What can it mean that you, a dead corpse, in full armor again, revisit the glimmering moonlight, making the night hideous — and that we, nature's fools, are shaken to the core of our being by thoughts beyond the reach of our souls? Say — why is this? What is it for? What should we do?

*[Ghost beckons Hamlet.]*

**HORATIO:** It's beckoning you to go away with it, as if it had something to communicate to you alone.

**MARCELLUS:** Look how courteously it waves you toward more distant ground. But don't go with it!

**HORATIO:** No — by no means.

**HAMLET:** It won't speak here; then I'll follow it.

**HORATIO:** Don't, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Why — what's there to fear? I don't value my life at a pin's worth; and as for my soul, what can it do to that, when the soul is a thing as immortal as itself? It's waving me on again. I'll follow it.

**HORATIO:** What if it lures you toward the water, my lord, or to the dreadful summit of the cliff that juts out over its base into the sea, and there takes on some other horrible form that might strip you of your sovereign reason and drag you into madness? Think of it. The place itself puts fantasies of desperation, with no other motive needed, into any brain that looks down so many fathoms to the sea and hears it roaring below.

**HAMLET:** It's still waving me on. — Go ahead; I'll follow you.

**MARCELLUS:** You shall not go, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Take your hands off me.

**HORATIO:** Listen to reason — you shall not go.

**HAMLET:** My fate is crying out, and it makes every small artery in this body as tough as the muscle of the Nemean lion. —

*[Ghost beckons.]*

It's still calling me. — Let go of me, gentlemen —

*[Breaking free from them.]*

By heaven, I'll make a ghost of any man who blocks me! — Get away, I say! — Go on; I'll follow you.

*[Exeunt Ghost and Hamlet.]*

**HORATIO:** His imagination is driving him desperate.

**MARCELLUS:** Let's follow — it's not right to obey him in this.

**HORATIO:** Let's go after him. — What will come of all this?

**MARCELLUS:** Something is rotten in the state of Denmark.

**HORATIO:** Heaven will direct it.

**MARCELLUS:** No — let's follow him.

*[Exeunt.]*

### Act I, Scene 5

> *Scene: A more remote part of the Castle.*

*[Enter Ghost and Hamlet.]*

**HAMLET:** Where are you leading me? Speak — I'll go no further.

**GHOST:** Mark my words.

**HAMLET:** I will.

**GHOST:** My hour is almost come, when I must surrender myself back to sulphurous, tormenting flames.

**HAMLET:** Alas, poor ghost!

**GHOST:** Don't pity me — just lend your serious hearing to what I'm about to unfold.

**HAMLET:** Speak. I am bound to hear.

**GHOST:** And bound to revenge, once you have heard.

**HAMLET:** What?

**GHOST:** I am your father's spirit — doomed for a certain term to walk the night, and confined by day to fasting in fires, until the foul crimes committed in my living days are burnt and purged away. If I were not forbidden to tell the secrets of my prison-house, I could unfold a tale whose lightest word would rake your soul raw, freeze your young blood, make your two eyes leap from their orbits like falling stars, part your knotted, tangled locks, and make every single hair stand on end like quills on the anxious porcupine. But this revelation of eternity must not be made to ears of flesh and blood. Listen — listen — oh, listen! If you ever loved your dear father—

**HAMLET:** O God!

**GHOST:** —revenge his foul and most unnatural murder.

**HAMLET:** Murder!

**GHOST:** Murder most foul — as it is even at its best; but this one most foul, strange, and unnatural.

**HAMLET:** Tell me quickly, so that I, with wings as swift as meditation or the thoughts of love, may sweep to my revenge.

**GHOST:** I find you ready. And you would have to be duller than the fat weed that rots at its ease on the wharf of Lethe not to be stirred by this. Now, Hamlet, hear me. The story given out is that while I slept in my orchard, a serpent stung me — and so the whole ear of Denmark is grossly deceived by a fabricated account of my death. But know this, you noble youth: the serpent that stung your father's life now wears his crown.

**HAMLET:** O my prophetic soul! My uncle!

**GHOST:** Yes — that incestuous, that adulterous beast. With the witchcraft of his cleverness, with treacherous gifts — oh, wicked cleverness and gifts, that have the power to seduce like that! — he won to his shameful lust the will of my most seeming-virtuous queen. O Hamlet, what a fall that was! From me, whose love was of such dignity that it walked hand in hand with the very vow I made to her at our marriage — and to sink to a wretch whose natural gifts were poor compared to mine! But just as virtue can never be seduced, though lewdness court it in a heavenly shape, so lust, though wedded to a radiant angel, will glut itself on a celestial bed and then prey on garbage. But wait — I think I smell the morning air. Let me be brief. Sleeping in my orchard, my regular habit every afternoon, in that unguarded hour your uncle crept up on me with a vial of the cursed juice of hebona, and into the porches of my ears he poured the leprous distillation — a poison whose effect is at such enmity with human blood that, swift as quicksilver, it races through the natural gates and alleys of the body, and with sudden force it curdles and clots the thin, wholesome blood, the way drops of acid curdle milk. So it did mine — and an instant scaly eruption crusted, leper-like, in a vile and loathsome bark over all my smooth body. So that's how I was — sleeping, by a brother's hand — stripped of life, of crown, of queen, all at one stroke. Cut off in the very blossom of my sins: no communion, no confession, no last rites; no accounts settled, but sent to my final reckoning with all my imperfections on my head. Oh, horrible! Oh, horrible! Most horrible! If you have natural feeling in you, don't stand for it. Don't let the royal bed of Denmark be a couch for lust and damned incest. But however you pursue this act, don't corrupt your own mind, and don't let your soul plot anything against your mother. Leave her to heaven, and to those thorns that lodge in her bosom, to prick and sting her. Farewell now, all at once! The glowworm shows that morning is near, and begins to dim its ineffectual fire. Adieu, adieu! Hamlet — remember me.

*[Exit.]*

**HAMLET:** O all you host of heaven! O earth! What else? Shall I add hell to the list? Oh, damn it! — Hold together, my heart; and you, my muscles, don't grow old on me this instant — hold me stiffly upright. Remember you! Yes, you poor ghost, as long as memory keeps a seat in this distracted globe of my head. Remember you! Yes — from the writing tablet of my memory I'll wipe away all trivial, foolish records, all the maxims of books, all impressions, all past imprints that youth and observation copied there; and your commandment, all alone, shall live within the book and volume of my brain, unmixed with anything baser. Yes, by heaven! O most pernicious woman! O villain, villain, smiling, damned villain! My notebook — it's fitting that I write this down: that one may smile, and smile, and be a villain. At least I'm certain it can be so in Denmark.

*[Writing.]*

So, uncle — there you are. Now to my watchword. It is: "Adieu, adieu! Remember me." I have sworn it.

**HORATIO:** *[Within.]* My lord, my lord!

**MARCELLUS:** *[Within.]* Lord Hamlet!

**HORATIO:** *[Within.]* Heaven protect him!

**HAMLET:** So be it!

**MARCELLUS:** *[Within.]* Illo, ho, ho, my lord!

**HAMLET:** Hillo, ho, ho, boy! Come, my bird, come!

*[Enter Horatio and Marcellus.]*

**MARCELLUS:** How is it, my noble lord?

**HORATIO:** What news, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Oh — wonderful!

**HORATIO:** My good lord, tell it.

**HAMLET:** No — you'll reveal it.

**HORATIO:** Not I, my lord, by heaven.

**MARCELLUS:** Nor I, my lord.

**HAMLET:** What do you say, then — would any human heart ever imagine it? — But you'll keep it secret?

**HORATIO and MARCELLUS:** Yes, by heaven, my lord.

**HAMLET:** There's not a single villain living in all Denmark — who isn't a thoroughgoing scoundrel.

**HORATIO:** It doesn't take a ghost coming from the grave, my lord, to tell us that.

**HAMLET:** Why, right — you're exactly right. And so, without any more ceremony at all, I think it best we shake hands and part: you to wherever your business and desires point you — for every man has business and desires, such as they are — and as for my own poor part, look you, I'll go pray.

**HORATIO:** These are just wild, whirling words, my lord.

**HAMLET:** I'm sorry they offend you — heartily. Yes, truly — heartily.

**HORATIO:** There's no offense, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Yes, by Saint Patrick, there is, Horatio — a great deal of offense too. As for this vision here: it is an honest ghost — let me tell you that much. As for your desire to know what passed between us, master it as best you can. And now, good friends — as you are friends, scholars, and soldiers — grant me one poor request.

**HORATIO:** What is it, my lord? We will.

**HAMLET:** Never make known what you have seen tonight.

**HORATIO and MARCELLUS:** My lord, we will not.

**HAMLET:** No — swear it.

**HORATIO:** On my faith, my lord — I won't tell.

**MARCELLUS:** Nor I, my lord — on my faith.

**HAMLET:** Swear on my sword.

**MARCELLUS:** We have already sworn, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Properly — on my sword. Properly.

**GHOST:** *[Beneath.]* Swear.

**HAMLET:** Ha, ha, boy! Is that what you say? Are you there, old faithful? — Come on! — You hear this fellow down in the cellar. Consent to swear.

**HORATIO:** Propose the oath, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Never to speak of what you have *seen* — swear by my sword.

**GHOST:** *[Beneath.]* Swear.

**HAMLET:** *Hic et ubique?* Here *and* everywhere? Then we'll shift our ground. — Come over here, gentlemen, and lay your hands again on my sword. Never to speak of what you have *heard* — swear by my sword.

**GHOST:** *[Beneath.]* Swear.

**HAMLET:** Well said, old mole! Can you tunnel through the earth that fast? A worthy sapper! — Once more, move again, good friends.

**HORATIO:** O day and night — but this is astonishingly strange!

**HAMLET:** Then welcome it as you would a stranger. There are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio, than are dreamt of in your philosophy. But come — here, as before, swear — so help you God's mercy — that however strange or odd my behavior may be (since I may in the future think it right to put on an antic disposition — an act of madness), you, seeing me at such times, will never — not with folded arms like this, or this shake of the head, or by uttering some suggestive phrase, like "Well, well, we know," or "We could, if we wanted to," or "If we chose to speak," or "There are those who, if they might" — or any such ambiguous hinting — let on that you know anything about me. Swear that you will not do this — so may grace and mercy help you in your greatest need — swear.

**GHOST:** *[Beneath.]* Swear.

**HAMLET:** Rest, rest, perturbed spirit! — So, gentlemen, with all my love I commend myself to you; and whatever a man as poor as Hamlet can do to express his love and friendship to you, God willing, will not be lacking. Let's go in together — and keep your fingers on your lips, I beg you. The time is out of joint. O cursed spite, that ever I was born to set it right! — No, come, let's go together.

*[Exeunt.]*
