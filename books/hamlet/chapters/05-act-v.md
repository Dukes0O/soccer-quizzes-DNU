# Act V

> William Shakespeare, *Hamlet, Prince of Denmark* (c. 1601) · Act V, Scenes 1–2
>
> [← Act IV](04-act-iv.md) · [Book index](../README.md)

## Overview

Act V opens in a churchyard, with two gravediggers cracking jokes over the grave they are digging — and the grave, it turns out, is Ophelia's. Their comedy is lawyer-humor about whether a suicide can receive Christian burial, and it slides straight into the play's deepest meditation: Hamlet, arriving unrecognized, watches skulls tossed out of the earth and traces every human career — politician, courtier, lawyer, jester, Alexander the Great, Caesar — down to the same anonymous dust. The skull of Yorick, the jester who carried him as a boy, makes the lesson personal. Then Ophelia's funeral procession arrives, and grief turns to violence: Laertes leaps into her grave, Hamlet leaps in after him, and the two grapple over who loved her more.

Scene 2 completes the story. Hamlet tells Horatio how, on the voyage to England, he opened the royal commission ordering his execution, forged a replacement condemning Rosencrantz and Guildenstern instead, and sealed it with his father's signet — reading in the whole sequence of lucky accidents the hand of providence. The foppish courtier Osric arrives with the King's wager on a fencing match against Laertes, and Hamlet, sensing something wrong at his heart but refusing to flinch, accepts: "the readiness is all."

The match is Claudius's final trap — Laertes's foil unblunted and poisoned, a poisoned cup as backup — and it destroys everyone. Gertrude drinks the cup meant for Hamlet; Laertes wounds Hamlet, then is wounded with his own poisoned blade in the scuffle; the dying Laertes confesses everything, and Hamlet at last kills Claudius — with both sword and cup. Dying, Hamlet gives his vote for the succession to Fortinbras and charges Horatio to live and tell his story truly. The act answers the play's long question of revenge not through Hamlet's plotting but through the plotters' schemes recoiling on themselves — "purposes mistook, fallen on the inventors' heads" — and closes on soldiers' music, cannon fire, and silence.

## Key Concepts

- **Death the leveler** — The graveyard scene runs the whole social ladder into the ground: politician, courtier, lawyer, fine lady, jester, Alexander, Caesar — all end as the same skull, the same loam that might plug a beer barrel. Hamlet's obsession with mortality becomes calm, even playful, contemplation.
- **Providence and readiness** — Hamlet returns transformed. "There's a divinity that shapes our ends," "there's a special providence in the fall of a sparrow," "the readiness is all": the delaying, plotting Hamlet of the earlier acts gives way to one who trusts the outcome to heaven and simply stands ready.
- **Equivocation and empty language** — The gravedigger answers Hamlet with maddening literal-mindedness ("We must speak by the card, or equivocation will undo us"), and Osric speaks a courtier's jargon so inflated it means nothing. Both are comic mirrors of a court where words have long concealed truth.
- **Suicide and Christian burial** — Ophelia's "doubtful" death gets her only "maimed rites," and only because royal command overrules church law. The gravediggers' opening debate makes explicit the class hypocrisy: a gentlewoman may drown herself with more license than her "even Christian."
- **Revenge accomplished by recoil, not design** — Every deliberate plot in the play misfires. Hamlet finally kills Claudius not by a plan but in the wreckage of Claudius's own plan: the poisoned foil and poisoned cup destroy Gertrude, Laertes, and the King himself. Laertes names the pattern: "I am justly killed with mine own treachery."
- **Report, memory, and the wounded name** — Hamlet's last anxiety is not death but reputation: Horatio must "absent himself from felicity awhile" to report Hamlet "and my cause aright." Horatio's closing promise to tell "the yet unknowing world how these things came about" is the play describing itself.
- **Succession and the restoration of order** — The election Claudius "popped in between" and Hamlet's hopes finally lands, with Hamlet's dying voice, on Fortinbras — the man of action who has shadowed Hamlet all play — and the stage is cleared with military honors.

## Modern Translation

### Act V, Scene 1

> *Scene: A churchyard.*

*[Enter two Clowns — a gravedigger and his companion — with spades and mattocks.]*

**FIRST CLOWN:** Is she really getting a Christian burial, when she deliberately went and sought her own salvation ahead of schedule?

**SECOND CLOWN:** I'm telling you she is — so dig her grave and be quick about it. The coroner held his inquest on her and ruled it a Christian burial.

**FIRST CLOWN:** How can that be? Unless she drowned herself in self-defense?

**SECOND CLOWN:** Well, that's the ruling.

**FIRST CLOWN:** It must have been *se offendendo* — it can't be anything else. Because here's the point: if I drown myself *knowingly*, that argues an act; and an act has three branches — to act, to do, and to perform. *Argal*: she drowned herself knowingly.

**SECOND CLOWN:** No, but listen here, Mister Digger—

**FIRST CLOWN:** Give me a second. Here lies the water — good. Here stands the man — good. If the man goes to this water and drowns himself, then like it or not, *he went* — note that. But if the water comes to *him* and drowns him, then he didn't drown himself. *Argal*: the man who is not guilty of his own death has not shortened his own life.

**SECOND CLOWN:** But is that the law?

**FIRST CLOWN:** It certainly is. Coroner's-inquest law.

**SECOND CLOWN:** You want the truth of it? If this hadn't been a gentlewoman, she'd have been buried outside Christian ground.

**FIRST CLOWN:** Now you've hit it. And the more's the pity that the great folk have a license in this world to drown or hang themselves that their fellow Christians don't get. — Come on, spade. There are no gentlemen of older lineage than gardeners, ditch-diggers, and grave-makers: we carry on Adam's profession.

**SECOND CLOWN:** Was Adam a gentleman?

**FIRST CLOWN:** He was the first man that ever bore arms.

**SECOND CLOWN:** Come off it — he didn't have any.

**FIRST CLOWN:** What are you, a heathen? How do you read your Scripture? Scripture says *Adam dug*. Could he dig without arms? Now I'll put another question to you — and if you don't answer to the point, you can confess yourself and be—

**SECOND CLOWN:** Go on, then.

**FIRST CLOWN:** Who builds stronger than the mason, the shipwright, or the carpenter?

**SECOND CLOWN:** The gallows-maker — because that frame outlives a thousand tenants.

**FIRST CLOWN:** I like your wit, honestly, I do. The gallows is a good answer — but how is it good? It's good to those that do bad. And now *you've* done bad, saying the gallows is built stronger than the church. *Argal*, the gallows might do some good to *you*. Have another go, come on.

**SECOND CLOWN:** "Who builds stronger than a mason, a shipwright, or a carpenter?"

**FIRST CLOWN:** That's it. Answer that and you can knock off for the day.

**SECOND CLOWN:** Ha — now I've got it!

**FIRST CLOWN:** Out with it.

**SECOND CLOWN:** ...Damn. I haven't got it.

*[Enter Hamlet and Horatio, at a distance.]*

**FIRST CLOWN:** Don't beat your brains about it any longer — you can whip a dull donkey all day and he won't go faster. And next time somebody asks you that question, say "a grave-maker": the houses *he* builds last till doomsday. Go on, run over to Yaughan's and fetch me a jug of liquor.

*[Exit Second Clown.]*

*[The First Clown digs, and sings.]*

> In my youth when I loved, when I loved,
> it seemed to me so sweet
> to pass — oh — the time — ah — as suited me,
> oh, nothing seemed half so right.

**HAMLET:** Has this fellow no feeling for his work, that he sings while he digs graves?

**HORATIO:** Habit has made it come easily to him.

**HAMLET:** That's just it. The hand that works least keeps the finer feeling.

**FIRST CLOWN:** *[Sings.]*

> But age with his sneaking steps
> has clawed me in his clutch,
> and shipped me off into the ground,
> as if I'd never been young.

*[Throws up a skull.]*

**HAMLET:** That skull had a tongue in it once, and could sing. And look how the rascal chucks it on the ground, as if it were the jawbone of Cain, who committed the first murder! That could be the head of a politician this donkey is now outmaneuvering — a man who could have talked his way around God himself. Couldn't it?

**HORATIO:** It could, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Or a courtier's, one who could say, "Good morning, sweet lord! How are you today, good lord?" This might be my lord So-and-so, who praised my lord Such-a-one's horse when he meant to beg it off him — mightn't it?

**HORATIO:** Yes, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Exactly so. And now it belongs to my Lady Worm — jawless, and knocked about the head with a sexton's spade. There's a fine turn of fortune's wheel, if we had the trick of seeing it. Did these bones cost no more in the raising than to end up as pieces in a game of skittles? Mine ache just thinking about it.

**FIRST CLOWN:** *[Sings.]*

> A pickaxe and a spade, a spade,
> and also a winding sheet;
> oh, a pit of clay to be dug
> is fit for such a guest.

*[Throws up another skull.]*

**HAMLET:** There's another. Why couldn't that be the skull of a lawyer? Where are his fine distinctions now, his quibbles, his cases, his tenures, and his tricks? Why does he let this rough oaf knock him about the head with a dirty shovel, and not sue him for battery? Hm! This fellow might in his day have been a great buyer of land, with his statutes, his bonds, his settlements, his double guarantors, his recoveries. Is this the final settlement of all his settlements, the last recovery of all his recoveries — a fine head packed full of fine dirt? Will his guarantors guarantee him no more of everything he bought — even with the guarantees doubled — than a plot the length and breadth of a pair of contracts? The deeds to his lands would barely fit in this box — and must the owner himself get no more room than that, eh?

**HORATIO:** Not an inch more, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Isn't legal parchment made of sheepskins?

**HORATIO:** Yes, my lord. And of calfskins too.

**HAMLET:** Then they're sheep and calves who look for security in parchment. I'm going to talk to this fellow. — Whose grave is this, sir?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Mine, sir. *[Sings.]*

> Oh, a pit of clay to be dug
> is fit for such a guest.

**HAMLET:** I suppose it is yours, since you're lying in it.

**FIRST CLOWN:** And you're lying *outside* it, sir, so it isn't yours. As for me, I don't *lie* in it — and yet it's mine.

**HAMLET:** You do lie in it — standing in it and saying it's yours. It's for the dead, not the living; therefore you lie.

**FIRST CLOWN:** It's a lively lie, sir — it'll jump straight from me to you.

**HAMLET:** What man are you digging it for?

**FIRST CLOWN:** For no man, sir.

**HAMLET:** What woman, then?

**FIRST CLOWN:** For no woman either.

**HAMLET:** Who is going to be buried in it?

**FIRST CLOWN:** One that *was* a woman, sir; but rest her soul, she's dead.

**HAMLET:** How exact this rascal is! We have to speak by the compass, or wordplay will be the death of us. By the Lord, Horatio, I've been noticing it these three years: the age has grown so refined that the peasant's toe treads so close on the courtier's heel it chafes his chilblain. — How long have you been a grave-maker?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Of all the days in the year, I started on the very day our late King Hamlet beat Fortinbras.

**HAMLET:** How long ago is that?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Can't you work that out? Any fool can tell you that: it was the very day young Hamlet was born — the one that's mad, and got shipped off to England.

**HAMLET:** Ah, right. And why was he sent to England?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Why, because he was mad. He'll get his wits back there — and if he doesn't, it's no great matter over *there*.

**HAMLET:** Why not?

**FIRST CLOWN:** No one will notice it in him there. The men there are all as mad as he is.

**HAMLET:** How did he go mad?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Very strangely, they say.

**HAMLET:** "Strangely" how?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Faith — by losing his wits.

**HAMLET:** On what ground?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Why, right here in Denmark. I've been sexton here, man and boy, thirty years.

**HAMLET:** How long will a man lie in the earth before he rots?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Faith, if he isn't rotten before he dies — and we get plenty of poxy corpses these days that barely survive the burying — he'll last you some eight or nine years. A tanner will last you nine.

**HAMLET:** Why him more than anyone else?

**FIRST CLOWN:** Why, sir, his hide is so tanned by his trade that it keeps the water out a good long while — and water, sir, is a vicious rotter of your wretched dead body. Now, here's a skull for you: this skull has lain in the earth twenty-three years.

**HAMLET:** Whose was it?

**FIRST CLOWN:** A crazy son-of-a-gun's, it was. Whose do you think?

**HAMLET:** No — I've no idea.

**FIRST CLOWN:** A plague on him, the mad rogue! He poured a whole flagon of Rhine wine over my head once. This very skull, sir, was Yorick's skull — the king's jester.

**HAMLET:** This?

**FIRST CLOWN:** That very one.

**HAMLET:** Let me see. *[Takes the skull.]* Alas, poor Yorick! I knew him, Horatio — a fellow of infinite jest, of most excellent fancy. He carried me on his back a thousand times — and now how my imagination recoils from it! My stomach turns. Here hung the lips that I kissed I don't know how often. Where are your gibes now? Your capers? Your songs? Your flashes of merriment that used to set the whole table roaring? Not one left, to mock your own grinning? Jaw completely fallen off? Now go to my lady's chamber and tell her this: she can paint her face an inch thick, but this is the face she'll come to in the end. Make her laugh at *that*. — Horatio, tell me one thing.

**HORATIO:** What's that, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Do you think Alexander the Great looked like this in the earth?

**HORATIO:** Just like this.

**HAMLET:** And smelled like this? Pah!

*[Throws down the skull.]*

**HORATIO:** Just like this, my lord.

**HAMLET:** To what humble uses we may return, Horatio! Why couldn't the imagination trace the noble dust of Alexander until it finds it plugging the hole in a barrel?

**HORATIO:** To think that way would be to think too curiously.

**HAMLET:** No, honestly, not a bit. You could follow him there quite soberly, with plain probability leading the way. Like this: Alexander died; Alexander was buried; Alexander returns to dust; the dust is earth; from earth we make loam — so why couldn't the loam he was turned into be used to stopper a beer barrel?

> Imperious Caesar, dead and turned to clay,
> might stop a hole to keep the wind away.
> Oh, that the earth which kept the world in awe
> should patch a wall to shut the winter's blast!

But quiet — quiet now, stand aside. Here comes the King.

*[Enter priests and others in procession; the corpse of Ophelia, with Laertes and mourners following; the King, the Queen, their attendants.]*

The Queen, the courtiers — who is it they're following? And with such stripped-down rites? That means the corpse they follow took its own life with a desperate hand. And it was someone of rank. Let's hide a while and watch.

*[Retiring with Horatio.]*

**LAERTES:** What other ceremony is there?

**HAMLET:** That's Laertes — a very noble young man. Listen.

**LAERTES:** What other ceremony is there?

**FIRST PRIEST:** Her funeral rites have been extended as far as we have authority. Her death was doubtful; and if a great command from above had not overruled the proper order, she would have lain in unsanctified ground until the last trumpet — and instead of charitable prayers, shards, flints, and pebbles would have been thrown on her. Yet she has been allowed her virgin garlands, her maiden flowers strewn on the grave, and burial in holy ground with the tolling of the bell.

**LAERTES:** Must nothing more be done?

**FIRST PRIEST:** Nothing more. We would profane the service of the dead if we sang a requiem for her and gave her the same rest as souls that departed in peace.

**LAERTES:** Lay her in the earth — and from her fair and unpolluted flesh may violets spring! I tell you this, you graceless priest: my sister will be a ministering angel when *you* lie howling in hell.

**HAMLET:** What — the fair Ophelia!

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Sweets to the sweet: farewell. *[Scattering flowers.]* I hoped you would be my Hamlet's wife. I thought I would be decking your bridal bed, sweet girl — not strewing your grave.

**LAERTES:** Oh, may triple grief fall ten times triple on that cursed head whose wicked deed robbed you of your matchless mind! — Hold back the earth a moment, until I have caught her once more in my arms. *[Leaps into the grave.]* Now pile your dust on the living and the dead, till you've raised a mountain on this flat ground to overtop old Pelion, or the sky-touching peak of blue Olympus.

**HAMLET:** *[Advancing.]* Who is this whose grief carries such an emphasis? Whose phrases of sorrow conjure the wandering stars and make them stand still like listeners struck with wonder? This is I, Hamlet the Dane. *[Leaps into the grave.]*

**LAERTES:** The devil take your soul!

*[Grappling with him.]*

**HAMLET:** That's not a good prayer. Take your fingers from my throat, please — because though I'm not hot-tempered and rash, I have something dangerous in me that your wisdom should be afraid of. Take your hand off!

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Pull them apart.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Hamlet! Hamlet!

**ALL:** Gentlemen!

**HORATIO:** My good lord, calm yourself.

*[The attendants part them, and they come out of the grave.]*

**HAMLET:** Why, I will fight him on this theme until my eyelids can no longer blink.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Oh my son — what theme?

**HAMLET:** I loved Ophelia. Forty thousand brothers, with all their quantity of love, could not add up to my sum. — What will *you* do for her?

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Oh, he is mad, Laertes.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** For the love of God, let him be!

**HAMLET:** God's wounds — show me what you'll do. Will you weep? Will you fight? Will you starve yourself? Tear yourself to pieces? Drink vinegar? Eat a crocodile? I'll do it. Did you come here to whine? To outdo me by leaping into her grave? Be buried alive with her — so will I. And if you're going to babble about mountains, then let them throw millions of acres on top of us, till our mound scorches its head against the sphere of fire and makes Mount Ossa look like a wart! No — if you want to rant, I can rant as well as you.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** This is pure madness. The fit will work on him like this for a while; and then, patient as the mother dove when her golden pair of chicks has hatched, his silence will sit drooping.

**HAMLET:** Listen, sir — why do you treat me this way? I always loved you. But it doesn't matter. Let Hercules himself do what he may, the cat will mew, and dog will have his day.

*[Exit.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Please, good Horatio, keep close to him. *[Exit Horatio.]* *[To Laertes]* Let our talk last night strengthen your patience; we'll bring the matter to an immediate test. — Good Gertrude, set a watch on your son. — This grave shall have a living monument. An hour of quiet is coming soon; until then, let us proceed with patience.

*[Exeunt.]*

### Act V, Scene 2

> *Scene: A hall in the Castle.*

*[Enter Hamlet and Horatio.]*

**HAMLET:** So much for that, sir. Now let me tell you the rest — you remember all the circumstances?

**HORATIO:** Remember it, my lord!

**HAMLET:** Sir, there was a kind of fighting in my heart that wouldn't let me sleep. I felt I lay worse than a mutineer in shipboard shackles. Rashly — and praised be rashness for it — let's admit this: our recklessness sometimes serves us well when our deep-laid plots go stale; and that should teach us there's a divinity that shapes our ends, rough-hew them how we will.

**HORATIO:** That is most certain.

**HAMLET:** Up from my cabin, my sea-gown wrapped around me, I groped through the dark to find them — found what I wanted, lifted their packet, and finally withdrew to my own room again. Then, my fears making me forget my manners, I was bold enough to unseal their grand commission — and there I found, Horatio — oh, royal villainy! — an exact command, larded with all sorts of official reasons touching Denmark's safety, and England's too, and — ha! — with such hobgoblins and terrors if I were left alive, that on the very reading of it, with no time wasted — no, not even a pause to sharpen the axe — my head should be struck off.

**HORATIO:** Is it possible?

**HAMLET:** Here's the commission — read it at your leisure. But do you want to hear how I proceeded?

**HORATIO:** Please.

**HAMLET:** Netted all round with villainies as I was — before I could even compose a prologue in my brain, they had already begun the play — I sat down, devised a new commission, and wrote it out in a fine formal hand. I used to consider it, as our statesmen do, beneath me to write neatly, and worked hard to unlearn that skill; but now, sir, it did me solid service. Do you want to know the substance of what I wrote?

**HORATIO:** Yes, my good lord.

**HAMLET:** An earnest charge in the King's name. As a letter, it ran like this:

> *As England is Denmark's faithful tributary; as the love between our two crowns should flourish like the palm tree; as peace should always wear her garland of wheat and stand as the link between our friendships* — and many more such weighty "whereas"-clauses — *upon the reading and understanding of these contents, without any further debate whatsoever, you are to put the bearers of this letter to sudden death, allowing them no time even for confession.*

**HORATIO:** How was it sealed?

**HAMLET:** Why, even in that, heaven was directing things. I had my father's signet ring in my purse — the very model of the Danish state seal. I folded the document up in the same form as the other, signed it, gave it the stamp of the seal, and put it back safely — the swap never noticed. The next day was our sea-fight, and what followed after that you already know.

**HORATIO:** So Guildenstern and Rosencrantz go to it.

**HAMLET:** Why, man, they courted this employment. They are nowhere near my conscience: their destruction grows out of their own meddling. It's dangerous when lesser natures come between the thrusting, furious points of mighty opponents.

**HORATIO:** Why, what a king this is!

**HAMLET:** Doesn't it now fall squarely to me, do you think — he killed my king and whored my mother; popped in between the election and my hopes; cast his hook for my very life, and with such trickery — isn't it perfect conscience to pay him off with this arm? And isn't it damnable to let this cancer of our nature go on into further evil?

**HORATIO:** He'll soon have to hear from England what the outcome of the business there was.

**HAMLET:** It will be soon. The interval is mine — and a man's life is no more than the time it takes to say "One." But I am very sorry, good Horatio, that I forgot myself with Laertes; for in the image of my cause I see the portrait of his. I'll seek his good will. But truly, the showiness of his grief threw me into a towering passion.

**HORATIO:** Quiet — who's coming here?

*[Enter Osric.]*

**OSRIC:** Your lordship is most welcome back to Denmark.

**HAMLET:** I humbly thank you, sir. *[Aside to Horatio]* Do you know this water-fly?

**HORATIO:** No, my good lord.

**HAMLET:** Then your state is the more blessed, because it's a vice to know him. He owns a great deal of land, and fertile land too. Let one beast be lord over other beasts, and his feeding trough will stand at the king's own table. He's a chattering jackdaw — but, as I say, extensively landed. In dirt.

**OSRIC:** Sweet lord, if your lordship has a moment of leisure, I have something to convey to you from His Majesty.

**HAMLET:** I will receive it, sir, with all diligence of spirit. Put your hat to its proper use — it's for the head.

**OSRIC:** I thank your lordship — it's very hot.

**HAMLET:** No, believe me, it's very cold; the wind is from the north.

**OSRIC:** It is somewhat cold, my lord, indeed.

**HAMLET:** And yet I find it very sultry and hot for my constitution.

**OSRIC:** Exceedingly, my lord — it is very sultry, as it were — I cannot say quite how. But my lord, His Majesty instructed me to inform you that he has laid a great wager on your head. Sir, this is the matter—

**HAMLET:** Please, I beg you — remember—

*[Hamlet gestures for him to put on his hat.]*

**OSRIC:** No, in good faith — for my own comfort, in good faith. Sir, Laertes has newly come back to court — believe me, an absolute gentleman, full of the most excellent distinctions, of very polished manners and grand appearance. Indeed, to speak of him with real feeling, he is the map and calendar of gentility; for you will find in him the whole continent of every quality a gentleman could wish to see.

**HAMLET:** Sir, his definition suffers no loss whatsoever in your account of him — though I know that to itemize him inventorially would dizzy the arithmetic of memory, and even then would only yaw along behind, considering the speed of his sail. But in the sheer truth of praise, I take him to be a soul of great substance, and his essence of such scarcity and rarity that — to speak truly of him — his only equal is his own mirror, and anyone who tried to follow in his track would be his shadow, nothing more.

**OSRIC:** Your lordship speaks most infallibly of him.

**HAMLET:** But the relevance, sir? Why are we wrapping the gentleman up in our cruder breath?

**OSRIC:** Sir?

**HORATIO:** Is it not possible to understand your own language when another man speaks it? You can manage it, sir, really.

**HAMLET:** What is the point of naming this gentleman?

**OSRIC:** Of Laertes?

**HORATIO:** *[Aside]* His purse is empty already — all his golden words are spent.

**HAMLET:** Of him, sir.

**OSRIC:** I know you are not ignorant—

**HAMLET:** I wish you did know it, sir — though, in truth, even if you did, it wouldn't much flatter me. Well, sir?

**OSRIC:** You are not ignorant of what excellence Laertes possesses—

**HAMLET:** I don't dare admit that, in case I seemed to compare myself with him in excellence — since to know a man well, you would have to know yourself.

**OSRIC:** I mean, sir, with his weapon. By the reputation others give him, in that particular merit he is unmatched.

**HAMLET:** What's his weapon?

**OSRIC:** Rapier and dagger.

**HAMLET:** That's two of his weapons. But fine.

**OSRIC:** The king, sir, has wagered six Barbary horses against him; against which he has staked — "imponed," as I understand it — six French rapiers and daggers with their accessories: belt, hangers, and so on. Three of the carriages, in faith, are very pleasing to the eye, very well matched to the hilts — most delicate carriages, and of very refined design.

**HAMLET:** What do you call the "carriages"?

**HORATIO:** *[Aside]* I knew you'd have to consult the footnotes before he was done.

**OSRIC:** The carriages, sir, are the hangers.

**HAMLET:** The word would be more appropriate to the matter if we wore cannons at our sides — until then, I'd rather they stayed "hangers." But go on: six Barbary horses against six French swords, their accessories, and three refined-design "carriages" — that's the French stake against the Danish. Why has all this been "imponed," as you call it?

**OSRIC:** The king, sir, has bet that in a dozen bouts between you and him, Laertes shall not beat you by more than three hits — he has laid the score at twelve to nine; and it would come to an immediate trial if your lordship would be so good as to answer.

**HAMLET:** What if I answer "no"?

**OSRIC:** I mean, my lord, the presenting of your person in the trial.

**HAMLET:** Sir, I will walk here in the hall. If it pleases His Majesty, this is my hour of the day for exercise. Let the foils be brought, the gentleman being willing and the king holding to his purpose — I'll win for him if I can. If not, I'll gain nothing but my shame and the odd hits.

**OSRIC:** Shall I deliver your answer exactly so?

**HAMLET:** To that effect, sir — with whatever flourishes your nature dictates.

**OSRIC:** I commend my duty to your lordship.

**HAMLET:** Yours, yours. *[Exit Osric.]* He does well to commend it himself — no one else's tongue would do it for him.

**HORATIO:** This lapwing runs off with the eggshell still on his head.

**HAMLET:** He paid compliments to his mother's breast before he sucked at it. That's how he — and many more of the same flock that this frivolous age dotes on — has merely picked up the tune of the times and the outward mannerisms of polite conversation: a kind of frothy collection that carries them through the most refined and discriminating opinions. But just blow on them to test them, and the bubbles pop.

*[Enter a Lord.]*

**LORD:** My lord, His Majesty sent his greetings to you by young Osric, who brought back word that you are waiting for him in the hall. He sends to know whether your pleasure still holds to fence with Laertes, or whether you would like more time.

**HAMLET:** I am constant to my purposes; they follow the king's pleasure. If his convenience calls, mine is ready — now or whenever, provided I'm as fit as I am now.

**LORD:** The King and Queen and the whole court are coming down.

**HAMLET:** Perfect timing.

**LORD:** The Queen desires you to say something courteous to Laertes before you begin the match.

**HAMLET:** Good advice — I'll take it.

*[Exit Lord.]*

**HORATIO:** You will lose this wager, my lord.

**HAMLET:** I don't think so. Since he went to France I've been in constant practice; at these odds, I'll win. But you can't imagine how sick everything feels here, around my heart. Still — it doesn't matter.

**HORATIO:** No, my good lord—

**HAMLET:** It's only foolishness — but it's the kind of misgiving that might trouble a woman.

**HORATIO:** If your mind is uneasy about anything, obey it. I'll head them off before they get here and say you're not fit.

**HAMLET:** Not a chance — we defy augury. There's a special providence in the fall of a sparrow. If it be now, it's not to come; if it is not to come, it will be now; if it is not now, still it will come. The readiness is all. Since no man knows anything of the world he leaves behind, what does it matter to leave it early? Let be.

*[Enter King, Queen, Laertes, Lords, Osric, and Attendants with foils and gauntlets. A table, with flagons of wine on it.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Come, Hamlet, come, and take this hand from mine.

*[The King puts Laertes's hand into Hamlet's.]*

**HAMLET:** Give me your pardon, sir. I have done you wrong; but pardon it, as you are a gentleman. Everyone present knows — and you must certainly have heard — how I have been punished with a severe mental affliction. Whatever I have done that might have roughly wakened your natural feeling, your honor, and your resentment, I here declare was madness. Was it Hamlet who wronged Laertes? Never Hamlet. If Hamlet is taken away from himself, and when he is not himself he wrongs Laertes, then Hamlet does not do it — Hamlet denies it. Who does it, then? His madness. If that is so, then Hamlet himself is on the side of the wronged: his madness is poor Hamlet's own enemy. Sir, before this audience, let my disavowal of any intended harm acquit me this far in your most generous thoughts: that I shot my arrow over the house and hit my brother.

**LAERTES:** As far as natural feeling goes — which in this case should most spur me to revenge — I am satisfied. But on the point of honor I stand apart, and will accept no reconciliation until elder masters of acknowledged honor give me a judgment and a precedent for peace that leaves my name unwounded. But until that time, I receive your offered love as love, and will not wrong it.

**HAMLET:** I embrace it freely, and will play this brothers' wager with an open heart. — Give us the foils. Come on.

**LAERTES:** Come — one for me.

**HAMLET:** I'll be your foil, Laertes: against my clumsiness your skill will blaze out like a star against the darkest night.

**LAERTES:** You're mocking me, sir.

**HAMLET:** No — by this hand.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Give them the foils, young Osric. Cousin Hamlet, you know the wager?

**HAMLET:** Very well, my lord. Your grace has laid the odds on the weaker side.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** I'm not worried about it. I have seen you both; and since he has improved, we have the odds in our favor.

**LAERTES:** This one is too heavy — let me see another.

**HAMLET:** This one suits me well. These foils are all the same length?

*[They prepare to play.]*

**OSRIC:** Yes, my good lord.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Set the flagons of wine on that table. If Hamlet lands the first or second hit, or evens the score on the third exchange, let all the battlements fire their cannon. The king shall drink to Hamlet's better breath, and into the cup he'll throw a pearl richer than the one four successive kings have worn in Denmark's crown. Give me the cups; and let the kettledrum speak to the trumpet, the trumpet to the cannoneer outside, the cannons to the heavens, and the heavens to the earth: "Now the king drinks to Hamlet." — Come, begin. And you, the judges, keep a sharp eye.

**HAMLET:** Come on, sir.

**LAERTES:** Come, my lord.

*[They play.]*

**HAMLET:** One.

**LAERTES:** No.

**HAMLET:** Judgment!

**OSRIC:** A hit — a very palpable hit.

**LAERTES:** Fine — again.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Wait — give me a drink. Hamlet, this pearl is yours; here's to your health.

*[Trumpets sound; a cannon is fired within.]*

Give him the cup.

**HAMLET:** I'll play this bout first; set it aside a while. — Come. *[They play.]* Another hit — what do you say?

**LAERTES:** A touch, a touch — I confess it.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Our son will win.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** He's out of condition, and short of breath. — Here, Hamlet, take my handkerchief and wipe your brow. The Queen drinks to your fortune, Hamlet.

**HAMLET:** Good madam!

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Gertrude, do not drink.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** I will, my lord — please excuse me.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** *[Aside.]* It is the poisoned cup. It is too late.

**HAMLET:** I don't dare drink yet, madam — in a little while.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Come, let me wipe your face.

**LAERTES:** *[To the King.]* My lord, I'll hit him now.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** I don't think so.

**LAERTES:** *[Aside.]* And yet it's almost against my conscience.

**HAMLET:** Come on, Laertes — the third bout. You're only toying with me. Please, attack with your very best violence; I'm afraid you're treating me like a spoiled child.

**LAERTES:** You say so? Come on.

*[They play.]*

**OSRIC:** Nothing, either way.

**LAERTES:** Have at you now!

*[Laertes wounds Hamlet; then, in the scuffle, they exchange rapiers, and Hamlet wounds Laertes.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Part them — they've lost their tempers.

**HAMLET:** No — come again!

*[The Queen falls.]*

**OSRIC:** Look to the Queen there — ho!

**HORATIO:** They're bleeding on both sides. — How is it, my lord?

**OSRIC:** How is it, Laertes?

**LAERTES:** Why, like a woodcock caught in my own snare, Osric — I am justly killed by my own treachery.

**HAMLET:** How is the Queen?

**KING CLAUDIUS:** She faints at the sight of their blood.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** No, no — the drink, the drink! Oh, my dear Hamlet! The drink, the drink — I am poisoned.

*[Dies.]*

**HAMLET:** Oh, villainy! Ho — lock the doors! Treachery — hunt it out!

*[Laertes falls.]*

**LAERTES:** It is here, Hamlet. Hamlet, you are slain. No medicine in the world can do you any good; there is not half an hour of life left in you. The treacherous instrument is in your own hand — unblunted, and poisoned. The foul plot has turned itself back on me: look — here I lie, never to rise again. Your mother is poisoned. I can say no more. The king — the king is to blame.

**HAMLET:** The point poisoned too! Then, venom, do your work.

*[Stabs the King.]*

**OSRIC AND LORDS:** Treason! Treason!

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Oh, defend me still, friends — I am only hurt.

**HAMLET:** Here, you incestuous, murderous, damned Dane — drink off this potion. Is your pearl in here? Follow my mother.

*[The King dies.]*

**LAERTES:** He is justly served — it is a poison he mixed himself. Exchange forgiveness with me, noble Hamlet. May my death and my father's not be charged against you, nor yours against me!

*[Dies.]*

**HAMLET:** Heaven make you free of it! I follow you. — I am dead, Horatio. — Wretched queen, goodbye! — You who look pale and tremble at this catastrophe, who are only silent extras or the audience to this act — if I had time (but this grim officer, Death, is strict in making his arrest) — oh, I could tell you — but let it be. Horatio, I am dead; you live. Report me and my cause truly to the unsatisfied.

**HORATIO:** Never believe it. I am more an ancient Roman than a Dane — here's still some liquor left.

**HAMLET:** As you are a man, give me the cup. Let it go — by heaven, I'll have it! O God, Horatio, what a wounded name will live on after me, if things are left unknown like this! If you ever held me in your heart, keep yourself away from the happiness of death a while longer, and draw your breath in pain in this harsh world, to tell my story.

*[A march far off, and a shot within.]*

What warlike noise is this?

**OSRIC:** Young Fortinbras, returning in conquest from Poland, fires this warlike volley in salute to the ambassadors of England.

**HAMLET:** Oh — I die, Horatio. The potent poison completely overpowers my spirit. I cannot live to hear the news from England; but I predict the election will fall on Fortinbras. He has my dying voice. Tell him so — together with the events, great and small, that have brought this about. The rest is silence.

*[Dies.]*

**HORATIO:** Now a noble heart cracks. Good night, sweet prince, and flights of angels sing thee to thy rest! — Why is the drum coming this way?

*[A march within.]*

*[Enter Fortinbras, the English Ambassadors, and others.]*

**FORTINBRAS:** Where is this sight?

**HORATIO:** What is it you wish to see? If anything of woe or wonder, stop your search — it is here.

**FORTINBRAS:** This heap of the dead cries out massacre. O proud Death, what feast is being prepared in your eternal cell, that you have struck down so many princes at a single shot, so bloodily?

**FIRST AMBASSADOR:** The sight is grim; and our news from England comes too late. The ears that should have heard us are past all sensing — we came to tell him his command has been fulfilled: that Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are dead. Where shall we get our thanks?

**HORATIO:** Not from his mouth, even if it still had life enough to thank you: he never gave the order for their deaths. But since you have arrived so exactly upon this bloody business — you from the Polish wars, and you from England — give the order that these bodies be placed high on a platform in full view; and let me tell the still-unknowing world how these things came about. Then you shall hear of carnal, bloody, and unnatural acts; of accidental judgments and casual slaughters; of deaths brought on by cunning and by contrived cause; and, in this final outcome, of schemes that misfired and fell back on the heads of their inventors. All this I can truly deliver.

**FORTINBRAS:** Let us hurry to hear it, and call the noblest to the audience. As for me, it is with sorrow that I embrace my fortune. I have some rights of memory in this kingdom, which my present advantage now invites me to claim.

**HORATIO:** Of that too I shall have cause to speak — and from the mouth of one whose vote will draw more after it. But let all this be done at once, while men's minds are still wild, in case more disasters grow out of plots and blunders.

**FORTINBRAS:** Let four captains bear Hamlet like a soldier to the platform; for he was likely, had he been put to the test, to have proved most royal. And for his passing, let the soldiers' music and the rites of war speak loudly for him. — Take up the bodies. A sight like this belongs on a battlefield; here it shows that much has gone amiss. Go — order the soldiers to shoot.

*[A dead march.]*

*[Exeunt, bearing off the dead bodies; after which, a peal of cannon is shot off.]*
