# Act II

> William Shakespeare, *Hamlet, Prince of Denmark* (c. 1601) · Act II, Scenes 1–2
>
> [← Act I](01-act-i.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Act III →](03-act-iii.md)

## Overview

Act II is the play's great act of watching and being watched. Nothing violent happens in it — no ghost, no duel, no poison — and yet by its end nearly every character has been converted into either a spy or a target. Polonius opens the act by sending his servant Reynaldo to Paris to spread little lies about his own son Laertes, on the theory that scattering "bait of falsehood" will catch a "carp of truth." It is a comic miniature of the whole Danish court: even fatherly concern here works by surveillance and indirection. Moments later Ophelia arrives, terrified, to report that Hamlet has burst into her room half-dressed, silent, and staring — the first news anyone has of his "antic disposition" in action — and Polonius leaps to the conclusion that thwarted love has driven the prince mad.

Scene 2 assembles the machinery of espionage on a larger scale. Claudius and Gertrude have summoned Hamlet's childhood friends Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to sound him out; Polonius arrives to pitch his love-madness theory, complete with Hamlet's mangled love letter read aloud to the throne; and a plan is set to plant Ophelia in Hamlet's path while the king and Polonius hide and watch. Sandwiched between these schemes, the ambassadors return from Norway with the news that young Fortinbras has been checked and redirected against Poland — the political subplot ticking quietly forward.

But the act belongs to Hamlet, who runs rings around every spy sent against him. He toys with Polonius ("you're a fishmonger"), pries the truth out of Rosencrantz and Guildenstern within minutes, and delivers to them, almost casually, one of the play's great meditations: "What a piece of work is man… and yet, to me, what is this quintessence of dust?" The arrival of the travelling players changes everything. Hearing the First Player weep real tears over the fictional grief of Hecuba, Hamlet turns on himself in the act's closing soliloquy — a coward who "unpacks his heart with words" while his father's murder goes unavenged — and then seizes on a plan: the players will perform a murder resembling his father's before Claudius, and Hamlet will watch the king's face. The ghost's word is not yet evidence; "the play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the king."

## Key Concepts

- **Surveillance and indirection** — Every plot in this act is a spying plot: Reynaldo set on Laertes, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern set on Hamlet, Ophelia to be "loosed" at him while the king and Polonius watch from behind a tapestry. Polonius states the method outright: "By indirections find directions out." The act asks whether truth gathered by deceit is truth at all.
- **Madness real and feigned** — Hamlet's "transformation" is now public fact, but its cause is contested. Polonius insists it is love-madness; Claudius suspects something deeper; Gertrude names the obvious ("his father's death and our o'erhasty marriage"). Hamlet's own performance is calibrated nonsense — "Though this be madness, yet there is a method in't" — and he tips his hand to his friends: he is "but mad north-north-west."
- **Acting and authenticity** — The players bring the act's central paradox to a head: a professional actor weeps genuinely for an imaginary queen, while Hamlet, with a real murdered father, "can say nothing." Fiction here produces more visible passion than fact, and Hamlet decides to turn that power against Claudius — using a play, a lie, to extract a true confession.
- **Delay and self-reproach** — The closing soliloquy ("O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!") is the play's first full-throated confrontation with Hamlet's inaction. He lashes himself as a coward and a "John-a-dreams," then catches himself cursing like a kitchen maid instead of acting — and channels the frustration into a plan rather than a deed.
- **The ghost's ambiguity** — Hamlet's delay gets its intellectual justification here: "The spirit that I have seen / May be the devil," exploiting his melancholy to damn him. The Mousetrap plan is framed as evidence-gathering — he wants "grounds more relative" than a ghost's word before he kills a king.
- **Thought makes the world** — Hamlet's prison exchange with Rosencrantz and Guildenstern ("Denmark's a prison… there is nothing either good or bad but thinking makes it so") crystallizes the act's subjectivism: the same court is a home to Claudius, a career to Polonius, and a dungeon to Hamlet.
- **Fathers and children as instruments** — Polonius slanders his son to check up on him and offers to "loose" his daughter as bait; Claudius deploys Hamlet's oldest friends against him. The act quietly indicts a world in which children are managed, monitored, and spent by their elders.

## Modern Translation

### Act II, Scene 1

> *Scene: A room in Polonius's house.*

*[Enter Polonius and Reynaldo.]*

**POLONIUS:** Give him this money and these notes, Reynaldo.

**REYNALDO:** I will, my lord.

**POLONIUS:** And you'd be acting very shrewdly, good Reynaldo, if before you visit him you asked around about how he's been behaving.

**REYNALDO:** My lord, I was planning to.

**POLONIUS:** Well said — very well said. Look here, sir. First find out for me what Danes are living in Paris: how they live, who they are, what money they have, where they lodge, what company they keep, what they spend. And when, by this circling, drifting line of questioning, you discover that they know my son, then move in closer than your general questions would ever get you. Pretend to some distant acquaintance with him — say, "I know his father and his friends, and I know him a little." Are you following this, Reynaldo?

**REYNALDO:** Yes, very well, my lord.

**POLONIUS:** "I know him a little — but," you might add, "not well. But if it's the man I'm thinking of, he's very wild, addicted to this and that" — and there you can pin on him whatever inventions you please. Well — nothing so foul it would disgrace him; be careful of that. Just the loose, wild, ordinary lapses that everyone knows go along with youth and freedom.

**REYNALDO:** Such as gambling, my lord.

**POLONIUS:** Yes — or drinking, duelling, swearing, quarrelling, whoring. You can go that far.

**REYNALDO:** My lord, that would disgrace him.

**POLONIUS:** No, honestly — not if you soften it in the telling. You mustn't add the further scandal that he's given over to sexual excess; that's not what I mean at all. Mention his faults so delicately that they seem the stains of a free life, the flash and outburst of a fiery mind, a wildness in untamed blood that takes everyone in his years.

**REYNALDO:** But, my good lord —

**POLONIUS:** Why should you do all this?

**REYNALDO:** Yes, my lord — that's what I'd like to know.

**POLONIUS:** Well, sir, here's my aim, and I believe it's a perfectly legitimate trick. When you've smudged my son with these slight stains — as if he were an article a little soiled in the handling — pay attention now — then the man you're talking to, the one you want to sound out, if he has ever actually seen the young man guilty of the aforesaid crimes, you can be sure he'll close with you and agree, saying "Good sir," or "friend," or "gentleman" — whatever form of address suits the man and the country.

**REYNALDO:** Very good, my lord.

**POLONIUS:** And then, sir, he does this — he does — what was I about to say? By the mass, I was about to say something. Where did I leave off?

**REYNALDO:** At "closes with you and agrees," at "friend or so," and "gentleman."

**POLONIUS:** At "closes with you and agrees" — yes, exactly! He'll close with you like this: "I know the gentleman; I saw him yesterday, or the other day, or this time or that, with so-and-so; and just as you say, there he was gambling; there he was caught drunk; there he got into a brawl at tennis." Or perhaps, "I saw him going into a certain house of trade" — that is to say, a brothel — and so on. Do you see it now? Your bait of falsehood catches this carp of truth. And that's how we men of wisdom and far sight, by roundabout casts and angled approaches, find the direct way by indirection. So, following my lecture and advice, you'll find out my son. You've got it, haven't you?

**REYNALDO:** My lord, I have.

**POLONIUS:** God be with you. Fare you well.

**REYNALDO:** My good lord!

**POLONIUS:** And observe his inclinations for yourself.

**REYNALDO:** I shall, my lord.

**POLONIUS:** And let him keep at his music.

**REYNALDO:** Very well, my lord.

**POLONIUS:** Farewell!

*[Exit Reynaldo.]*

*[Enter Ophelia.]*

What now, Ophelia — what's the matter?

**OPHELIA:** Oh, my lord, my lord, I've had such a fright!

**POLONIUS:** With what, in God's name?

**OPHELIA:** My lord, as I was sewing in my room, Lord Hamlet came before me — his jacket all unlaced, no hat on his head, his stockings filthy and ungartered, fallen down around his ankles like a prisoner's shackles; pale as his shirt, his knees knocking together, and with a look so pitiful in its meaning that it was as if he had been let out of hell to tell of its horrors.

**POLONIUS:** Mad for love of you?

**OPHELIA:** My lord, I don't know — but truly, I'm afraid of it.

**POLONIUS:** What did he say?

**OPHELIA:** He took me by the wrist and held me hard. Then he steps back the full length of his arm, and with his other hand held over his brow — like this — he falls to studying my face as if he meant to draw it. He stayed like that a long time. At last, giving my arm a little shake, and nodding his head up and down three times — like this — he heaved a sigh so pitiful and deep that it seemed to shatter his whole frame and end his life. That done, he lets me go, and with his head turned back over his shoulder he seemed to find his way without his eyes — he went out the door without their help, and kept their light fixed on me to the very last.

**POLONIUS:** Come, go with me. I'll go find the king. This is the true frenzy of love, whose violent nature destroys itself and drives the will to desperate acts, as often as any passion under heaven that afflicts our natures. I'm sorry — tell me, have you spoken harshly to him lately?

**OPHELIA:** No, my good lord. But as you commanded, I sent back his letters and refused to let him see me.

**POLONIUS:** That is what has made him mad. I'm sorry I didn't watch him with better care and judgment. I feared he was only trifling with you and meant to ruin you — but damn my suspicious mind! It seems it's as natural to us old men to overshoot the mark with our suspicions as it is common for the young to lack discretion. Come, let's go to the king. This has to be made known: kept secret, it might cause more grief than the telling of this love will cause anger.

*[Exeunt.]*

### Act II, Scene 2

> *Scene: A room in the Castle.*

*[Enter King, Rosencrantz, Guildenstern, and Attendants.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Welcome, dear Rosencrantz and Guildenstern! Besides the fact that we've long wanted to see you, it was our need of your services that prompted this hasty summons. You've heard something of Hamlet's transformation — I call it that because neither the outer nor the inner man resembles what he was. What it could be, other than his father's death, that has put him so far out of his own understanding, I cannot begin to guess. I beg you both — since you were brought up with him from such early years, and have stayed so close to his youth and his temperament — to consent to remain here at our court a little while, so that your company may draw him out toward his old pleasures, and so that you may gather, from whatever opportunity offers, whether anything unknown to us is afflicting him — something that, once brought into the open, lies within our power to cure.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Good gentlemen, he has talked of you constantly, and I'm certain there are no two men living he's more attached to. If you'll be so courteous and kind as to spend your time with us a while, to serve and further our hopes, your visit will receive the kind of thanks that befits a king's gratitude.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Both your majesties might, by the sovereign power you hold over us, put your dread wishes as commands rather than requests.

**GUILDENSTERN:** We both obey — and here give ourselves up entirely, laying our service freely at your feet, to be commanded.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Thanks, Rosencrantz and gentle Guildenstern.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Thanks, Guildenstern and gentle Rosencrantz. And I beg you, go at once and visit my too-much-changed son. — Some of you, go and bring these gentlemen to where Hamlet is.

**GUILDENSTERN:** May heaven make our presence and our efforts pleasant and helpful to him!

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Yes — amen!

*[Exeunt Rosencrantz, Guildenstern, and some Attendants.]*

*[Enter Polonius.]*

**POLONIUS:** The ambassadors from Norway, my good lord, have returned — and joyfully.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** You have always been the father of good news.

**POLONIUS:** Have I, my lord? I assure you, my good liege, I hold my duty as I hold my soul — owed both to my God and to my gracious king. And I believe — or else this brain of mine no longer hunts the trail of statecraft as surely as it used to — that I have found the true cause of Hamlet's lunacy.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Oh, speak of that — that is what I long to hear.

**POLONIUS:** First give the ambassadors their audience; my news will be the dessert to that great feast.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Do them the honor yourself, and bring them in.

*[Exit Polonius.]*

He tells me, my sweet queen, that he has found the head and source of all your son's disorder.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** I suspect it's nothing but the obvious cause — his father's death and our over-hasty marriage.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Well — we'll sift him.

*[Enter Polonius, with Voltimand and Cornelius.]*

Welcome, my good friends! Tell me, Voltimand — what news from our brother king of Norway?

**VOLTIMAND:** The fairest return of greetings and good wishes. On our first approach, he sent out orders to suppress his nephew's troop-raising, which he had taken to be a preparation against Poland; but on looking closer he found it was in truth aimed at your highness. Grieved that his sickness, age, and weakness had been so exploited and deceived, he sent out orders for Fortinbras's arrest — which the young man, in short, obeys. He is rebuked by Norway, and in the end vows before his uncle never again to make trial of arms against your majesty. At this, old Norway, overcome with joy, grants him three thousand crowns a year and a commission to employ the soldiers he had raised — against Poland instead. He sends with this a request, set out more fully here — *[gives a paper]* — that it might please you to grant free passage through your lands for this enterprise, under such guarantees of safety and such conditions as are set down in the document.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** This pleases us well. At a more considered time we'll read it, answer it, and think this business over. In the meantime, we thank you for labor well performed. Go take your rest; tonight we'll feast together. Most welcome home!

*[Exeunt Voltimand and Cornelius.]*

**POLONIUS:** That business is nicely concluded. My liege, and madam: to hold forth on what majesty ought to be, what duty is, why day is day, night is night, and time is time, would be nothing but a waste of night, day, and time. Therefore — since brevity is the soul of wit, and tediousness its limbs and outward trimmings — I will be brief. Your noble son is mad. "Mad" I call it — for to define true madness, what is it but to be nothing else but mad? But let that go.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** More matter, with less art.

**POLONIUS:** Madam, I swear I use no art at all. That he is mad — that is true; it is true that it's a pity, and a pity that it's true. A foolish figure of speech — but farewell to it, for I will use no art. Mad, then, let us grant he is. And now it remains that we find out the cause of this effect — or rather say, the cause of this *defect*, for this defective effect comes from a cause. Thus it remains, and the remainder is thus. Consider. I have a daughter — have her while she is still mine — who, in her duty and obedience, mark you, has given me this. Now gather round, and draw your conclusions. *[Reads.]* "To the celestial, and my soul's idol, the most beautified Ophelia" — that's a bad phrase, a vile phrase; "beautified" is a vile phrase. But you shall hear. Thus: *[reads]* "In her excellent white bosom, these, etc."

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Did this come from Hamlet — to her?

**POLONIUS:** Good madam, wait a moment; I will be exact. *[Reads.]*

> Doubt that the stars are fire;
> Doubt that the sun moves in the sky;
> Doubt that truth itself is true;
> But never doubt I love.

"O dear Ophelia, I am no good at these verses; I have no art to measure out my groans. But that I love you best — oh, best beyond best — believe it. Adieu. Yours evermore, most dear lady, as long as this body-machine belongs to him — HAMLET."

This, in her obedience, my daughter has shown me — and more than that, she has confided to my ear all his approaches as they came: when, by what means, and where.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** But how has she received his love?

**POLONIUS:** What do you think of me?

**KING CLAUDIUS:** As a man faithful and honorable.

**POLONIUS:** I would gladly prove so. But what might you have thought, when I had seen this hot love taking wing — and I noticed it, I must tell you, before my daughter told me — what might you, or my dear majesty your queen here, have thought, if I had played the desk or the notebook and merely filed it away? Or winked my heart silent, mute and dumb? Or looked on this love with an idle eye? What might you have thought? No — I went straight to work, and spoke to my young miss plainly: "Lord Hamlet is a prince, out of your orbit. This must not be." And then I gave her instructions: that she should lock herself away from his visits, admit no messengers, accept no gifts. Which done, she took the fruits of my advice; and he, rejected — to make a long story short — fell into a sadness, then into a fast, from that into sleeplessness, from that into weakness, from that into light-headedness, and, by this downward slide, into the madness in which he now raves and which we all mourn.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Do you think this is it?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** It may be — very likely.

**POLONIUS:** Has there ever been a time — I'd like to know — when I have said positively "It is so" and it proved otherwise?

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Not that I know of.

**POLONIUS:** Take this from this, if it prove otherwise. *[Points to his head and shoulder.]* If circumstances lead me, I will find where truth is hidden — even if it were hidden at the very center of the earth.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** How can we test it further?

**POLONIUS:** You know he sometimes walks for hours at a stretch here in the lobby.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** So he does, indeed.

**POLONIUS:** At such a time I'll let my daughter loose to him. You and I will hide behind a tapestry and watch the encounter. If he doesn't love her — if that's not the reason he's fallen from his wits — then let me be no minister of state, but keep a farm and cart-drivers instead.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** We will try it.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** But look — here the poor wretch comes, reading, and looking so sad.

**POLONIUS:** Away, I beg you, both of you — away. I'll accost him at once. Oh, give me room.

*[Exeunt King, Queen, and Attendants.]*

*[Enter Hamlet, reading.]*

How does my good Lord Hamlet?

**HAMLET:** Well, God have mercy on you.

**POLONIUS:** Do you know me, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Extremely well — you're a fishmonger.

**POLONIUS:** Not I, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Then I wish you were as honest a man as one.

**POLONIUS:** Honest, my lord!

**HAMLET:** Yes, sir. To be honest, the way this world goes, is to be one man picked out of ten thousand.

**POLONIUS:** That's very true, my lord.

**HAMLET:** For if the sun breeds maggots in a dead dog — kissing carrion like a god — Have you a daughter?

**POLONIUS:** I have, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Don't let her walk in the sun. Conception is a blessing — but not the kind of conceiving your daughter might do. Friend, see to it.

**POLONIUS:** What do you mean by that? *[Aside.]* Still harping on my daughter. Yet he didn't know me at first — he said I was a fishmonger. He's far gone, far gone. And truly, in my youth I suffered terrible extremes for love — very near to this. I'll speak to him again. — What are you reading, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Words, words, words.

**POLONIUS:** What is the matter, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Between whom?

**POLONIUS:** I mean the matter that you're reading, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Slanders, sir. For this satirical scoundrel says here that old men have grey beards, that their faces are wrinkled, their eyes ooze thick amber and plum-tree gum, and that they have an abundant lack of wit together with very weak hams. All of which, sir, though I most powerfully and potently believe it, I still don't consider it decent to have it written down like this — for you yourself, sir, would be as old as I am, if, like a crab, you could go backwards.

**POLONIUS:** *[Aside.]* Though this be madness, yet there is a method in't. — Will you step out of the air, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Into my grave?

**POLONIUS:** Indeed, that *is* out of the air. *[Aside.]* How full of meaning his replies sometimes are! A knack that madness often hits on, which reason and sanity couldn't deliver half so happily. I'll leave him, and immediately arrange the means for a meeting between him and my daughter. — My honorable lord, I will most humbly take my leave of you.

**HAMLET:** You cannot, sir, take from me anything I would more willingly part with — except my life, except my life, except my life.

**POLONIUS:** Fare you well, my lord.

**HAMLET:** These tedious old fools!

*[Enter Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.]*

**POLONIUS:** You're looking for Lord Hamlet — there he is.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** *[To Polonius.]* God save you, sir!

*[Exit Polonius.]*

**GUILDENSTERN:** My honored lord!

**ROSENCRANTZ:** My most dear lord!

**HAMLET:** My excellent good friends! How are you, Guildenstern? Ah, Rosencrantz! Good lads, how are you both?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Like the average children of the earth.

**GUILDENSTERN:** Happy in that we're not over-happy. On Fortune's cap we are not the very button.

**HAMLET:** Nor the soles of her shoe?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Neither, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Then you live around her waist — in the middle of her favors?

**GUILDENSTERN:** Honestly, we're her private parts.

**HAMLET:** In the secret parts of Fortune? Oh, most true — she's a whore. What's the news?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** None, my lord, except that the world's grown honest.

**HAMLET:** Then doomsday must be near — but your news isn't true. Let me question you more particularly. What have you, my good friends, done to deserve at the hands of Fortune that she sends you here — to prison?

**GUILDENSTERN:** Prison, my lord!

**HAMLET:** Denmark's a prison.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Then the world is one.

**HAMLET:** A fine large one, with many cells, wards, and dungeons in it — Denmark being one of the worst.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** We don't think so, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Why, then it isn't one — to you. For there is nothing either good or bad but thinking makes it so. To me it is a prison.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Why then, it's your ambition that makes it one; it's too narrow for your mind.

**HAMLET:** O God, I could be bounded in a nutshell and count myself a king of infinite space — were it not that I have bad dreams.

**GUILDENSTERN:** Which dreams, in fact, are ambition — for the very substance of the ambitious man is merely the shadow of a dream.

**HAMLET:** A dream itself is only a shadow.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** True — and I consider ambition a thing so airy and light that it's only a shadow's shadow.

**HAMLET:** Then our beggars are the only solid bodies, and our monarchs and strutting heroes are the beggars' shadows. Shall we go in to the court? Because, by my faith, I can't argue any further.

**ROSENCRANTZ and GUILDENSTERN:** We'll wait upon you.

**HAMLET:** No, none of that. I won't class you with the rest of my servants — for, to speak to you like an honest man, I am most dreadfully attended. But — on the plain road of friendship — what are you doing at Elsinore?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** To visit you, my lord; no other reason.

**HAMLET:** Beggar that I am, I'm poor even in thanks — but I do thank you; and believe me, dear friends, my thanks are overpriced at a halfpenny. Weren't you sent for? Is this your own idea? Is it a voluntary visit? Come, deal fairly with me. Come, come — no, speak.

**GUILDENSTERN:** What should we say, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Why, anything — as long as it's to the point. You *were* sent for; and there's a kind of confession in your faces which your modesty hasn't craft enough to disguise. I know the good king and queen sent for you.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** For what purpose, my lord?

**HAMLET:** That's what you must teach me. But let me entreat you — by the rights of our fellowship, by the harmony of our youth, by the obligation of our always-preserved love, and by whatever dearer plea a better pleader could press on you — be straight and direct with me: were you sent for or not?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** *[To Guildenstern.]* What do you say?

**HAMLET:** *[Aside.]* Ah, so — I've got my eye on you. — If you love me, don't hold back.

**GUILDENSTERN:** My lord, we were sent for.

**HAMLET:** I'll tell you why — that way my guessing first will spare you the disclosure, and your promise of secrecy to the king and queen won't shed a feather. I have lately — though why, I don't know — lost all my cheerfulness, given up all my customary exercise; and indeed things sit so heavily on my spirits that this goodly frame, the earth, seems to me a barren headland; this most excellent canopy, the air — look at it — this brave overhanging sky, this majestic roof studded with golden fire — why, it appears to me nothing but a foul and pestilent congregation of vapors. What a piece of work is man! How noble in reason! How infinite in faculties! In form and movement, how precise and admirable! In action, how like an angel! In understanding, how like a god! The beauty of the world! The paragon of animals! And yet, to me, what is this quintessence of dust? Man delights not me — no, nor woman neither, though by your smiling you seem to say otherwise.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** My lord, there was no such thought in my head.

**HAMLET:** Then why did you laugh when I said "Man delights not me"?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** I was thinking, my lord, that if men give you no delight, what a meager reception the players are going to get from you. We passed them on the road; they're on their way here to offer you their services.

**HAMLET:** The one who plays the king shall be welcome — his majesty will have tribute from me. The adventurous knight shall use his sword and shield; the lover shall not sigh for free; the eccentric character shall finish his part in peace; the clown shall set laughing everyone whose lungs are on a hair-trigger; and the lady shall speak her mind freely, or the blank verse will limp for it. What players are they?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** The very ones you used to take such delight in — the tragedians of the city.

**HAMLET:** How does it happen that they're touring? Staying put in the city was better for them, both for reputation and for profit.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** I think their being kept from the city comes of the recent innovation.

**HAMLET:** Are they as highly regarded as they were when I was in the city? Are they as well attended?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** No, indeed they're not.

**HAMLET:** How's that? Are they getting rusty?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** No, their work keeps up its usual standard. But there is, sir, a nest of children — little unfledged hawks — who shriek out their lines at the top of the argument and are most tyrannically applauded for it. These are now the fashion; and they so rattle the public playhouses — that's what they call them — that many a gentleman wearing a sword is afraid of the goose-quills of the satirists, and hardly dares go there.

**HAMLET:** What, are they children? Who maintains them? How are they paid? Will they stay in the profession only as long as their voices can sing? And won't they say later — if they grow up into common players themselves, which is most likely if they have no better prospects — that their writers did them wrong, making them cry down the very profession they'll inherit?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Truthfully, there's been a great to-do on both sides, and the nation sees no sin in egging them into the quarrel. For a while, no one would pay money for a play unless the poet and the actor came to blows over the question.

**HAMLET:** Is that possible?

**GUILDENSTERN:** Oh, there's been a great throwing about of brains.

**HAMLET:** And do the boys carry the day?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Yes, they do, my lord — Hercules and his globe along with it.

**HAMLET:** It's not so very strange. My uncle is King of Denmark, and the very people who used to make faces at him while my father lived now pay twenty, forty, fifty, a hundred ducats apiece for his miniature portrait. By God's blood, there's something in this more than natural — if philosophy could only work it out.

*[Flourish of trumpets within.]*

**GUILDENSTERN:** There are the players.

**HAMLET:** Gentlemen, you are welcome to Elsinore. Your hands — come. Fashion and ceremony are the accessories of welcome, so let me do the courtesies with you in this style — otherwise my warmth toward the players, which I tell you must make a fair show, might look like a better reception than yours. You are welcome. But my uncle-father and aunt-mother are deceived.

**GUILDENSTERN:** In what, my dear lord?

**HAMLET:** I am but mad north-north-west. When the wind is southerly, I know a hawk from a handsaw.

*[Enter Polonius.]*

**POLONIUS:** All good be with you, gentlemen!

**HAMLET:** Listen, Guildenstern — and you too — a listener at each ear: that great baby you see there is not yet out of his swaddling clothes.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Perhaps he's come round to them a second time; they say an old man is twice a child.

**HAMLET:** I'll prophesy: he's coming to tell me about the players — watch. — You're quite right, sir; on Monday morning, that's just when it was.

**POLONIUS:** My lord, I have news to tell you.

**HAMLET:** My lord, *I* have news to tell *you*. When Roscius was an actor in Rome —

**POLONIUS:** The actors have arrived here, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Buzz, buzz!

**POLONIUS:** On my honor —

**HAMLET:** "Then came each actor riding on his ass" —

**POLONIUS:** The best actors in the world — for tragedy, comedy, history, pastoral, pastoral-comical, historical-pastoral, tragical-historical, tragical-comical-historical-pastoral; for plays that observe the unities or poems without limit. Seneca cannot be too heavy for them, nor Plautus too light. Whether for scripted rules or free improvisation, these are the only men.

**HAMLET:** O Jephthah, judge of Israel — what a treasure you had!

**POLONIUS:** What treasure did he have, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Why —

> One fair daughter, and no more,
> The one he loved surpassing well.

**POLONIUS:** *[Aside.]* Still on my daughter.

**HAMLET:** Am I not right, old Jephthah?

**POLONIUS:** If you call me Jephthah, my lord, I do have a daughter I love surpassing well.

**HAMLET:** No, that doesn't follow.

**POLONIUS:** What follows, then, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Why —

> As by lot, God knows,

and then, you know,

> It came to pass, as most likely it would —

The first verse of the pious ballad will show you the rest — for look, here comes what cuts me short.

*[Enter four or five Players.]*

You are welcome, masters — welcome, all. — I'm glad to see you well. — Welcome, good friends. — Oh, my old friend! Your face has grown a fringe of beard since I saw you last — have you come to beard me in Denmark? — What, my young lady and mistress! By our Lady, your ladyship is nearer to heaven than when I saw you last, by the height of a platform shoe. Pray God your voice, like a gold coin no longer current, hasn't cracked within the ring. — Masters, you are all welcome. We'll get right to it, like French falconers — fly at the first thing we see. Let's have a speech straight away. Come, give us a taste of your quality — come, a passionate speech.

**FIRST PLAYER:** What speech, my good lord?

**HAMLET:** I heard you deliver a speech once — but it was never acted, or if it was, not more than once; for the play, I remember, didn't please the crowd. It was caviar to the general public. But it was — as I took it, and others whose judgment in such matters outranked mine — an excellent play: well constructed in its scenes, written with as much restraint as skill. I remember someone said there were no spicy bits in the lines to make the matter tasty, nor anything in the phrasing that could convict the author of affectation — he called it an honest method, as wholesome as it was sweet, and far more handsome than showy. One speech in it I especially loved: Aeneas's tale to Dido, and in it particularly where he speaks of Priam's slaughter. If it lives in your memory, begin at this line — let me see, let me see —

"The rugged Pyrrhus, like the Hyrcanian beast" —

no, that's not it — it begins with Pyrrhus —

> The rugged Pyrrhus — he whose black-clad arms,
> dark as his purpose, resembled the night
> when he lay hidden in the fatal horse —
> has now smeared that dread black complexion
> with grimmer heraldry: head to foot
> he is now solid red, hideously painted
> with the blood of fathers, mothers, daughters, sons,
> baked and crusted on him by the burning streets
> that cast a tyrannous and damned light
> on their owners' vile murders. Roasted in wrath and fire,
> and plastered over with congealing gore,
> his eyes like red-hot jewels, hellish Pyrrhus
> hunts old grandfather Priam.

So — you take it from there.

**POLONIUS:** Before God, my lord, well spoken — with good delivery and good taste.

**FIRST PLAYER:**

> Soon he finds him,
> striking too short at Greeks. His antique sword,
> rebellious to his arm, lies where it falls,
> deaf to command. Unequally matched,
> Pyrrhus drives at Priam; in his rage he swings wide,
> but the mere rush and wind of his cruel sword
> fells the unstrung old father. Then lifeless Troy,
> as if it felt the blow, its towers in flame,
> crashes down to its foundations — and the hideous roar
> takes Pyrrhus's ear prisoner: for look — his sword,
> which was descending on the milk-white head
> of venerable Priam, seemed to stick in the air.
> So Pyrrhus stood, like a tyrant in a painting,
> and, as if neutral between his will and its object,
> did nothing.
> But as we often see before a storm
> a silence in the heavens — the cloud-rack still,
> the bold winds speechless, and the earth below
> as hushed as death — then suddenly the dreadful thunder
> tears the sky apart: so, after Pyrrhus's pause,
> a roused vengeance sets him back to work;
> and never did the Cyclops' hammers fall
> on Mars's armor, forged to be proof for eternity,
> with less remorse than Pyrrhus's bleeding sword
> now falls on Priam. —
> Out, out, you whore, Fortune! All you gods,
> in full assembly, take away her power!
> Break all the spokes and rims from her wheel,
> and roll its round hub down the hill of heaven
> as low as to the fiends!

**POLONIUS:** This is too long.

**HAMLET:** Then it can go to the barber's — along with your beard. — Please, keep going. He's for a jig or a dirty story, or he falls asleep. Keep going — come to Hecuba.

**FIRST PLAYER:**

> But who — oh, who — had seen the muffled queen —

**HAMLET:** "The muffled queen"?

**POLONIUS:** That's good! "Muffled queen" is good.

**FIRST PLAYER:**

> — run barefoot up and down, threatening the flames
> with blinding tears; a rag upon that head
> where the crown so lately stood; and for a robe,
> around her thin, worn-out, child-wasted hips,
> a blanket, snatched up in the panic of alarm —
> whoever had seen this, with a tongue steeped in venom,
> would have cried treason against the rule of Fortune.
> But if the gods themselves had seen her then,
> when she watched Pyrrhus make his malicious sport
> of mincing her husband's limbs with his sword,
> the instant burst of outcry that she made —
> unless mortal things do not move them at all —
> would have drawn milk-tears from the burning eyes of heaven
> and grief from the gods themselves.

**POLONIUS:** Look — see if he hasn't changed color, and has tears in his eyes. — Please, no more!

**HAMLET:** It's well done. I'll have you speak the rest of this soon. — My good lord, will you see the players well lodged? Do you hear me? Let them be well treated, for they are the summaries and living chronicles of the age. You'd be better off with a bad epitaph after your death than with their bad report of you while you live.

**POLONIUS:** My lord, I will treat them according to what they deserve.

**HAMLET:** God's little body, man — better than that! Treat every man according to what he deserves, and who would escape a whipping? Treat them according to your own honor and dignity: the less they deserve, the more merit there is in your generosity. Take them in.

**POLONIUS:** Come, sirs.

**HAMLET:** Follow him, friends. We'll hear a play tomorrow.

*[Exeunt Polonius with all the Players but the First.]*

Listen to me, old friend — can you play "The Murder of Gonzago"?

**FIRST PLAYER:** Yes, my lord.

**HAMLET:** We'll have it tomorrow night. And you could, if needed, learn a speech of some twelve or sixteen lines which I would write out and insert into it — couldn't you?

**FIRST PLAYER:** Yes, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Very well. Follow that lord — and see that you don't mock him.

*[Exit First Player.]*

— My good friends *[to Rosencrantz and Guildenstern]*, I'll leave you till tonight. You are welcome to Elsinore.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** My good lord!

*[Exeunt Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.]*

**HAMLET:** Yes, yes — God be with you! Now I am alone.

Oh, what a rogue and peasant slave am I! Isn't it monstrous that this actor here, in nothing but a fiction, a dream of passion, could force his soul so completely into his own imagining that its working drained all the color from his face — tears in his eyes, wildness in his look, his voice breaking, his whole body shaping itself to match the idea? And all for nothing! For Hecuba! What's Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba, that he should weep for her? What would he do if he had the motive and the cue for passion that I have? He would drown the stage with tears, and split the ears of the whole audience with horrifying speech; drive the guilty mad and appall the innocent; confound the ignorant and stun, truly, the very faculties of eyes and ears. Yet I — a dull, mud-blooded rascal — mope about like John-a-dreams, empty of my own cause, and can say nothing. Nothing — not even for a king whose property and most dear life were destroyed by a damnable stroke. Am I a coward? Who calls me villain? Cracks my skull? Plucks off my beard and blows it in my face? Tweaks me by the nose? Calls me a liar and rams the word down my throat as deep as my lungs? Who does this to me? Ha! By God's wounds, I'd have to take it — for it can only be that I am pigeon-livered, and lack the gall to make oppression taste bitter; otherwise, before now, I'd have fattened every kite in the sky with this slave's guts. Bloody, bawdy villain! Remorseless, treacherous, lecherous, unnatural villain! Oh, vengeance!

Why, what an ass I am! How very brave this is — that I, the son of a dear father murdered, prompted to my revenge by heaven and hell, must, like a whore, unpack my heart with words and fall to cursing like a common streetwalker, a kitchen wench! Damn it! Ugh! — Get to work, my brain! Hmm — I have heard that guilty creatures, sitting at a play, have been struck to the soul by the sheer cunning of the scene, so that on the spot they have proclaimed their own crimes. For murder, though it has no tongue, will speak with a most miraculous voice. I'll have these players play something like the murder of my father before my uncle. I'll watch his face; I'll probe him to the raw flesh: if he so much as flinches, I know my course. The spirit that I have seen may be the devil — and the devil has the power to assume a pleasing shape; yes, and perhaps, out of my weakness and my melancholy — since he is very powerful with such moods — he is deceiving me in order to damn me. I'll have grounds more solid than this. The play's the thing wherein I'll catch the conscience of the king.

*[Exit.]*
