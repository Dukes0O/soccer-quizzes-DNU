# Act III

> William Shakespeare, *Hamlet, Prince of Denmark* (c. 1601) · Act III, Scenes 1–4
>
> [← Act II](02-act-ii.md) · [Book index](../README.md) · [Act IV →](04-act-iv.md)

## Overview

Act III is the hinge of the whole play — the act where every scheme launched in Act II collides with reality. It opens with the famous "nunnery scene": Claudius and Polonius plant Ophelia in Hamlet's path and hide to watch, hoping to confirm that frustrated love is the cause of his madness. Instead they get the "To be, or not to be" soliloquy — Hamlet weighing existence itself — followed by a brutal encounter in which he savages Ophelia, marriage, and womankind. Claudius draws the right conclusion for the wrong reasons: whatever ails Hamlet, it isn't love, and it's dangerous. He decides to ship Hamlet to England.

Scene 2 delivers the play-within-the-play. Hamlet coaches the actors, confides his plan to Horatio, and then stage-manages "The Mousetrap" — a performance of a king murdered by poison poured in his ear, whose murderer then wins the widow. Claudius watches his own crime re-enacted, breaks, and storms out calling for light. For Hamlet and Horatio, the ghost's story is confirmed: Claudius is guilty.

Scene 3 gives Hamlet his one clean chance at revenge — and he refuses it. Claudius, alone, tries to pray, and in the play's most searching confession admits the murder while conceding he cannot repent, since he still keeps everything the crime bought him. Hamlet finds him kneeling, draws his sword, and then sheathes it: killing Claudius at prayer, he reasons, would send his soul to heaven — no revenge at all. The bitter irony lands in Claudius's closing couplet: his prayer never got off the ground anyway.

Scene 4, the "closet scene," turns confirmation into catastrophe. Hamlet confronts his mother so violently that she cries for help; Polonius, hidden behind the tapestry, cries out too, and Hamlet runs him through, thinking — or hoping — it is the king. Over the corpse, Hamlet forces Gertrude to compare her two husbands and face what she has done, until the Ghost appears (visible to Hamlet alone) to re-sharpen his "almost blunted purpose." Hamlet exits dragging Polonius's body, now a killer himself, with the trip to England — and his two false friends carrying sealed letters — waiting for him.

## Key Concepts

- **Surveillance and the watched stage** — Nearly every scene is an eavesdropping operation: Claudius and Polonius behind a curtain watching Ophelia and Hamlet; Hamlet and Horatio watching Claudius watch the play; Polonius behind the arras in the Queen's room. The act asks who is really observing whom — and Polonius dies of the game.
- **Theater as a moral instrument** — Hamlet's advice to the players ("hold the mirror up to nature") and "The Mousetrap" itself develop the idea that performance can expose truth that interrogation cannot. Fiction catches the conscience of the king when spies could not catch Hamlet's.
- **Revenge and delay** — Act III gives the delay its most explicit moment: Hamlet standing over the praying Claudius with a drawn sword and choosing not to strike. Whether his theological reasoning is genuine or a rationalization is the play's central open question, and the Ghost returns precisely to accuse him of letting the command slide.
- **Conscience and the impossibility of repentance** — "To be, or not to be" argues that conscience (both moral scruple and reflective thought) paralyzes action; Claudius's prayer scene is the mirror image — full knowledge of guilt, full inability to give up its rewards. "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below."
- **Seeming vs. being** — Polonius sugaring over villainy with a devotional pose, Claudius's "painted word," Ophelia deployed as a prop with a prayer book, Hamlet's "mad in craft": the act is saturated with false faces, and Hamlet's rage at "paintings" and pretense boils over onto Ophelia and Gertrude.
- **Madness, real and feigned** — Rosencrantz and Guildenstern report a "crafty madness"; Claudius concludes what Hamlet said "was not like madness"; Gertrude decides the Ghost is "the very coinage of your brain"; and Hamlet insists to her face that he is "not in madness, but mad in craft." Everyone in the act is forced to take a position on the question.
- **Words versus deeds** — Hamlet resolves to "speak daggers but use none" to his mother — and within minutes has used a real one on Polonius. Claudius's words rise while his thoughts stay below. The gap between saying and doing kills its first bystander in this act.
- **The body politic** — Rosencrantz's "massy wheel" speech articulates the era's doctrine that a king's death drags down everything mortised to it — dramatic irony, since the wheel is already falling, and Rosencrantz has hitched himself to it.

## Archaic Terms

Words and usages in this act that have fallen out of use or shifted meaning.

| Term | Meaning in Shakespeare's usage |
|---|---|
| nunnery | A convent — but in Elizabethan slang also a brothel; Hamlet's repeated "get thee to a nunnery" cuts both ways, and no single modern word keeps both edges. |
| honest | Of a woman: chaste, virtuous — not merely truthful. Hamlet's "are you honest?" asks Ophelia if she is chaste. |
| bodkin | A dagger or stiletto — "a bare bodkin" is an unsheathed blade, the suicide's tool. |
| quietus | Legal term for the settling of an account; Hamlet uses it for the final discharge of life's debts — death. |
| fardels | Burdens, heavy packs. |
| bourn | Boundary, limit — death is "the undiscovered country from whose bourn no traveller returns." |
| orisons | Prayers. |
| espials | Spies — Claudius calls himself and Polonius "lawful espials." |
| groundlings | The spectators who paid a penny to stand in the pit of the theater, at the actors' feet. |
| Termagant / Herod | Ranting stock villains of the old mystery plays; to "out-herod Herod" is to overact even more outrageously than the archetype of overacting. |
| country matters | Sexual intercourse — an obscene pun on the first syllable of "country," aimed at Ophelia; "nothing" in the same exchange was slang for the female anatomy. |
| jig-maker | A composer of comic song-and-dance afterpieces — Hamlet's bitter "your only jig-maker" means "the court's best clown, me." |
| hobby-horse | A mock horse worn by a dancer in May games; a fading folk custom, hence the ballad line "the hobby-horse is forgot" for something once loved, now abandoned. |
| miching mallecho | Sneaking mischief, skulking villainy (from Spanish *malhecho*, misdeed). |
| galled jade / withers unwrung | A *jade* is a worn-out horse; one *galled* is chafed to sores; *withers* are a horse's shoulders. Hamlet's proverb: let the sore horse flinch — our shoulders are unhurt, i.e., only the guilty will squirm. |
| chorus | The actor who explained a play's action to the audience — Ophelia's dry compliment when Hamlet keeps narrating. |
| pajock | Probably "peacock" (a vain, lecherous bird) — the insulting word Hamlet substitutes where the rhyme demands "ass." |
| recorder | A simple wooden flute; its *ventages* are the finger-holes, its *stops* the fingerings. Hamlet also puns on *fret* — the ridge on a lute's neck and "to irritate." |
| closet | A private chamber or sitting room — not a wardrobe. The Queen's "closet" is her private apartment. |
| arras | A hanging tapestry screening a wall, with space behind it to hide — where Polonius conceals himself and dies. |
| rood | The cross of Christ; "by the rood" is a mild oath. |
| ecstasy | Madness, frenzy — a fit that takes one out of oneself; not rapture or delight. |
| unction | A soothing ointment; a "flattering unction" is a comforting self-deception applied to the soul. |
| petard | A small bomb for blowing in gates; the *enginer* (military engineer) "hoist with his own petard" is blown up by his own device. |
| scourge and minister | Heaven's whip and heaven's agent — Hamlet's terms for his own appointed role as both punisher and instrument. |

## Modern Translation

### Act III, Scene 1

> *Scene: A room in the Castle.*

*[Enter King, Queen, Polonius, Ophelia, Rosencrantz, and Guildenstern.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** And you can't, by any roundabout steering of the conversation, get out of him why he puts on this confusion — grinding up all his peaceful days with such turbulent and dangerous lunacy?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** He does admit he feels unsettled in his mind, but what's causing it he absolutely will not say.

**GUILDENSTERN:** And we don't find him willing to be probed. Whenever we try to lead him toward some admission of his true state, he slips away from us with a kind of crafty madness.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Did he receive you well?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Like a perfect gentleman.

**GUILDENSTERN:** But he was forcing himself to be sociable — you could tell.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Stingy with questions of his own, but very free in answering ours.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Did you tempt him toward any entertainment?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Madam, it happened that we overtook a company of actors on the road here. We told him about them, and it seemed to spark a kind of joy in him. They're around the court now, and I believe they've already been engaged to perform for him tonight.

**POLONIUS:** That's quite true; and he asked me to invite your majesties to hear and see the piece.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** With all my heart — and it genuinely pleases me to hear he's inclined that way. Good gentlemen, sharpen his appetite further, and keep pushing him on toward these amusements.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** We shall, my lord.

*[Exit Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Sweet Gertrude, leave us too, because we've secretly sent for Hamlet to come here, so that he can run into Ophelia as if by accident. Her father and I — legitimate spies, you might say — will position ourselves where we can see without being seen, judge the encounter honestly, and gather from his behavior whether or not it's the torment of love that makes him suffer like this.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** I'll do as you say. And for your part, Ophelia — I do hope it's your sweet face that turns out to be the happy cause of Hamlet's wildness. Then I could hope your virtues will bring him back to his usual self, to the credit of you both.

**OPHELIA:** Madam, I wish it may.

*[Exit Queen.]*

**POLONIUS:** Ophelia, walk here. — Gracious sir, if you please, we'll take our places. *[To Ophelia.]* Read from this prayer book — a show of devotion like that will explain why you're alone. We're all guilty of this — it's been proven too often — with a pious face and a devout posture we can sugar-coat the Devil himself.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** *[Aside.]* Oh, that's too true! What a stinging lash that remark lays across my conscience! A whore's cheek, prettied up with layers of makeup, is no uglier next to the paint that helps it than my deed is next to my most painted words. Oh, heavy burden!

**POLONIUS:** I hear him coming. Let's withdraw, my lord.

*[Exit King and Polonius.]*

*[Enter Hamlet.]*

**HAMLET:** To be, or not to be — that is the question. Whether it's nobler in the mind to endure the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune, or to take up arms against a sea of troubles and, by fighting, end them. To die — to sleep — nothing more; and if by sleeping we could say we end the heartache and the thousand natural shocks that flesh inherits — that's an ending to be prayed for. To die, to sleep. To sleep — perhaps to dream. Yes, there's the catch: because in that sleep of death, what dreams might come, once we've shuffled off this mortal coil, has to make us stop and think. That's the consideration that makes misery last so long a lifetime. Who would put up with the whips and scorns of the age — the oppressor's abuse, the arrogant man's insults, the agony of rejected love, the law's endless delays, the insolence of officials, and all the kicks that patient merit takes from the worthless — when he could settle his own account with one bare dagger? Who would carry these burdens, grunting and sweating through an exhausting life, if it weren't that the dread of something after death — that undiscovered country from whose border no traveller returns — bewilders the will, and makes us rather bear the ills we have than fly to others we know nothing of? So conscience does make cowards of us all; and so the natural color of resolution gets sicklied over with the pale tint of thinking, and enterprises of great weight and consequence have their currents pushed off course by this one consideration, and lose the name of action. — Quiet, now! The fair Ophelia! — Nymph, in your prayers, may all my sins be remembered.

**OPHELIA:** My good lord, how has your honor been, all these days?

**HAMLET:** I humbly thank you — well, well, well.

**OPHELIA:** My lord, I have keepsakes of yours that I've long been wanting to return. Please, take them back now.

**HAMLET:** No, not I. I never gave you anything.

**OPHELIA:** My honored lord, you know perfectly well you did — and with them, words composed of such sweet breath that they made the gifts richer still. Their perfume is gone now; take them back. To a noble mind, rich gifts turn poor when the giver turns unkind. There, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Ha, ha! Are you virtuous?

**OPHELIA:** My lord?

**HAMLET:** Are you beautiful?

**OPHELIA:** What does your lordship mean?

**HAMLET:** That if you're virtuous and beautiful, your virtue should allow no dealings with your beauty.

**OPHELIA:** Could beauty, my lord, keep better company than with virtue?

**HAMLET:** Oh yes, truly — because the power of beauty will turn virtue into a pimp faster than the force of virtue can make beauty resemble itself. This used to sound like a paradox, but the present age has proved it. I did love you once.

**OPHELIA:** Indeed, my lord, you made me believe so.

**HAMLET:** You shouldn't have believed me — virtue can be grafted onto our old sinful stock, but the original taste comes through. I didn't love you.

**OPHELIA:** Then I was deceived all the more.

**HAMLET:** Get yourself to a nunnery. Why would you want to breed sinners? I'm reasonably decent myself, and yet I could accuse myself of such things that it would have been better if my mother had never given birth to me. I am very proud, vengeful, ambitious — with more offenses on call than I have thoughts to put them in, imagination to give them shape, or time to commit them. What should creatures like me be doing, crawling between earth and heaven? We're complete scoundrels, all of us. Believe none of us. Take yourself off to a nunnery. Where's your father?

**OPHELIA:** At home, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Then lock the doors on him, so he can play the fool nowhere but in his own house. Farewell.

**OPHELIA:** Oh, help him, you sweet heavens!

**HAMLET:** If you marry, I'll give you this curse for a dowry: be as chaste as ice, as pure as snow, and you still won't escape slander. Get yourself to a nunnery — go, farewell. Or if you absolutely must marry, marry a fool, because wise men know perfectly well what monsters you make of them. To a nunnery, go — and quickly, too. Farewell.

**OPHELIA:** Oh, heavenly powers, restore him!

**HAMLET:** I've heard all about your face-painting too, plenty. God gave you one face, and you make yourselves another. You strut, you mince, you put on lisps, you give God's creatures cute nicknames, and you pass off your loose behavior as innocent ignorance. Enough — I'll have no more of it; it's driven me mad. I tell you, we'll have no more marriages. Those who are married already — all but one — shall live; the rest will stay as they are. To a nunnery, go.

*[Exit.]*

**OPHELIA:** Oh, what a noble mind is ruined here! The courtier's eye, the scholar's tongue, the soldier's sword; the hope and flower of our fair kingdom; the mirror of fashion and the mold of good breeding; the man all watchers watched — utterly, utterly destroyed! And I, most dejected and wretched of women, who tasted the honey of his musical vows, now watch that noble, sovereign intellect jangling like sweet bells knocked out of tune — that matchless form and figure of youth in full bloom blasted by madness. Oh, misery is mine — to have seen what I have seen, and see what I see now!

*[Enter King and Polonius.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Love! His feelings aren't running in that direction. And what he said, though it was a bit shapeless, wasn't like madness. There's something in his soul that his melancholy sits brooding over, and I suspect that what hatches out of it will be dangerous. To head that off, I've made a quick decision and settled it: he goes to England, at once, to demand the tribute they've neglected to pay us. With luck, the sea and different countries and a change of scenery will drive out this thing that's lodged in his heart — the thing his brain keeps hammering at until it knocks him out of his own character. What do you think of it?

**POLONIUS:** It should work well. But I still believe the origin and starting point of his grief was rejected love. — Well now, Ophelia! You needn't tell us what Lord Hamlet said; we heard it all. — My lord, do as you please; but if you think it wise, after the play let his mother the queen get him alone and press him to reveal his grief. Let her be blunt with him. And I'll station myself — with your permission — where I can hear every word of their conversation. If she can't get to the bottom of him, then send him to England, or lock him up wherever your wisdom thinks best.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** So it shall be. Madness in the great must not go unwatched.

*[Exit all.]*

### Act III, Scene 2

> *Scene: A hall in the Castle.*

*[Enter Hamlet and certain Players.]*

**HAMLET:** Deliver the speech, I beg you, the way I performed it for you — trippingly, on the tongue. But if you bellow it, the way plenty of your actors do, I'd just as soon have the town crier speak my lines. And don't saw the air with your hand like this — use everything with restraint. Because even in the very torrent, tempest — the whirlwind, you might say — of passion, you must acquire and cultivate a control that gives it smoothness. Oh, it offends me to the soul to hear a lung-powered fellow in a wig tear a passion to tatters, to absolute rags, to split the ears of the groundlings — who, for the most part, can't appreciate anything but incomprehensible pantomime and noise. I'd have a fellow like that whipped for out-ranting Termagant. It out-Herods Herod. Please — avoid it.

**FIRST PLAYER:** I promise you, your honor.

**HAMLET:** But don't be too tame either. Let your own judgment be your tutor: fit the action to the word and the word to the action, with this one special rule — that you never overstep the modesty of nature. Anything overdone like that defeats the whole purpose of acting, whose aim, from the beginning until now, was and is to hold the mirror up to nature, as it were: to show virtue her own face, contempt its own image, and the very age and body of the time its true shape and imprint. Overdo this, or come at it feebly, and though it may make the ignorant laugh, it can only make the discerning wince — and the judgment of one such viewer must, by your own reckoning, outweigh a whole theater-full of the others. Oh, there are actors I have seen perform — and heard others praise, and highly — who, not to put it profanely, having neither the accent of Christians nor the walk of Christian, pagan, nor man, strutted and bellowed so badly that I thought some of nature's hired hands had manufactured men, and made them poorly — they imitated humanity so abominably.

**FIRST PLAYER:** I hope we've reformed that reasonably well in our company, sir.

**HAMLET:** Oh, reform it altogether. And make sure those who play your clowns speak no more than is written for them — because there are some who will start laughing themselves to get a crowd of empty-headed spectators laughing too, even while some essential point of the play is waiting to be attended to. That's contemptible, and it shows a pitiful ambition in the fool who does it. Go and get yourselves ready.

*[Exit Players.]*

*[Enter Polonius, Rosencrantz, and Guildenstern.]*

Well now, my lord! Will the king hear this piece of work?

**POLONIUS:** And the queen too — and right away.

**HAMLET:** Tell the players to hurry.

*[Exit Polonius.]*

Will you two help speed them along?

**ROSENCRANTZ AND GUILDENSTERN:** We will, my lord.

*[Exit Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.]*

**HAMLET:** Hello there — Horatio!

*[Enter Horatio.]*

**HORATIO:** Here, sweet lord, at your service.

**HAMLET:** Horatio, you are as fair-minded a man as any my dealings with people ever brought me up against.

**HORATIO:** Oh, my dear lord —

**HAMLET:** No, don't think I'm flattering you. What promotion could I hope to get from you, who have no income but your own good spirits to feed and clothe you? Why would anyone flatter the poor? No — let the sugared tongue lick at ridiculous pomp, and let the knee bend its supple hinges where there's profit to be had from fawning. Listen to me. Ever since my own soul became mistress of her choices and learned to tell one man from another, her choice has marked you out as her own. Because you have been like a man who, in suffering everything, suffers nothing — a man who has taken Fortune's blows and Fortune's rewards with equal thanks. And blessed are those whose passion and judgment are so well blended that they're not a flute for Fortune's finger, to play whatever note she pleases. Give me the man who is not passion's slave, and I will wear him in my heart's core — yes, in my heart of hearts — as I do you. But enough of this. There's a play tonight before the king. One scene of it comes close to the circumstances of my father's death, which I've told you about. I beg you — when you see that part underway, watch my uncle with the very concentration of your soul. If his hidden guilt doesn't flush itself out of cover at one particular speech, then what we saw was a damned ghost, and my imaginings are as filthy as Vulcan's forge. Give him your careful attention. As for me, I'll rivet my eyes to his face, and afterward we'll put our two judgments together in weighing how he looked.

**HORATIO:** Very well, my lord. If he steals anything past me while this play is playing and escapes detection, I'll pay for the theft myself.

**HAMLET:** They're coming to the play. I have to act the fool. Find yourself a place.

*[Danish march. A flourish of trumpets. Enter King, Queen, Polonius, Ophelia, Rosencrantz, Guildenstern, and others.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** How is our nephew Hamlet doing?

**HAMLET:** Excellently, I swear — on the chameleon's diet: I eat air, stuffed full of promises. You can't fatten roosters on that.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** I can make nothing of this answer, Hamlet. These words are not mine.

**HAMLET:** No — nor mine now, once spoken. *[To Polonius.]* My lord, you acted once at the university, you said?

**POLONIUS:** So I did, my lord, and I was considered a good actor.

**HAMLET:** What role did you play?

**POLONIUS:** I played Julius Caesar. I was killed in the Capitol; Brutus killed me.

**HAMLET:** It was a brute part of him, to kill so capital a calf there. — Are the players ready?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Yes, my lord. They're waiting on your pleasure.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Come here, my dear Hamlet; sit by me.

**HAMLET:** No, good mother — here's metal more magnetic.

**POLONIUS:** *[To the King.]* Oh ho! Did you notice that?

**HAMLET:** *[Lying down at Ophelia's feet.]* Lady, shall I lie in your lap?

**OPHELIA:** No, my lord.

**HAMLET:** I mean, my head on your lap?

**OPHELIA:** Yes, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Did you think I meant something cruder — country matters?

**OPHELIA:** I think nothing, my lord.

**HAMLET:** That's a fair thought, to lie between a maiden's legs.

**OPHELIA:** What is, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Nothing.

**OPHELIA:** You are merry, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Who, me?

**OPHELIA:** Yes, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Oh God — your one and only comedy-man! What should a man do but be merry? Just look how cheerful my mother is, and my father died within these two hours.

**OPHELIA:** No — it's twice two months, my lord.

**HAMLET:** That long? Well then, let the devil wear black, because I'm getting myself a suit of expensive furs. Oh heavens! Dead two months, and not forgotten yet? Then there's hope a great man's memory might outlive his life by half a year. But by our Lady, he'd better build churches, then — or else he'll have to endure being forgotten, like the hobby-horse, whose epitaph runs: "For, oh, for, oh, the hobby-horse is forgot!"

*[Trumpets sound. The dumb show enters.]*

*[Enter a King and a Queen, very lovingly, the Queen embracing him and he her. She kneels and makes a show of vows and protestations to him. He raises her up and rests his head on her neck. He lies down on a bank of flowers. She, seeing him asleep, leaves him. Presently a fellow comes in, takes off the King's crown, kisses it, pours poison in the King's ears, and exits. The Queen returns, finds the King dead, and acts out her grief passionately. The Poisoner, with three or four silent attendants, comes in again, seeming to mourn with her. The dead body is carried away. The Poisoner courts the Queen with gifts. She seems reluctant and unwilling for a while, but in the end accepts his love.]*

*[Exit performers.]*

**OPHELIA:** What does this mean, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Well now, this is skulking villainy — it means mischief.

**OPHELIA:** Perhaps this show tells the plot of the play.

*[Enter Prologue.]*

**HAMLET:** We'll find out from this fellow. Actors can't keep a secret — they'll tell everything.

**OPHELIA:** Will he tell us what that show meant?

**HAMLET:** Yes — or any show you care to show him. Don't you be ashamed to show, and he won't be ashamed to tell you what it means.

**OPHELIA:** You're wicked, you're wicked. I'll watch the play.

**PROLOGUE:**
For us, and for our tragedy,
here bowing to your mercy,
we beg your patient hearing.

**HAMLET:** Is that a prologue, or the motto engraved in a ring?

**OPHELIA:** It's brief, my lord.

**HAMLET:** Like a woman's love.

*[Enter two Players, a King and a Queen.]*

**PLAYER KING:**
Full thirty times has the sun's chariot circled
Neptune's salt sea and the round globe of Earth,
and thirty dozen moons with borrowed light
have made twelve-times-thirty journeys round the world,
since love joined our hearts, and the god of marriage our hands,
in the most sacred of mutual bonds.

**PLAYER QUEEN:**
May sun and moon make us count that many journeys
over again before our love is done!
But — misery — you've been so sick of late,
so far from cheerfulness and your old self,
that I fear for you. Yet though I fear,
it must not trouble you at all, my lord:
for a woman's fear and love are matched in measure —
either nothing of both, or both extreme.
What my love is, the proof of it has taught you,
and as my love is sized, so is my fear.
Where love is great, the smallest doubts turn fearful;
where little fears grow great, great love grows there.

**PLAYER KING:**
Truly, love, I must leave you, and soon:
my vital powers are giving up their work.
And you shall live on in this fair world behind me,
honored, beloved — and perhaps one just as kind
you'll take for husband —

**PLAYER QUEEN:**
Oh, silence the rest!
Such love would have to be treason in my breast.
Let me be cursed in a second husband!
No one weds the second who didn't kill the first.

**HAMLET:** *[Aside.]* Wormwood — bitter, bitter!

**PLAYER QUEEN:**
The motives that lead anyone to a second marriage
are cheap calculations of profit — none of love.
I kill my husband a second time over
when a second husband kisses me in bed.

**PLAYER KING:**
I do believe you mean what you now say,
but what we resolve on, we often break.
Purpose is only memory's slave —
born violently, but of feeble constitution:
green fruit now, clinging tightly to the tree,
but falling of itself when it grows ripe.
It is only natural that we forget
to pay ourselves the debts we owe ourselves.
What we propose to do while passion burns,
loses its purpose when the passion ends.
The violence of grief or of joy alike
destroys its own resolves in the very act.
Where joy runs wildest, grief laments the loudest;
grief turns to joy, joy grieves, at the slightest accident.
This world is not forever, and it's not strange
that even our loves should change with our fortunes,
for it's a question still unsettled for us
whether love leads fortune, or fortune leads love.
When the great man falls, watch his favorite flee;
the poor man risen makes friends of former enemies.
And so far love has always waited on fortune:
the man who has no needs will never lack a friend,
and whoever, in need, puts a hollow friend to the test
promptly ripens him into an enemy.
But — to end in good order where I began —
our wills and our fates run so contrary
that our plans are constantly overthrown:
our thoughts are ours; their outcomes, none of our own.
So think now that you'll wed no second husband —
but those thoughts will die when your first lord is dead.

**PLAYER QUEEN:**
May earth refuse me food and heaven light!
May day and night lock sport and rest away from me!
May my trust and hope turn to despair!
May a hermit's rations in a prison cell be all my portion!
May every dark reversal that blanches the face of joy
meet each thing I wish well, and destroy it!
May unending strife pursue me here and hereafter,
if, once a widow, I am ever again a wife!

**HAMLET:** *[To Ophelia.]* If she should break that vow now!

**PLAYER KING:**
That's deeply sworn. Sweet, leave me here awhile;
my spirits are growing heavy, and I'd gladly cheat
the tedious day with sleep.
*[He sleeps.]*

**PLAYER QUEEN:**
Sleep, rock your brain,
and may misfortune never come between us two!

*[Exit.]*

**HAMLET:** Madam, how do you like this play?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** The lady protests too much, I think.

**HAMLET:** Oh, but she'll keep her word.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Do you know the plot? Is there nothing offensive in it?

**HAMLET:** No, no! They're only joking — poisoning in jest. No offense in the world.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** What do you call the play?

**HAMLET:** *The Mousetrap.* And why that, you ask? Figuratively speaking. This play is the portrait of a murder committed in Vienna. Gonzago is the duke's name; his wife is Baptista. You'll see in a moment. It's a nasty piece of work — but so what? Your majesty, and the rest of us with clear consciences, it doesn't touch us. Let the saddle-sore old nag flinch; our shoulders are unchafed.

*[Enter Lucianus.]*

This is one Lucianus — nephew to the king.

**OPHELIA:** You're as good as a chorus, my lord.

**HAMLET:** I could narrate the dialogue between you and your lover, if I could watch the puppets going at it.

**OPHELIA:** You are sharp, my lord — you are sharp.

**HAMLET:** It would cost you some groaning to blunt my edge.

**OPHELIA:** Still better, and worse.

**HAMLET:** That's how you women take your husbands — for better, for worse. — Begin, murderer! Damn it, drop those hideous faces and begin. Come on: "The croaking raven bellows for revenge."

**LUCIANUS:**
Thoughts black, hands ready, drug prepared, and the hour right;
the time my accomplice, and no other creature watching.
You foul mixture, gathered from midnight weeds,
three times blasted, three times infected by Hecate's curse,
let your natural magic and your dreadful power
usurp this healthy life — immediately.

*[Pours the poison into the sleeper's ears.]*

**HAMLET:** He poisons him in the garden to get his estate. His name is Gonzago. The story survives — it's written in very fine Italian. You'll see in a moment how the murderer wins the love of Gonzago's wife.

**OPHELIA:** The King is getting up.

**HAMLET:** What — frightened by a blank cartridge!

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** How is my lord?

**POLONIUS:** Stop the play!

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Give me some light. Away!

**ALL:** Lights, lights, lights!

*[Exit all but Hamlet and Horatio.]*

**HAMLET:**
Why, let the wounded deer go weep,
the unhurt buck go play;
for some must watch, while some must sleep —
so runs the world away.
Wouldn't this performance, sir — plus a forest of feathers in my hat, if the rest of my fortunes turn traitor on me, and two Provençal rosettes on my slashed shoes — get me a full partnership in a company of actors?

**HORATIO:** Half a share.

**HAMLET:** A whole one, for me.
For you must know, dear Damon, friend,
this realm was stripped and shorn
of Jove himself; and now here reigns
a very, very — peacock.

**HORATIO:** You might have rhymed. [The rhyme called for "ass."]

**HAMLET:** Oh, good Horatio — I'd stake a thousand pounds on the ghost's word now! Did you notice?

**HORATIO:** Very clearly, my lord.

**HAMLET:** At the mention of the poisoning?

**HORATIO:** I watched him very closely.

**HAMLET:** Ah, ha! — Come, some music! Come, the recorders! —
For if the king dislikes the comedy,
why then — he just dislikes it, by God.
Come, some music!

*[Enter Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.]*

**GUILDENSTERN:** My good lord, grant me a word with you.

**HAMLET:** Sir — a whole history.

**GUILDENSTERN:** The king, sir —

**HAMLET:** Yes, sir — what about him?

**GUILDENSTERN:** Has withdrawn to his rooms, extraordinarily out of sorts.

**HAMLET:** With drink, sir?

**GUILDENSTERN:** No, my lord — with anger, rather.

**HAMLET:** Your wisdom would show itself richer by reporting this to his doctor. Because if I administer his purgative, it might well plunge him into far worse anger.

**GUILDENSTERN:** My good lord, put some order into your talk, and stop shying so wildly away from my business.

**HAMLET:** I am tame, sir. Proceed.

**GUILDENSTERN:** The queen your mother, in the greatest distress of spirit, has sent me to you.

**HAMLET:** You are welcome.

**GUILDENSTERN:** No, my good lord — this politeness isn't the genuine article. If it pleases you to give me a sane answer, I'll carry out your mother's instruction; if not, then your pardon and my departure will be the end of my business.

**HAMLET:** Sir, I cannot.

**GUILDENSTERN:** Cannot what, my lord?

**HAMLET:** Give you a sane answer — my wits are diseased. But sir, such answer as I *can* make, you shall have — or rather, as you say, my mother shall. So — no more of this, but to the point. My mother, you say —

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Then this is what she says: your behavior has struck her with astonishment and wonder.

**HAMLET:** Oh, wonderful son, who can astonish a mother so! But is there no sequel trailing at the heels of this mother's wonder? Report.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** She wants to speak with you in her private chamber before you go to bed.

**HAMLET:** We shall obey — were she ten times our mother. Have you any further business with us?

**ROSENCRANTZ:** My lord, you once loved me.

**HAMLET:** And so I still do — I swear it by these two hands, these born pickers and stealers.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** My good lord, what is the cause of your distress? You're locking the door on your own freedom if you keep your griefs from your friend.

**HAMLET:** Sir — I lack advancement.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** How can that be, when you have the word of the king himself naming you his successor in Denmark?

**HAMLET:** Yes, sir, but "While the grass grows, the horse starves" — the proverb has gone a bit stale.

*[Enter the Players, with recorders.]*

Oh, the recorders! Let me see one. — A word in private with you: why do you keep circling downwind of me, as if you wanted to drive me into a snare?

**GUILDENSTERN:** Oh my lord, if my duty is too bold, it's my love that makes me ill-mannered.

**HAMLET:** I don't quite follow that. Will you play this pipe?

**GUILDENSTERN:** My lord, I cannot.

**HAMLET:** Please.

**GUILDENSTERN:** Believe me, I cannot.

**HAMLET:** I do beg you.

**GUILDENSTERN:** I don't know a single fingering of it, my lord.

**HAMLET:** It's as easy as lying. Manage these holes with your fingers and thumb, give it breath with your mouth, and it will speak the most eloquent music. Look — these are the stops.

**GUILDENSTERN:** But I can't command those to make any harmony at all. I don't have the skill.

**HAMLET:** Well, look at that — what a worthless thing you take me for! You would play upon *me*. You'd act as if you knew my stops. You'd pluck out the heart of my mystery. You'd sound me from my lowest note to the top of my range. And there is plenty of music, an excellent voice, in this little instrument — yet you can't make *it* speak. God's blood — do you think I'm easier to play than a pipe? Call me whatever instrument you like: you can finger me and fret me all you please, but you cannot play me.

*[Enter Polonius.]*

God bless you, sir!

**POLONIUS:** My lord, the queen wants to speak with you — right away.

**HAMLET:** Do you see that cloud over there, shaped almost like a camel?

**POLONIUS:** By the mass — it is like a camel, indeed.

**HAMLET:** I think it's like a weasel.

**POLONIUS:** It has a back like a weasel's.

**HAMLET:** Or like a whale.

**POLONIUS:** Very like a whale.

**HAMLET:** Then I will come to my mother by and by. — *[Aside.]* They play me for a fool until my bow is bent to breaking. — I will come by and by.

**POLONIUS:** I will say so.

*[Exit.]*

**HAMLET:** "By and by" is easily said.

*[Exit Polonius.]*

Leave me, friends.

*[Exit Rosencrantz, Guildenstern, Horatio, and Players.]*

Now is the very witching hour of night, when graveyards yawn open and hell itself breathes contagion out into this world. Now I could drink hot blood, and do such bitter business as the daylight would tremble to look at. Steady — now to my mother. O heart, don't lose your nature; never let the soul of Nero enter this steadfast chest. Let me be cruel, but not unnatural. I will speak daggers to her, but use none. In this, let my tongue and soul play the hypocrite with each other: however my words may shame and wound her, my soul — never consent to set a seal of action on them!

*[Exit.]*

### Act III, Scene 3

> *Scene: A room in the Castle.*

*[Enter King, Rosencrantz, and Guildenstern.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** I don't like the look of him — nor is it safe for us to let his madness roam free. So prepare yourselves. I'll have your commission drawn up immediately, and he goes to England with you. The interests of our state cannot tolerate a hazard growing this close to us, hour by hour, out of his lunacies.

**GUILDENSTERN:** We will make ourselves ready. It is a most holy and religious duty, this care to keep safe the many, many lives that live and feed upon your majesty.

**ROSENCRANTZ:** Even the single private life is bound to protect itself from harm with all the strength and armor of the mind — but how much more so that spirit on whose well-being the lives of so many depend and rest. When majesty dies, it does not die alone; like a whirlpool it drags down whatever is near it. It is a massive wheel fixed on the summit of the highest mountain, and to its huge spokes ten thousand lesser things are mortised and fastened — so that when it falls, every small attachment, every petty appendage, joins in the thundering ruin. Never once did a king sigh but a whole nation groaned with him.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Equip yourselves, I beg you, for this urgent voyage. We're going to clap fetters on this danger, which now walks around on far too free a foot.

**ROSENCRANTZ AND GUILDENSTERN:** We will hurry.

*[Exit Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.]*

*[Enter Polonius.]*

**POLONIUS:** My lord, he's on his way to his mother's chamber. I'll tuck myself behind the tapestry to hear how it goes. I guarantee she'll take him to task severely. And, as you said — and it was wisely said — it's fitting that some audience beyond a mother, since nature makes mothers biased, should overhear the conversation from a spot of advantage. Farewell, my liege. I'll call on you before you go to bed and tell you what I learn.

**KING CLAUDIUS:** Thank you, my dear lord.

*[Exit Polonius.]*

Oh, my offense is rank — it stinks to heaven. It carries the primal, eldest curse upon it: a brother's murder! I cannot pray, though my inclination is as sharp as my will. My guilt is stronger than my strong intent, and like a man bound to two pieces of business at once, I stand paralyzed over where to begin, and neglect both. What if this cursed hand were coated twice its thickness in brother's blood — is there not rain enough in the sweet heavens to wash it white as snow? What is mercy for, if not to meet the face of sin head on? And what is there in prayer but this double power — to hold us back before we fall, or to pardon us once we're down? Then I'll lift my eyes up; my fault is in the past. But oh — what form of prayer can serve my case? "Forgive me my foul murder"? That cannot work, since I still possess the things I did the murder for: my crown, my own ambition, and my queen. Can a man be pardoned and keep the loot? In the corrupted currents of this world, sin's gilded hand can shove justice aside, and we've often seen the wicked man's own plunder buy off the law. But it is not so above. There, there is no dodging; there the deed lies exposed in its true nature, and we ourselves are compelled, face to face with the very teeth and forehead of our faults, to give evidence against ourselves. What then? What is left? Try what repentance can do. What can't it do? And yet — what can it do, when a man cannot repent? Oh wretched condition! Oh heart as black as death! Oh trapped soul, stuck in the lime, that struggling to get free only sticks the faster! Help, angels! Make the attempt. Bend, stubborn knees; and heart with strings of steel, be soft as the sinews of a newborn baby! All may yet be well.

*[He withdraws and kneels.]*

*[Enter Hamlet.]*

**HAMLET:** Now I could do it — perfectly — now, while he's praying. And now I'll do it. *[Draws his sword.]* And so he goes to heaven — and so I am revenged. That needs a closer look. A villain kills my father, and in return I, his only son, send this same villain — to heaven. Why, that's wages and a bonus, not revenge. He took my father brutally, gross with appetite, all his sins in full bloom and sap, lush as May. And how his account stands now, who knows except heaven? But by our human reckoning and way of thinking, it weighs heavy on him. So am I revenged if I take this man in the very purging of his soul, when he is fit and seasoned for his passage? No. Back in your sheath, sword, and wait for a more horrifying grip: when he's dead drunk asleep, or in his rage, or in the incestuous pleasure of his bed; gambling, swearing, or busy with some act that has no flavor of salvation in it — *then* trip him, so his heels kick at heaven and his soul is as damned and black as the hell it's bound for. My mother is waiting. This medicine only stretches out your sick remaining days.

*[Exit.]*

*[The King rises and comes forward.]*

**KING CLAUDIUS:** My words fly up; my thoughts remain below. Words without thoughts never reach heaven.

*[Exit.]*

### Act III, Scene 4

> *Scene: Another room in the castle.*

*[Enter Queen and Polonius.]*

**POLONIUS:** He'll be here directly. See that you take him firmly to task. Tell him his pranks have been too outrageous to put up with, and that your grace has stood as a screen between him and a great deal of heat. I'll quiet myself right here. Please — be blunt with him.

**HAMLET:** *[Offstage.]* Mother, mother, mother!

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** I promise you; don't worry about me. Withdraw — I hear him coming.

*[Polonius hides behind the tapestry.]*

*[Enter Hamlet.]*

**HAMLET:** Now, mother — what's the matter?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Hamlet, you have deeply offended your father.

**HAMLET:** Mother, you have deeply offended my father.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Come, come — you answer with a foolish tongue.

**HAMLET:** Go, go — you question with a wicked one.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Why — what is this, Hamlet!

**HAMLET:** What's the matter now?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Have you forgotten who I am?

**HAMLET:** No, by the cross, not at all. You are the Queen — your husband's brother's wife — and, though I wish it weren't so, you are my mother.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Very well — then I'll send people to you who *can* speak to you.

**HAMLET:** Come, come, sit down; you're not going anywhere. You don't leave until I've set up a mirror where you can see the innermost part of yourself.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** What are you going to do? You're not going to murder me? Help — help, ho!

**POLONIUS:** *[Behind the tapestry.]* What, ho! Help, help, help!

**HAMLET:** What's this — a rat? *[Draws.]* Dead, for a ducat — dead!

*[He thrusts his sword through the tapestry.]*

**POLONIUS:** *[Behind.]* Oh — I am slain!

*[Falls and dies.]*

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Oh God — what have you done?

**HAMLET:** Honestly, I don't know. Is it the king?

*[Lifts the tapestry and pulls out Polonius's body.]*

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Oh, what a rash and bloody deed this is!

**HAMLET:** A bloody deed! Almost as bad, good mother, as killing a king and marrying his brother.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** As killing a *king*!

**HAMLET:** Yes, lady — that's what I said. *[To Polonius's body.]* You wretched, rash, meddling fool — farewell! I took you for your better. Accept your fortune; you've discovered that being too busy carries certain risks. — Stop wringing your hands. Quiet! Sit down, and let me wring your heart instead. And I shall, if it's made of stuff that can still be pierced — if damnable habit hasn't plated it in brass until it's armored, a fortress against feeling.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** What have I done, that you dare wag your tongue against me with such filthy noise?

**HAMLET:** Such an act as smears the grace and blush right off of modesty; calls virtue a hypocrite; takes the rose from the fair forehead of an innocent love and plants a blister there; makes marriage vows as false as gamblers' oaths. Oh, such a deed as rips the very soul out of the body of the marriage contract, and turns sweet religion into a babble of empty words. Heaven's face burns red over it; yes, this solid, compounded mass of earth itself, with a mournful face, as if Judgment Day were at hand, is sick with thinking about the act.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Oh God — what act, that roars so loud and thunders in the mere announcement of it?

**HAMLET:** Look at this portrait here — and at this one: painted likenesses of two brothers. See what grace sat upon this brow: Hyperion's curls; the forehead of Jove himself; an eye like Mars', made to threaten and command; a bearing like the herald Mercury just alighted on a hill that kisses heaven. A combination, a form, in fact, on which every god seemed to have set his seal, to give the world its guarantee of what a man is. This was your husband. Now look at what follows. Here is your husband — like a mildewed ear of grain, blighting his healthy brother beside him. Do you have eyes? Could you leave your pasture on this fair mountain and gorge yourself on this swamp? Ha! Have you *eyes*? You can't call it love, for at your age the wildfire in the blood is tame — it's humble; it defers to judgment. And what judgment would step from *this* to *this*? You have senses, certainly, or you couldn't move at all — but surely that sense of yours is paralyzed. Because madness itself wouldn't blunder like this; no sense was ever so enslaved to frenzy that it didn't keep back enough power of choice to tell a difference this size. What devil was it that tricked you at blind man's buff? Eyes without feeling, feeling without sight, ears without hands or eyes, smell all by itself — even a diseased sliver of one true sense couldn't have been this stupefied. O shame — where is your blush? Rebellious hell, if you can mutiny in a matron's bones, then let virtue be mere wax to flaming youth and melt in its own fire. Proclaim no shame when hot compulsion gives the marching orders — since frost itself burns just as eagerly, and reason plays the pimp for desire.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** O Hamlet, say no more! You've turned my eyes into my very soul, and there I see stains so black, so deep in the grain, that they will never wash out.

**HAMLET:** No — but to live in the rank sweat of a grease-soaked bed, stewed in corruption, honeying and making love over the filthy sty —

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Oh, speak to me no more! These words enter my ears like daggers. No more, sweet Hamlet.

**HAMLET:** A murderer and a villain — a lackey not one two-hundredth part of your first husband; a clown-Vice among kings; a pickpocket of the empire and the throne, who lifted the precious crown off a shelf and stuffed it in his pocket —

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** No more!

**HAMLET:** A king of rags and patches —

*[Enter Ghost.]*

Save me — hover over me with your wings, you heavenly guards! — What does your gracious figure want?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Oh no — he's mad!

**HAMLET:** Have you come to scold your dawdling son, who lets time and passion slip away and leaves undone the urgent execution of your dread command? Oh, speak!

**GHOST:** Do not forget. This visitation is only to sharpen your almost blunted purpose. But look — bewilderment has seized your mother. Oh, step between her and her struggling soul. Imagination works most powerfully in the weakest bodies. Speak to her, Hamlet.

**HAMLET:** How is it with you, lady?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** No — how is it with *you*, that you fix your eyes on empty air and hold conversation with bodiless space? Your spirits stare wildly out through your eyes; and like sleeping soldiers at the alarm, your flattened hair — as if the dead growth had life in it — starts up and stands on end. O gentle son, sprinkle cool patience on the heat and flame of your fever! What are you looking at?

**HAMLET:** At him — at him! Look how pale his stare is! His form and his cause together, preaching to stones, would make the stones respond. — Don't look at me like that, or that pitiful gaze will soften what I have to do so sternly. Then my task will lose its true color — tears, perhaps, instead of blood.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Who are you saying this to?

**HAMLET:** Do you see nothing there?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Nothing at all — yet everything that is there, I see.

**HAMLET:** And you heard nothing either?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** No — nothing but our two selves.

**HAMLET:** Why, look there! Look how it steals away! My father — dressed just as he was in life! Look — there he goes, right now, out through the doorway!

*[Exit Ghost.]*

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** This is nothing but the minting of your own brain. Madness is very skilled at this kind of bodiless invention.

**HAMLET:** Madness! My pulse keeps time as steadily as yours and makes music just as healthy. It is not madness that I've spoken. Put me to the test: I'll repeat the whole matter word for word — which madness would skip and caper away from. Mother, for the love of grace, don't smear on your soul that flattering ointment — that it's my madness speaking, and not your sin. It will only skin a film over the ulcerated place while rank corruption, tunneling underneath, infects everything unseen. Confess yourself to heaven. Repent what's past; avoid what is to come. And don't spread compost over the weeds to make them ranker still. Forgive me this virtue of mine — for in the fatness of these bloated, wheezing times, virtue itself must beg pardon of vice; yes, must bow and plead for permission to do vice good.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** O Hamlet, you have split my heart in two.

**HAMLET:** Oh, throw away the worse half of it, and live all the purer with the other. Good night. But do not go to my uncle's bed. Put on a virtue, even if you don't possess it. That monster, habit — which devours all sensitivity to what we do, the devil of our routines — is an angel in this one respect: that for the practice of fair and good actions it likewise hands out a coat or uniform that slips on easily. Abstain tonight, and that will lend a kind of ease to the next abstinence, and the next one easier still. For practice can almost change the stamp of nature itself — can either restrain the devil, or throw him out altogether with astonishing power. Once more, good night. And when you feel the desire to be blessed, I'll come begging a blessing from you. As for this same lord — *[Pointing to Polonius.]* — I do repent. But heaven has willed it so: to punish me with this, and this man with me, making me its scourge and its minister. I'll dispose of him, and I'll answer fully for the death I gave him. So again — good night. I must be cruel only to be kind. So the bad begins, and worse remains behind. One word more, good lady.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** What am I to do?

**HAMLET:** Not this — under no circumstances this, which is exactly what I *tell* you to do: let the bloated king tempt you back to bed; let him pinch your cheek playfully and call you his mouse; and let him, for a pair of his reeking kisses, or for paddling at your neck with his damned fingers, get you to unravel this whole business — that I'm not essentially mad at all, only mad by design. It would be *such* a good idea to let him know! Because what queen — merely fair, sober, and wise — would hide matters this precious from a toad, from a bat, from a tomcat? Who would do that? No — in defiance of sense and secrecy alike, go up on the roof of the house, unlatch the birdcage, let the birds fly off, and then, like the famous ape in the story, climb into the cage yourself to see how it's done — and break your own neck on the way down.

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Be assured: if words are made of breath, and breath of life, I have no life in me to breathe one word of what you've said to me.

**HAMLET:** I must go to England. You know that?

**QUEEN GERTRUDE:** Oh no — I had forgotten. It's been decided.

**HAMLET:** The letters are sealed, and my two old schoolfellows — whom I'll trust exactly as I'd trust vipers with their fangs in — carry the orders. They're to sweep the road ahead of me and march me straight into some treachery. Let it run its course. It's the best sport there is, to see the engineer blown sky-high by his own bomb; and it will go hard with me if I don't dig one yard beneath their tunnels and blast them to the moon. Oh, it's the sweetest thing, when two plots meet head-on along a single line. — This man will get me packed off in a hurry. I'll lug the guts into the next room. — Mother, good night. — Truly, this counsellor is now most silent, most discreet, most grave — who all his life was a foolish prattling knave. — Come, sir, let's draw this business of yours to an end. — Good night, mother.

*[Exit in different directions; Hamlet dragging out Polonius.]*
